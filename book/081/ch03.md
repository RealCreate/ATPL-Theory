## Chapter 3 · The Real Wing: Vortices and Induced Drag

A real wing has tips. At the tips the high-pressure air underneath leaks round to the low-pressure air on top, and that small leak reshapes the whole flow: it rolls up into tip vortices, tilts the airflow down behind the wing, adds a drag that gets worse the slower the aeroplane flies, and leaves a wake that can upset a following aircraft. This chapter follows that chain and ends with the drag polar, which sums up a wing's efficiency.

### 3.1 Angle of attack and attitude in three dimensions

For a whole aeroplane, the **angle of attack** is the angle between the **longitudinal axis** and the relative airflow (for a wing section it was the chord line). `081.01.04.01.01`

The **attitude** (pitch angle) is the angle between the longitudinal axis and the **horizon**. They are different things. In level flight the flight path is horizontal, so the attitude and the angle of attack are the same. In a climb the attitude equals the angle of attack plus the climb angle; in a glide the nose can be below the horizon while the angle of attack is positive. A wing can stall at any attitude. `081.01.04.01.02`

### 3.2 Spanwise flow and tip vortices

Seen from above, the air over the wing does not flow straight back. Under the wing the pressure is higher than ambient, so the air there drifts **outwards towards the tips**; over the wing it is lower, so the air drifts **inwards towards the root**. At the tips the high-pressure air flows round from below to the low-pressure side above. `081.01.04.02.01–02`

Where the upper and lower flows meet at the trailing edge they cross at a small angle and form a sheet of small vortices, which rolls up into two strong **wing-tip vortices** just behind and inboard of the tips. Seen from behind, the left vortex turns clockwise and the right one anticlockwise: both rise outboard of the tips and sink inboard. Together they push the air behind the wing downwards; this adds to the **downwash** behind the wing. `081.01.04.02.03`

The strength of the vortices depends on the pressure difference between the surfaces, so on the lift coefficient. At **high angles of attack** (low speed, high weight, steep turns), CL is high and the vortices are strong; at high speed and low angle of attack they are weak. `081.01.04.02.04`

### 3.3 Spanwise lift distribution

Lift is not the same all along the span. It falls to zero at the tips, where the pressures equalise. An **elliptical** planform (or an equivalent mix of taper and twist) gives an elliptical lift distribution with a uniform downwash, which produces the least induced drag for a given span. A **rectangular** wing carries relatively more lift near the tips than the ideal and its root sections work harder; a highly **tapered** or swept wing carries relatively more lift outboard. That distribution also decides where the stall begins (Chapter 7). `081.01.04.02.05`

**Extending flaps** concentrates lift on the inner wing. The trailing vortices then form partly at the outboard ends of the flaps, and the tip vortices start further inboard; the effective span of the lifting system is reduced, so for the same lift the vortex wake is more concentrated. Flaps do not make the wake disappear. `081.01.04.02.07`

### 3.4 Wake turbulence

The two vortices trail behind every aeroplane producing lift. Their strength depends on the aircraft's **weight**, **speed** and **wingspan** (and configuration): the strongest vortices come from an aeroplane that is **heavy, clean and slow**, as in the take-off climb and the approach. They start when the nosewheel lifts at rotation and stop when it touches down on landing. `081.01.04.02.08`

Behind the aircraft the vortices sink at roughly 300–500 ft per minute, levelling off about 500–900 ft below the generating aircraft's flight path, and drift with the wind. Close to the ground they spread sideways at about 5 kt each; a light crosswind of 2–3 kt can hold the upwind vortex stationary over the runway. They decay slowly, typically lasting up to two or three minutes, longer in calm, stable air. The hazard to a following aeroplane is a strong induced roll, loss of height or structural load, worst when it flies straight along a vortex core. `081.01.04.02.06`

>
> | Behaviour | Typical value |
> | --- | --- |
> | Sink rate | 300–500 ft/min |

> | Lateral spread near the ground | ≈ 5 kt each way |

### 3.5 Induced drag

Behind the wing the downwash tilts the airflow down. The wing therefore meets air flowing slightly downwards, at a smaller **effective angle of attack** than its geometric angle of attack: the difference is the **induced angle of attack**. `081.01.04.03.07–08`

Lift is perpendicular to the local, tilted airflow, so it is tilted **backwards** by the induced angle. Its component along the flight path is a drag force: **induced drag**. It is the price of making lift with a wing of finite span, and its root cause is the pressure difference that drives spanwise flow and the tip vortices. `081.01.04.03.01` `081.01.04.03.09`

The **induced drag coefficient** is approximately

**CDi = CL² / (π × A × e)**

so it grows with the **square of the lift coefficient** and falls with the **aspect ratio** A; e is an efficiency factor (1 for an ideal elliptical lift distribution, about 0.8 for a real aeroplane). `081.01.04.03.02` `081.01.04.03.11`

In straight and level flight the lift equals the weight, so the CL needed is high at low speed and low at high speed. Induced drag is therefore **largest at low speed**, falling roughly with **1/V²** as speed rises; parasite drag does the opposite. At low speed induced drag is most of the total; at high speed it is a small part. `081.01.04.03.03`

At a given IAS, more **mass** needs a higher CL, and induced drag grows with the **square of the weight**: 10 % more mass gives about 21 % more induced drag. `081.01.04.03.04`

Taken together, induced drag depends on: `081.01.04.03.10`

- **Speed**: inversely proportional to the square of the speed.
- **Aspect ratio**: inversely proportional to it. Long, slender wings have weaker vortices for the same lift.
- **Planform**: least for an elliptical lift distribution; more for a rectangular or very tapered wing.
- **Bank angle in a level turn**: the load factor n = 1/cos φ raises the lift needed, so induced drag grows with n². At 60° of bank (n = 2) it is four times as large.

A wing designer can reduce induced drag with: `081.01.04.03.05–06`

- a **higher aspect ratio**;
- **winglets**, which recover some energy from the tip vortex and increase the effective span;
- **tip tanks**, which act as end plates and slow the flow round the tip;
- **wing twist** (washout), which shapes the lift distribution towards the ideal;
- **camber change** along the span, which does the same;
- in general, a lift distribution close to **elliptical**.

### 3.6 Induced drag on the graphs

Induced drag changes the **lift curve** of a three-dimensional wing. Because of the induced angle, a wing needs a larger geometric angle of attack to reach the same CL than an infinite wing would. The lift curve of a **low-aspect-ratio** wing is therefore **less steep**, and it reaches CLMAX at a higher angle of attack; a **high-aspect-ratio** wing has a steeper curve and stalls at a lower angle of attack. `081.01.04.03.12`

The **drag polar** plots CL against CD (or CD against CL) for the whole aeroplane. For most aeroplanes it is close to a parabola:

**CD = CDP + k × CL²**

where CDP is the parasite drag coefficient, independent of lift, and k × CL² the induced drag, with k = 1/(πAe). A higher aspect ratio gives a smaller k and a polar that rises less steeply. `081.01.04.03.12–13`

The point of **minimum drag coefficient** is where the curve is closest to the CL axis, at or near CL = 0. The more important point is where a straight line from the origin just **touches** the curve: there **CL/CD is a maximum**, which is the same as the best lift-to-drag ratio. The angle of attack at that point gives the least drag for the weight carried, the speed VMD (Chapter 4). `081.01.04.03.14`

The **L/D ratio** measures aerodynamic efficiency: how much lift the aeroplane gets for each newton of drag. Its maximum fixes the best glide angle, the minimum thrust needed in level flight and, for a jet, the best endurance. A modern airliner reaches an L/D of about 17–20; a high-performance glider over 50. `081.01.04.03.15`

> **Worked example · induced drag.** A = 9, e = 0.8, CL = 0.5: CDi = 0.25 / (π × 9 × 0.8) = 0.25 / 22.6 ≈ **0.011**. In a 60° turn at the same speed, CL doubles to 1.0 and CDi becomes about **0.044**.
