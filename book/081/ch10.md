## Chapter 10 · Limitations and Flight Envelopes

Every aeroplane is certified for a limited range of speeds and loads. Above them the structure may bend permanently or fail, flutter may tear a surface off, or a flap or gear door may be damaged. This chapter explains the operating speed limits, the manoeuvring envelope (the V–n diagram) and the gust envelope, and what changes them.

### 10.1 Flutter

**Flutter** is a self-sustaining, rapidly growing oscillation of a structure, such as a wing, tailplane or control surface, in which aerodynamic forces and the structure's elastic and inertial properties feed one another. A wing bends, which changes its angle of attack, which changes the lift, which bends it further, while the inertia of a control surface or engine lags behind and adds energy each cycle. Above the **flutter speed** the oscillation grows until the structure fails, sometimes within seconds. The likelihood increases with **IAS** (dynamic pressure supplies the energy) and with an unfavourable **mass distribution**, especially when a surface's centre of mass lies **behind** its hinge line or elastic axis. `081.06.01.01.01`

The cure is **mass balance**: placing mass so that the inertia works against the oscillation. `081.06.01.01.02`

- **Wing-mounted engines on pylons** are placed **ahead of** the wing's elastic axis, where their mass damps the coupling of bending and twisting.
- **Control surfaces** have balance weights **ahead of the hinge line**, so that their centre of mass is on or ahead of the hinge, and the surface does not lag behind the wing's motion.

The aeroplane is designed and tested so that the whole **flight envelope**, up to the design dive speed VD/MD **with a margin** (typically 1.2 × VD), is **free of flutter**. Damaged balance weights, loose control surfaces, excess play or extra paint layers can lower the flutter speed into the normal envelope. `081.06.01.01.03`

### 10.2 Gear and flap speeds

Landing gear and flaps are not designed to take the loads of high-speed flight. `081.06.01.03.01`

- **VLO** (landing gear operating speed) is the maximum speed at which the gear may be **extended or retracted**.
- **VLE** (landing gear extended speed) is the maximum speed at which the aeroplane may be **flown with the gear extended** (and locked).

On some types **VLO is lower than VLE**, because during extension or retraction the gear doors are open and the gear is unlocked and moving, so it is more exposed to the airflow and its actuators and doors can only take lower loads than the locked, extended gear. `081.06.01.03.02`

**VFE** (flaps extended speed) is the maximum speed with the flaps in a given extended position. Each flap setting has its own VFE, lower for larger flap settings, set by the structural strength of the flaps and their mechanism and by the larger loads the bigger deflections carry. `081.06.01.03.03`

To prevent flap overload, aeroplanes use placards and VFE markings (the white arc on light aircraft), **flap load relief** systems that automatically retract the flaps to a smaller setting if the speed exceeds VFE and re-extend them when the speed drops, overspeed warnings, and procedures that require the speed to be below the next setting's VFE before selecting it. `081.06.01.03.04`

### 10.3 Maximum speeds

`081.06.01.04.01–02`

- **VNO** (maximum structural cruising speed, CS-23) is the speed that should not be exceeded except in **smooth air**, and then only with caution; it is the top of the green arc. It is set no higher than the design cruising speed VC.
- **VNE** (never-exceed speed, CS-23) is the speed that must **never** be exceeded: the red line. It is set at no more than 0.9 VD, leaving a margin for inadvertent upsets.
- **VMO** (maximum operating speed, CS-25 and turbine aeroplanes) is the maximum IAS that may not be deliberately exceeded in any phase of flight; it is no higher than VC, with a margin to VD for upsets.

They differ in what they protect: VNO marks the start of a caution range where gusts could overstress the structure; VNE and VMO are hard limits, chosen to keep a margin below the design dive speed, where flutter, control and structural problems could occur.

Flying above **VNE or VMO** risks: structural loads above limit, especially in gusts or manoeuvres; **flutter** and other aeroelastic effects; reduced or reversed **control effectiveness**; damage to doors, fairings and windscreens; and, close to VD, loss of the margin the designer built in. `081.06.01.04.03`

**MMO** (maximum operating Mach number) is the Mach number that may not be deliberately exceeded. It is set by high-speed buffet and shock-induced separation, **Mach tuck** and changes in stability and control, **flutter** and aeroelastic margins, and the need to keep a margin below the design dive Mach number MD. `081.06.01.05.01`

### 10.4 The manoeuvring-load (V–n) diagram

The **manoeuvring-load diagram** shows, for a given mass and configuration, the load factors the aeroplane can produce and withstand against **equivalent airspeed**. Its boundaries are the stall at the left (the most load factor the wing can produce at each speed), the limit load factors at the top and bottom, and the design dive speed at the right. Inside, the aeroplane can manoeuvre safely; outside, it either stalls or is overstressed. `081.06.02.01.01`

The **limit load factor** is the largest load the aeroplane is expected to meet in service. The structure must carry it **without permanent deformation**. The **ultimate load factor** is the limit load times a **safety factor of 1.5**; the structure must carry it without failure for at least three seconds, but may be permanently deformed. Exceeding the limit load risks permanent bending of the structure; exceeding the ultimate load risks structural failure. `081.06.02.01.02`

The design speeds are: `081.06.02.01.03`

- **VA**: the **design manoeuvring speed**, the speed at which the aeroplane stalls at its positive limit load factor.
- **VB**: the **design speed for maximum gust intensity**, used for transport aeroplanes in turbulence.
- **VC**: the **design cruising speed**.
- **VD**: the **design diving speed**, the highest speed the structure is designed for.

On the diagram: `081.06.02.01.04`

- the vertical axis is the **load factor n**;
- the horizontal axis is the **speed**, as **EAS**, so the diagram applies at any altitude (below where compressibility matters);
- the outline is the **EAS envelope**: stall curves on the left, +limit and −limit horizontally, and VD on the right;
- the **1-g stall speed** VS1G is where the positive stall curve crosses n = 1;
- the **stall boundary** follows n = (V / VS1G)², a parabola, rising to the positive limit at VA (and similarly for negative load factors with the negative CLMAX).

![V–n diagram for a CS-25 transport: stall boundaries, limit load factors, VA, VC and VD, with gust lines](img/p-vn.jpg)

**VMO** or **VNE** lies between VC and VD: VMO no higher than VC for transport aeroplanes, VNE about 0.9 VD for light aeroplanes, leaving a margin for upsets. `081.06.02.01.05`

> **Key numbers · limit load factors**
>
> | Category | Flaps up | Flaps extended |
> | --- | --- | --- |
> | CS-25 (large aeroplanes) | +2.5 to −1.0 | +2.0 to 0 |
> | CS-23 normal | +3.8 to −1.52 | +2.0 to 0 |
> | CS-23 utility | +4.4 to −1.76 | +2.0 to 0 |
> | CS-23 aerobatic | +6.0 to −3.0 | +2.0 to 0 |
>
> For CS-25 the positive limit is 2.1 + 24 000 / (W + 10 000), W in lb, between 2.5 and 3.8; the negative limit of −1.0 applies up to VC and reduces to 0 at VD. `081.06.02.01.06`

Since the stall speed at n is VS√n, the speed at which the aeroplane stalls exactly at its positive limit is **VA = VS1G × √nlimit**. `081.06.02.01.07`

> **Worked example · VA.** VS1G = 120 kt and n = 2.5: VA = 120 × √2.5 ≈ **190 kt**. A CS-23 normal-category aeroplane with VS = 60 kt and n = 3.8: VA = 60 × √3.8 ≈ **117 kt**.

Below **VA**, a full, abrupt nose-up elevator input will stall the wing before the limit load factor is reached, so the structure is protected by the stall. Above VA, the same input can produce a load factor **above the limit**, causing permanent deformation or failure, before the wing stalls. Full, abrupt deflection of a control is acceptable only below VA, and even then repeated or reversing inputs (especially of the rudder) can exceed the design loads. `081.06.02.01.08`

### 10.5 Mass and altitude

The limit load factor is a load per unit weight. A heavier aeroplane at the same load factor puts a higher absolute load on the structure; the **accelerated stall boundary** moves to higher speeds with √mass. `081.06.02.02.01` Because VA = VS√n and VS changes with √mass, **VA decreases at lower mass**: **VA2 = VA1 × √(m2 / m1)**. A light aeroplane reaches its limit load factor at a lower speed, because it stalls at a lower speed. `081.06.02.02.02`

> **Worked example · VA and mass.** VA = 110 kt at 1100 kg. At 880 kg: VA = 110 × √(880/1100) = 110 × 0.894 ≈ **98 kt**.

At **high altitude** VA loses its meaning. There the stall IAS rises because of compressibility, high-speed buffet appears, and the envelope is bounded by Mach number limits rather than by the EAS-based V–n diagram; VA may lie close to or even above the maximum operating speed, and the useful protection comes from the buffet boundaries (Chapter 7). `081.06.02.02.03`

**MC** is the **design cruising Mach number** and **MD** the **design diving Mach number**: the Mach equivalents of VC and VD that bound the envelope at high altitude. `081.06.02.02.04`

### 10.6 The gust envelope

A vertical gust suddenly changes the wing's angle of attack and so its lift and load factor. The **gust-load diagram** shows, against EAS, the load factors produced by gusts of specified vertical speed: straight lines fanning out from n = 1 at zero speed, steeper for stronger gusts. The aeroplane must withstand them within the envelope. `081.06.03.01.01`

> **Key numbers · design gusts (EAS)**
>
> | At | Gust | m/s | kt |
> | --- | --- | --- | --- |
> | VB | 66 ft/s | 20.1 | 39 |
> | VC | 50 ft/s | 15.2 | 30 |
> | VD | 25 ft/s | 7.6 | 15 |
>
> These are the classic values used in the exam, applying up to 20 000 ft and reducing linearly above it. Modern CS-25 uses a refined discrete-gust method with similar reference values.

The **rough-air speed VRA** is the speed recommended for flight in turbulence. It is chosen to give a good margin against the **stall and low-speed buffet** in the strongest gusts, while staying far enough below **VMO/MMO** that speed excursions in turbulence will not exceed it, and no higher than VB. It is close to VB. `081.06.03.01.02`

Turbulence causes **structural loads** (fatigue and possible overstress), **speed and height excursions**, **stall or buffet** if gusts raise the angle of attack, possible **loss of control**, difficulty reading instruments, and passenger and crew **injuries**. Fly at VRA, keep the attitude rather than chasing speed and height, and keep seat belts fastened. `081.06.03.01.03`

### 10.7 What sets the gust load

The increase in load factor from a gust is approximately:

**Δn = ρ0 × a × Ue × VE / (2 × W/S)**

where ρ0 is sea-level density, a the lift-curve slope, Ue the equivalent gust speed and VE the EAS. So the gust load factor `081.06.03.02.01`

- **increases** with a steeper **lift-curve slope**: a higher **aspect ratio** makes it worse, more **sweep** (a flatter lift curve) better;
- **increases** with **EAS** and with the **speed of the vertical gust**;
- **decreases** with higher **wing loading**: a **heavier** aeroplane (or a smaller **wing area**) feels a smaller load factor from the same gust, and a light one is thrown around more;
- in terms of **altitude**, at the same EAS it is unchanged by the formula, but at the same TAS the lower density at altitude reduces it.
