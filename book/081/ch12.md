## Chapter 12 · Flight Mechanics

Flight mechanics applies Newton's laws to the four forces in each kind of steady flight: level, climbing, descending, gliding and turning. The same few formulas give climb gradients, glide range, turn radius and rate, and the load factor. The second half of the chapter deals with the most demanding steady case, flight with one engine inoperative, and the minimum control speeds that come from it.

### 12.1 The four forces in level flight

In straight, horizontal, steady flight the aeroplane is in equilibrium: no acceleration in any direction, so the forces balance. `081.08.01.01.01`

The four forces are: `081.08.01.01.02`

- **Lift**, perpendicular to the flight path, acting through the wing's centre of pressure;
- **Weight**, vertically downwards through the **CG**;
- **Thrust**, along the thrust line of the engines;
- **Drag**, parallel and opposite to the flight path, acting through a centre of drag.

**Lift equals weight** and **thrust equals drag**. Because the four forces do not all act through the same point, they also produce pitching moments: lift behind the CG pitches the nose down, thrust below the drag line pitches it up, and so on. The **tailplane** provides the extra (usually downward) force that brings the sum of the moments to zero, which means the wing must lift a little more than the weight. `081.08.01.01.03`

### 12.2 The climb

The **flight-path angle** γ (gamma) is the angle between the flight path and the horizontal. `081.08.01.02.01` With no wind and no bank, the **pitch attitude = γ + α**: the nose is above the horizon by the climb angle plus the angle of attack. `081.08.01.02.02`

In a straight, steady climb the weight no longer acts perpendicular to the flight path. Split along and across the path, it has a component **W sin γ** acting backwards along the path and **W cos γ** perpendicular to it. `081.08.01.02.03` Balancing the forces: `081.08.01.02.04`

- **parallel to the path: T = D + W sin γ**;
- **perpendicular to the path: L = W cos γ**.

So in a climb **thrust is greater than drag**, because it must also lift part of the weight up the slope, `081.08.01.02.05` and **lift is less than weight**, because part of the weight is carried by the thrust. `081.08.01.02.06`

For small angles sin γ ≈ γ (in radians) and lift ≈ weight, so

**sin γ = (T − D) / W ≈ T/W − 1/(L/D)**

The climb gradient depends on the **excess thrust** per unit of weight. `081.08.01.02.07`

> **Worked example · climb gradient.** Weight 500 kN, thrust 100 kN, L/D 12. Drag ≈ 500/12 ≈ 41.7 kN. sin γ = (100 − 41.7) / 500 = 0.117: a gradient of about **11.7 %**, γ ≈ 6.7°. With the thrust halved (one engine of two), sin γ = (50 − 41.7)/500 = 0.017: **1.7 %**. Losing half the thrust cut the gradient by about 86 %.

In a climb with **constant vertical speed** (such as autopilot V/S mode) and **constant thrust setting**, the thrust available falls as altitude increases, so the excess thrust falls. To keep the same rate of climb the aeroplane must use its kinetic energy: the **IAS falls**, the **angle of attack rises**, and the **flight-path angle increases** as TAS decreases (rate of climb = TAS × sin γ). Left alone, the speed decays towards the stall. `081.08.01.02.08`

### 12.3 The descent

In a straight, steady **powered descent**, the weight component W sin γ now acts **forwards** along the path. `081.08.01.03.01` So: `081.08.01.03.02`

- **parallel: T = D − W sin γ** (with γ taken as the descent angle);
- **perpendicular: L = W cos γ**.

**Lift is less than weight**, as in the climb, because the weight is tilted relative to the path. `081.08.01.03.03` **Thrust is less than drag**, because part of the weight helps pull the aeroplane along. `081.08.01.03.04`

### 12.4 The glide

In a **glide** there is no thrust at all. `081.08.01.04.01` The forces are: `081.08.01.04.02`

- **parallel: D = W sin γ**;
- **perpendicular: L = W cos γ**.

Dividing the two: **tan γ = D / L**. The **glide angle** depends only on the **lift-to-drag ratio**: the higher the L/D, the flatter the glide. The glide distance in still air is **height × L/D**. With wind, the air distance is unchanged but the ground distance is multiplied by **GS / TAS**. `081.08.01.04.03`

> **Worked example · glide range.** From 10 000 ft with L/D 12: still-air range = 10 000 × 12 = 120 000 ft ≈ **19.7 NM**. Gliding at 120 kt TAS into a 20 kt headwind (GS 100 kt): 19.7 × 100/120 ≈ **16.4 NM**.

**VMD**, the speed for minimum drag, is flown at the angle of attack that gives **(L/D)max**. It therefore gives the **flattest glide** and the greatest still-air glide distance. `081.08.01.04.04`

**Wind** does not change the glide angle through the air, nor the time in the air (which depends on the rate of descent), but it changes the angle and distance over the ground. A **headwind** steepens the glide over the ground and shortens the distance; a **tailwind** flattens it and extends the distance. Duration is unchanged at the same airspeed. `081.08.01.04.05`

**Mass** does not change (L/D)max. At **VMD** for each mass, a heavier aeroplane glides at the **same angle** and over the **same distance**, but VMD is higher (∝ √W), so it descends **faster** and the **duration is shorter**. If instead the heavier aeroplane keeps the **same airspeed** as the light one flew at its VMD, it is now below its own VMD, its L/D is lower, and it glides **more steeply** and less far. `081.08.01.04.06`

**Configuration** changes reduce L/D: extending gear, flaps or speed brakes, or a windmilling propeller, makes the glide **steeper** and, at a given speed, increases the rate of descent, shortening the **duration**. `081.08.01.04.07`

The **rate of descent** is **TAS × sin γ**. For the same gradient a higher TAS (higher altitude or higher weight) means a higher rate of descent. `081.08.01.04.08`

**VMP**, the speed for **minimum power required** (power = drag × TAS), is lower than VMD, roughly 0.76 VMD. In a glide the power lost is W × rate of descent, so the **minimum rate of descent** is at **VMP**. It gives the **longest time** in the air, but not the longest distance, which is at VMD. `081.08.01.04.09`

The pilot chooses between them. `081.08.01.04.10`

- **Minimum glide angle (VMD)**, to reach the furthest point, such as a distant landing field after engine failure.
- **Minimum rate of descent (VMP)**, to stay airborne longest, for example to restart an engine or wait for help, or a glider in a thermal.

In a **headwind**, fly faster than VMD for the best range over the ground, because time in the wind is reduced; in a **tailwind**, slightly slower. In practice a speed a little **above VMD** is preferred anyway, because VMD and VMP lie in the region of neutral or negative speed stability, where speed is hard to hold.

### 12.5 The level turn

In a steady, coordinated turn the lift is tilted by the bank angle φ. Its **vertical** component, **L cos φ**, balances the weight; its **horizontal** component, **L sin φ**, provides the **centripetal force** that turns the aeroplane, m V² / R. `081.08.01.05.01`

Dividing the two: **tan φ = V² / (g R)**. `081.08.01.05.02`

A **coordinated** turn has no sideslip: the ball of the slip indicator is centred. In a **slipping** turn the aeroplane has too much bank for its rate of turn (or too little rudder) and slips inwards: the ball moves to the **inside** of the turn. In a **skidding** turn it has too little bank for its rate of turn (too much rudder) and skids outwards: the ball moves to the **outside**. Correct either with rudder **towards the ball** ("step on the ball"), or by adjusting the bank. `081.08.01.05.03`

The formula tan φ = V²/(gR) contains **no mass**. For a given TAS and radius, every aeroplane needs the same bank angle, light or heavy: the extra lift needed for a heavier aeroplane comes from a higher CL, not more bank. `081.08.01.05.04` And for a given bank, the **radius depends only on TAS**: **R = V² / (g tan φ)**. `081.08.01.05.05`

> **Worked example · turn radius.** TAS 200 kt = 102.9 m/s, bank 30°: R = 102.9² / (9.81 × 0.577) ≈ 1870 m ≈ **1.0 NM**. At 250 kt the same bank gives about **1.6 NM**: the radius grows with the square of TAS. `081.08.01.05.06`

Increasing the bank angle in a level turn has these effects. `081.08.01.05.07`

- The **load factor** n = **1 / cos φ** rises: 1.15 at 30°, 1.41 at 45°, 2 at 60°.
- The **angle of attack** must rise (more CL for more lift) at the same speed.
- The **drag** rises, mainly induced drag, which grows with n².
- So the **thrust** needed rises, or the aeroplane slows down.

The **angular velocity** is the rate at which an angle changes, in degrees or radians per second. `081.08.01.05.08` The **rate of turn** is the angular velocity of the heading change: **ω = V / R = g tan φ / V**. A **rate-1 turn** is **3° per second**, a full 360° in 2 minutes. `081.08.01.05.09`

At a given bank angle, a **higher TAS** gives a **lower rate of turn** and a larger radius. To keep rate 1, the bank needed increases with TAS; a useful approximation is bank ≈ TAS (kt) / 10 + 7. `081.08.01.05.10`

> **Worked example · rate-1 bank.** At 150 kt: bank ≈ 15 + 7 = **22°**. At 250 kt: bank ≈ 32°, close to the 25–30° limit used in instrument flying.

The **load factor and stall speed in a turn** are n = 1/cos φ and VS turn = VS1G × √n. `081.08.01.05.11`

> **Worked example · stall in a turn.** VS1G = 140 kt, bank 45°: n = 1.41 and VS = 140 × 1.19 ≈ **166 kt**.

Turn radius matters wherever protected airspace is designed around a speed. Departure and arrival procedures carry **maximum IAS limits**, and approach procedures use **speed categories** (A to E), because the obstacle-protected areas are sized for the radius of turn at that speed. Flying faster than the published speed makes the turn wider than the protected area, risking obstacles, terrain or other traffic, especially at high-altitude airports where the TAS for a given IAS is higher. `081.08.01.05.12`

Using the **rudder** to tighten a turn in a **swept-wing** aeroplane is hazardous. The yaw creates sideslip, and the swept wing's strong lateral stability turns it into a powerful roll, which can quickly steepen the bank beyond control; large or reversing rudder inputs can also overload the fin. Turns are made with aileron and coordinated with the minimum rudder. `081.08.01.05.13`

### 12.6 Asymmetric thrust

When an engine fails on a multi-engine aeroplane, the thrust of the remaining engine(s) and the drag of the failed one act on opposite sides of the CG. The aeroplane **yaws towards the dead engine**; the yaw speeds up the live engine's wing, and the loss of slipstream (on propeller aeroplanes) reduces lift on the dead side, so it also **rolls towards the dead engine**. Without correction it enters a spiral. The total drag increases (windmilling, sideslip, control deflections), and climb performance falls by far more than the proportion of thrust lost. On propeller aeroplanes the loss of slipstream over the wing and asymmetric propeller effects make it worse than on jets. `081.08.02.01.01`

The **critical engine** is the engine whose failure has the **most adverse effect** on handling and performance. On a propeller twin with both propellers turning **clockwise** (from behind), it is the **left** engine, because P-factor moves the right engine's thrust line outboard (Chapter 11). On a jet with both engines identical, there is in principle no critical engine, though a crosswind or other asymmetries may define one. `081.08.02.01.02`

With asymmetric thrust in steady flight, a conventional **slip indicator** does not show sideslip directly. In the **wings-level** method, with rudder alone holding the heading, the ball is **centred** but the aeroplane flies with **sideslip** towards the dead engine. In the **wings-down** method, with about 5° of bank towards the live engine and less rudder, the sideslip is zero but the ball lies about **half a ball's width** towards the live engine. `081.08.02.01.03`

A **crosswind** adds a weathercocking yaw into wind. A crosswind from the side of the dead engine adds to the yaw towards it and demands more rudder; one from the live side helps. On the ground this directly affects the minimum control speed. `081.08.02.01.04`

### 12.7 Balancing the yawing moments

About the CG, the yawing moments are: the **live engine's thrust** times its distance from the CG, plus the **failed engine's drag** (windmilling or not) times its distance; opposed by the **rudder and fin side force** times the fin's arm, and any sideslip moments. `081.08.02.02.01`

The thrust depends on **air density**. At low altitude and low temperature (high density) the live engine gives more thrust and so more yawing moment; at high altitude or high temperature, less. `081.08.02.02.02`

The further the engines are from the CG (wide-spaced wing engines), the larger the arm and the **larger the yawing moment**; engines close to the fuselage (rear-mounted jets) give a smaller moment. `081.08.02.02.03`

To restore **directional balance** after an engine failure: `081.08.02.02.04`

- apply **rudder** against the yaw (towards the live engine);
- **bank** slightly (up to 5°) towards the live engine to remove the sideslip;
- **feather** the propeller (or reduce the windmilling drag);
- if necessary, **reduce thrust** on the live engine;
- keep **speed** up, because the rudder's power grows with dynamic pressure.

### 12.8 Side forces and the hazards near VMC

To hold a heading, the side forces must also balance. `081.08.02.03.01`

- The **fin and rudder** produce a side force that holds the yaw, pushing the tail towards the dead-engine side.
- In the **wings-level** method, this side force is balanced by a **fuselage side force** from sideslip: the aeroplane flies with its relative wind from the dead-engine side. The sideslip changes the fin's angle of attack against the rudder, so more rudder is needed and drag is higher.
- In the **wings-down** method, a bank of up to 5° towards the live engine **tilts the lift vector**, and its horizontal component balances the fin force with **no sideslip**. Less rudder is needed, drag is lower, and VMC is lower.

Flying near **VMC** brings several hazards. `081.08.02.03.02` The **angle of attack** is high at low speed, close to the stall, and a stall with asymmetric thrust can lead to a spin. The **sideslip** adds drag and, in the wrong sense, makes control harder. The **fin** works with full rudder at high loads, and its **angle of attack**, increased by sideslip and rudder deflection, can approach the fin stall, after which directional control is lost suddenly.

A **sideslip** changes the angle at which the air meets the fin. In the wings-level case, the sideslip from the dead-engine side gives the fin an angle of attack against the rudder's force, so the rudder must be deflected further for the same side force. A sideslip in the opposite sense, for example from too much bank towards the live engine, adds to the fin's angle of attack and, with full rudder, takes it towards the fin stall. `081.08.02.03.03`

### 12.9 Mass and minimum control speeds

The rudder's power depends on dynamic pressure, so on **speed**, while the asymmetric thrust depends on the engines, not on mass. A **light** aeroplane has low stall speeds and so low take-off and approach speeds; at those speeds the rudder may not be able to balance full asymmetric thrust. Controllability with one engine inoperative is therefore a typical problem at **low mass**, and the minimum control speeds may limit the take-off speeds (V1, VR, V2) instead of the stall. `081.08.02.04.01`

### 12.10 VMC, VMCL and VMCG

**VMC** (minimum control speed in flight, also VMCA) is the calibrated airspeed at which, when the **critical engine is suddenly made inoperative**, it is still possible to **maintain control** of the aeroplane and to maintain **straight flight** with a bank angle of **not more than 5°** towards the live engine. `081.08.02.08.01`

It is determined in flight test with: maximum available take-off thrust on the operating engine(s); the most unfavourable (normally the **aft**) CG; the aeroplane trimmed for take-off; take-off flaps; gear retracted; the propeller of the failed engine in the condition it would normally reach (windmilling, or feathered if automatic); out of ground effect; and at the most critical weight. The **rudder force** must not exceed **150 lbf (667 N)**, the heading change must stay within about 20°, and VMC may not exceed **1.13 VSR**. `081.08.02.08.02`

An **aft CG** shortens the arm between the CG and the rudder, so the rudder produces less yawing moment and **VMC is higher**. That is why VMC is determined at the aft limit. `081.08.02.08.03`

**VMCL** (minimum control speed during approach and landing) is the calibrated airspeed at which, with the **critical engine suddenly inoperative** in the **approach and landing configuration**, control can be maintained and straight flight held with not more than 5° of bank. `081.08.02.09.01` It is determined with the aeroplane in the landing configuration, trimmed for approach, **go-around thrust** on the operating engine(s), the failed engine's propeller in its normal condition, the most unfavourable weight and **aft CG**, again with rudder force limits. `081.08.02.09.02` As for VMC, an **aft CG** gives a **higher VMCL**. `081.08.02.09.03`

**VMCG** (minimum control speed on the ground) is the calibrated airspeed during the take-off run at which, when the **critical engine is suddenly made inoperative**, control can be maintained using the **primary aerodynamic controls alone** (no nosewheel steering) so that the take-off can safely continue, with the aeroplane deviating no more than **30 ft (9.1 m)** laterally from the centreline. `081.08.02.10.01` It is determined with take-off thrust on the operating engine(s), the most critical take-off configuration, the aeroplane trimmed for take-off, the most unfavourable (aft) CG and weight, and a rudder force limit of 150 lbf. V1 may not be less than VMCG. `081.08.02.10.02` An **aft CG** again gives the shortest rudder arm and so the **highest VMCG**. `081.08.02.10.03`

### 12.11 Density and minimum control speeds

The thrust of the live engine depends on **air density**: it is highest at low pressure altitude and low temperature. `081.08.02.11.01` The minimum control speeds are set by the balance between the asymmetric thrust and the rudder's power. Less thrust means less yawing moment, which the rudder can balance at a lower dynamic pressure, so **VMC, VMCL and VMCG all decrease** when thrust is reduced, at high altitudes and temperatures, or with derated take-off thrust. That is why the published values are highest at low, cold airfields. `081.08.02.11.02`

### 12.12 Significant points on the polar

The aeroplane's **polar curve** (CL against CD) has several significant points. `081.08.03.01.01`

- **CL = 0**: the zero-lift point, giving the minimum value of drag coefficient close to it (CDmin).
- The **tangent from the origin**: **(CL/CD)max**, the angle of attack for **VMD**, the best glide angle and, for a jet, maximum endurance.
- The point of **(CL^(3/2)/CD)max**, at a higher CL: the angle of attack for **VMP**, minimum power and minimum rate of descent (and, for a propeller aeroplane, maximum endurance).
- The point of **(CL^(1/2)/CD)max**, at a lower CL: the angle of attack for the jet's best range speed (about 1.32 VMD).
- **CLMAX**: the top of the curve, the stall.
