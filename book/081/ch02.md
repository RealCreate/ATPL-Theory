## Chapter 2 · Lift on an Aerofoil

This chapter follows the air around a wing section: where it splits, where it speeds up, how the pressure falls on top and rises underneath, and how that pressure field becomes lift, drag and a pitching moment. It ends with the coefficients that let one formula describe any wing at any speed, and with the lift curve, the single most useful graph in the subject.

### 2.1 The streamline pattern

Seen from the wing, the air approaches in parallel streamlines. Ahead of the leading edge they start to curve upwards, which is called **upwash**. They divide at the leading edge, pass over and under the section, and leave the trailing edge deflected downwards, which is called **downwash**. Over the curved upper surface the streamlines are squeezed together; under the lower surface they stay further apart. `081.01.02.01.01` `081.01.02.01.03`

Where streamlines **converge**, the stream tube narrows, so by continuity the air speeds up and by Bernoulli its static pressure falls. Where they **diverge**, the air slows down and its pressure rises. Over the front of the upper surface the streamlines converge strongly; towards the trailing edge they diverge again. `081.01.02.01.02`

### 2.2 The stagnation point

The streamline that divides the flow going over the wing from the flow going under it meets the leading edge at the **stagnation point**. There the air is brought to rest, its speed is zero and its static pressure is the highest anywhere on the section: it equals the total pressure, with a pressure coefficient of +1. `081.01.02.02.01`

As the angle of attack **increases**, the stagnation point moves **down and back** along the lower surface of the leading edge. More of the air then has to go round the nose to reach the upper surface, and the suction peak just behind the leading edge becomes sharper. As the angle of attack decreases, the stagnation point moves up towards the nose, and at negative angles onto the upper surface. `081.01.02.02.02`

### 2.3 Pressure distribution

The air over the upper surface moves faster than the free stream and has a lower static pressure, which acts as **suction**. Under the lower surface at positive angles of attack the air moves a little slower and the pressure is slightly **above** ambient. Lift is the sum of the pressure difference between the two surfaces, and at normal angles of attack roughly two-thirds of it comes from the suction on top. The suction is greatest a short way behind the leading edge, the **suction peak**, and decreases towards the trailing edge, where the two surfaces return to about ambient pressure. `081.01.02.03.01`

Increasing the angle of attack strengthens the suction peak and moves it forward, and raises the pressure under the lower surface. Adding camber spreads the suction along more of the chord and increases the lift at any angle of attack, so a cambered section gives lift even at zero angle of attack, while a symmetrical one gives none there.

### 2.4 Centre of pressure and aerodynamic centre

The **centre of pressure** (CP) is where the resultant aerodynamic force acts: the point about which the pressure distribution has no moment. On a cambered section it moves as the angle of attack changes. As the angle of attack increases, the suction peak moves forward and the CP moves **forward**, towards about 25–30 % of the chord, until the stall, when it moves back sharply. At small angles of attack the CP of a cambered wing is far aft. That movement makes the CP awkward for stability work.

The **aerodynamic centre** (AC) is the fixed point, about 25 % of the chord in subsonic flow, about which the pitching moment stays constant as the angle of attack changes. Lift changes act at the AC, and the constant camber moment is added to them. For a symmetrical section the AC and CP coincide. `081.01.02.04.01`

> **Exam trap.** The CP moves; the AC does not. For a positively cambered aerofoil the CP moves forward as the angle of attack increases, up to the stall.

### 2.5 Drag and the wake

Two physical phenomena cause the drag of an aerofoil: **friction** in the thin layer of air next to the surface, and the **pressure difference** between the front and back of the body. `081.01.02.06.01`

**Skin friction drag** is caused by the viscosity of the air: in the boundary layer the air is slowed down by the surface, and the surface is dragged backwards in return. It depends on the wetted area, the surface roughness and whether the boundary layer is laminar or turbulent; a turbulent boundary layer has much higher friction. `081.01.02.06.02`

**Form (pressure) drag** arises because the flow does not fully recover its pressure at the back of the body. The boundary layer thickens and may separate, leaving a low-pressure region behind; the higher pressure on the front then pushes the body backwards. It depends on the shape: blunt bodies with separated flow have high form drag, streamlined bodies low. `081.01.02.06.03`

Both appear as a **wake**: a region of slowed-down, often turbulent air trailing the body. The air in the wake has lost speed, so it has lost momentum; by Newton's laws the force that took that momentum out of the air is the drag. The energy that the engines must supply to overcome drag ends up as the motion and heat of the wake. `081.01.02.06.04`

### 2.6 Lift and the angle of attack

As the angle of attack increases from small values, the flow is turned more strongly, the suction on top deepens and the pressure underneath rises, so the lift increases, almost in proportion. This continues up to a point where the air can no longer follow the upper surface: the flow separates, and lift stops increasing and then falls (the stall, Chapter 7). `081.01.02.07.01`

### 2.7 The lift curve

The **CL–α graph** (the lift curve) plots the lift coefficient against the angle of attack. Over most of its range it is a **straight line**; near the top it curves over to a maximum and then drops away. `081.01.02.09.01`

Three points on it matter. `081.01.02.09.02`

- Where the curve **crosses the horizontal axis**, CL = 0: the **zero-lift angle of attack**. It is 0° for a symmetrical section and negative (about −2° to −4°) for a positively cambered one.
- Where the curve **crosses the vertical axis**, α = 0: the lift coefficient at zero angle of attack. It is zero for a symmetrical section and positive for a cambered one.
- The **maximum** of the curve: **CLMAX**, reached at the **critical angle of attack** (αCRIT, the stalling angle), typically 15–20° for a wing without high-lift devices.

### 2.8 Why coefficients

The force on a wing depends on its size, the air density, the speed and its shape and angle of attack. Coefficients separate the part that depends only on shape and angle of attack from the rest. A **coefficient** is a dimensionless number: the force divided by the dynamic pressure and the wing area. Once it is known for a shape, the force can be worked out for any size, speed and density, and results from a wind tunnel model apply to the full-size aeroplane. `081.01.03.01.01`

### 2.9 The lift formula

**L = CL × ½ ρ V² × S**

Lift is the lift coefficient times the dynamic pressure times the wing area. So lift is proportional to the **density**, to the **square of the TAS** (or simply to the square of the EAS, since ½ρV² = ½ρ0 EAS²), to the **wing area** and to the **lift coefficient**, which depends on the angle of attack and the shape. Doubling the speed at the same angle of attack gives four times the lift. `081.01.03.02.01`

> **Worked example · lift.** A wing of 30 m² at CL 0.5 flies at 60 m/s TAS at sea level (ρ = 1.225 kg/m³). L = 0.5 × ½ × 1.225 × 60² × 30 = 0.5 × 2205 × 30 ≈ **33 kN**, enough to hold up a mass of about 3370 kg.

**CL** depends in low subsonic flight on the **angle of attack**, the **shape** of the section (camber, thickness, nose radius), the **planform** and aspect ratio, the **configuration** (flaps and slats), and the state of the **surface** (contamination, ice). It is not affected by speed or density as such, which already appear in the formula, except through Mach number effects at high speed (Chapter 6). `081.01.03.02.05`

**Camber** shifts the lift curve. A symmetrical section's curve passes through the origin. A positively cambered section's curve is moved **up and to the left**: it gives lift at zero angle of attack, its zero-lift angle is negative, and its CLMAX is usually somewhat higher. A negatively cambered section's curve is moved down and to the right. The slope stays about the same. `081.01.03.02.02`

A **fast aerofoil**, thin and with little camber and a sharp nose, has a lower CLMAX and stalls at a lower angle of attack, often abruptly. A **slow aerofoil**, thicker, more cambered and with a larger nose radius, has a higher CLMAX at a higher stalling angle and a gentler stall. `081.01.03.02.03`

**CLMAX** is the highest lift coefficient the wing can reach; **αCRIT** is the angle of attack at which it occurs. Beyond αCRIT the lift coefficient falls: the wing has stalled. `081.01.03.02.04`

### 2.10 The drag formula

Drag follows the same pattern:

**D = CD × ½ ρ V² × S**

with CD the drag coefficient, depending on shape, angle of attack and surface. For a two-dimensional section, CD is made up of friction and form drag (the profile drag), and at a given CD drag is proportional to the dynamic pressure. `081.01.03.03.01`

The drag coefficient of a body depends strongly on its **shape**: a flat plate across the flow has a CD of about 1.2, a sphere about 0.5, and a streamlined body only about 0.05 for the same frontal area. A larger **cross-sectional (frontal) area** gives more drag for the same CD, which is why drag coefficients must always say which reference area they use. A **rough surface** makes the boundary layer turbulent earlier and raises the friction drag. `081.01.03.03.02`

### Check yourself

1. Where on the section is the static pressure highest? *At the stagnation point, where the air is brought to rest.*
2. Which way does the stagnation point move as the angle of attack increases? *Down and back, along the lower surface of the leading edge.*
3. Which surface gives most of the lift? *The upper surface, through suction.*
4. What are the two physical causes of drag? *Friction in the boundary layer, and pressure (form) differences between the front and back.*
5. What does camber do to the lift curve? *Moves it up and to the left: lift at zero angle of attack and a negative zero-lift angle, with about the same slope.*
6. Speed doubles at the same angle of attack and density. What happens to lift? *It increases four times.*
7. Which kind of aerofoil has the higher CLMAX, thick and cambered or thin and symmetrical? *Thick and cambered.*
