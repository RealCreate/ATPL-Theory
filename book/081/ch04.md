## Chapter 4 · Total Drag, Speed and Ground Effect

Drag comes in two families that behave in opposite ways: parasite drag grows with speed and induced drag shrinks. Their sum has a minimum, and that minimum, the speed VMD, organises much of what follows: speed stability, best glide, endurance. This chapter builds the drag curve, shows how weight and altitude move it, and then looks at what happens to the induced part close to the ground.

### 4.1 Parasite and induced drag

**Total drag = parasite drag + induced drag.** Parasite drag is all the drag that does not come from producing lift; induced drag is the drag caused by producing it. `081.01.05.01.01`

Parasite drag itself has three parts. `081.01.05.02.01`

- **Form (pressure) drag** comes from the pressure difference between the front and the back of a body when the flow does not close smoothly behind it. It depends on the **shape** and the **frontal area**, and grows sharply if the flow separates; streamlining and fairings reduce it. `081.01.05.02.02`
- **Interference drag** appears where two parts meet, such as the wing and fuselage, a pylon and wing, or the tailplane and fin. Their boundary layers and pressure fields interact and the combined drag exceeds the sum of the separate drags. It depends on the **angle** between the surfaces and how the joint is shaped; **fillets** and fairings reduce it. `081.01.05.02.03`
- **Friction drag** is the shear in the boundary layer. It depends on the **wetted area**, the **surface roughness** (dirt, insects, rivets, paint, ice) and the proportion of **laminar or turbulent** boundary layer: a turbulent layer has several times the friction of a laminar one. `081.01.05.02.04`

Parasite drag is proportional to the dynamic pressure, so at constant configuration it grows with the **square of the speed** (IAS or EAS): twice the speed gives four times the parasite drag. `081.01.05.03.01`

### 4.2 The drag curve

Plotting drag against speed for an aeroplane in level flight at a given weight gives two opposite curves: parasite drag rising as V², induced drag falling as 1/V². Their sum, the **total drag**, is a U-shaped curve. `081.01.05.05.01`

The bottom of that curve is the **minimum drag speed**, **VMD**. There parasite drag and induced drag are **equal**, and the aeroplane is flying at the angle of attack for **(L/D)max**. Flying faster, parasite drag dominates; flying slower, induced drag dominates. `081.01.05.05.02`

> **Exam trap.** VMD is where parasite and induced drag are equal, not where either one is smallest. It is also the speed for best L/D and best glide angle.

### 4.3 Weight and altitude

A **heavier** aeroplane needs more lift, so a higher CL at any speed and more induced drag. The whole curve moves **up and to the right**: the minimum drag is higher (proportional to the weight, since L/D at VMD is unchanged) and VMD is higher (proportional to √W). `081.01.05.07.01`

**Pressure altitude** has a different effect depending on which speed is on the horizontal axis. Against **IAS** (more strictly EAS), the drag curve **does not change** with altitude, because drag depends on dynamic pressure, which a given EAS fixes. Against **TAS**, the curve moves **to the right**: the same drag is reached at a higher TAS in thinner air. VMD in TAS terms therefore increases with altitude, while VMD as an IAS stays the same. `081.01.05.07.02`

### 4.4 Speed stability

The drag curve also shows how the aeroplane reacts to a small change of speed at a fixed thrust setting. `081.01.05.07.03–04`

- **Above VMD** (the front side of the drag curve), a speed **decrease** reduces drag. With thrust unchanged, the surplus thrust accelerates the aeroplane back to its original speed; a speed increase raises drag and slows it down again. This region is **speed stable**.
- **At VMD**, drag hardly changes with a small change of speed: the speed is **neutral**, and it wanders.
- **Below VMD** (the back side of the drag curve, or the region of reversed command), a speed **decrease increases** drag. With thrust unchanged, drag now exceeds thrust, so the aeroplane slows down further, and drag rises further. This region is **speed unstable**.

In the unstable region a small speed loss, from a gust or from raising the nose to correct a low glide path on approach, grows by itself. The aeroplane keeps decelerating towards the stall, or, if the pilot holds height, keeps needing more thrust. It typically happens at low speed and high weight, on a slow approach or a long, slow climb, and is the reason approach speeds are kept above VMD or flown with autothrust. `081.01.05.07.05`

### 4.5 Ground effect

Within about one wingspan of the ground, and strongly within half a span, the ground gets in the way of the downwash and of the tip vortices. The vortices cannot develop fully, the downwash behind the wing is reduced and the airflow behind it is less deflected. `081.01.06.01.01`

With less downwash, the **induced angle of attack** is smaller and the lift vector is tilted back less, so the **induced drag coefficient falls**. At a height of one-tenth of the span, CDi can be roughly halved. `081.01.06.01.02`

So at a given geometric angle of attack the **effective angle of attack is higher** and the wing gives **more lift** (a higher CL). Seen on the lift curve, ground effect makes it steeper. `081.01.06.03.01`

Because the effective angle of attack is higher, the wing reaches its critical angle at a **lower geometric angle of attack**: in ground effect **αCRIT is reduced**. `081.01.06.02.01`

**Entering** ground effect, for example in the landing flare, the aeroplane experiences: `081.01.06.01.03`

- reduced induced drag, so it **floats** and loses speed slowly;
- more lift at the same attitude;
- reduced downwash at the tailplane, which increases its angle of attack and gives a **nose-down** pitching tendency;
- a change in the static pressure at the static ports, which can make the airspeed indicator read differently.

**Leaving** ground effect after take-off reverses these: induced drag increases, the aeroplane needs a higher angle of attack for the same lift, it tends to **pitch nose-up**, and if it lifts off too early at too low a speed it may not be able to climb out of ground effect, or may sink back.

For **take-off and landing**, ground effect lets an aeroplane lift off at a lower speed than it could sustain in free air, which can lead to a premature lift-off and a sink after leaving ground effect; on landing it lengthens the float, especially if the approach speed is too high, and so the landing distance. `081.01.06.04.01`

A **low-wing** aeroplane has its wing much closer to the ground, so it feels ground effect more strongly than a **high-wing** aeroplane: it floats more on landing and its take-off behaviour changes more. `081.01.06.04.02`

### 4.6 Lift coefficient and speed

In steady, straight and level flight, lift equals weight: W = CL × ½ρV² × S. At a given weight and altitude, **CL × V² is constant**. Fly faster and CL must fall (lower angle of attack); fly slower and CL must rise. `081.01.07.01.01`

> **Worked example · CL and speed.** At 200 kt the aeroplane flies at CL 0.4. At 140 kt the same lift needs CL = 0.4 × (200/140)² = 0.4 × 2.04 ≈ **0.82**. If CLMAX is 1.2, the stall speed is 200 × √(0.4/1.2) ≈ **115 kt**.

Plotted, CL against speed is a curve falling steeply at low speed and flattening at high speed (CL ∝ 1/V²). Reading along it, any change in CL at a given weight means a change of speed: the minimum speed in level flight is where CL reaches CLMAX, the stall speed. `081.01.07.02.01`

### Check yourself

1. What three drags make up parasite drag? *Form (pressure), interference and friction drag.*
2. What happens to parasite drag when the IAS doubles? *It increases four times.*
3. At VMD, how do parasite and induced drag compare? *They are equal.*
4. How does a weight increase move the drag–speed curve? *Up and to the right; VMD rises with √W.*
5. Does VMD as an IAS change with altitude? *No; as a TAS it increases.*
6. You are below VMD on approach and the speed drops. Without a thrust change, what happens? *Drag rises, so the speed keeps falling: the region is speed unstable.*
7. In ground effect, what happens to induced drag and to αCRIT? *Induced drag falls; αCRIT is reduced.*
8. Which feels ground effect more, a low-wing or a high-wing aeroplane? *The low-wing aeroplane.*
