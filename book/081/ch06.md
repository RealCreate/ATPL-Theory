## Chapter 6 · High-Speed Flight

Below about Mach 0.4 the air behaves as if it were incompressible. A jet cruises at Mach 0.78–0.85, where it is not: the air is squeezed as it flows round the wing, local flow becomes supersonic, shock waves form, and lift, drag, pitching moment and control effectiveness all change. This chapter explains why, and how swept, thin and supercritical wings put those effects off to a higher Mach number.

### 6.1 The speed of sound

The **speed of sound** is the speed at which a small pressure disturbance travels through the air. `081.02.01.01.01` It depends **only on the air temperature**:

**a = √(γ R T)**, or in knots, **a ≈ 38.95 √T** (T in kelvin)

Warmer air carries sound faster. `081.02.01.01.03` In ISA it is about **661 kt** at sea level (15 °C) and falls with the temperature as altitude increases, to about **573 kt** at the tropopause (−56.5 °C, 36 089 ft). Above the tropopause, where the temperature is constant, it stays constant. `081.02.01.01.02`

### 6.2 Mach number

The **Mach number** is the true airspeed divided by the local speed of sound: **M = TAS / a**. Mach 0.8 means flying at 80 % of the speed of sound in the air around the aeroplane. `081.02.01.02.01`

At a constant **flight level and CAS**, the Mach number does **not** change with temperature. A warmer day raises the speed of sound, but it lowers the density at that pressure level, so the same CAS gives a higher TAS in the same proportion. Mach number is a function of the dynamic and static pressures only, which a given CAS and pressure altitude fix. `081.02.01.03.01`

In a **climb at constant IAS (CAS)**, TAS rises as density falls and the speed of sound falls with temperature, so the **Mach number rises**. In a **climb at constant Mach number**, TAS falls as the speed of sound falls (below the tropopause), and IAS falls faster. Because IAS is falling, the aeroplane needs a higher **CL** and so a higher **angle of attack**; at a given vertical speed the **flight-path angle** increases as TAS falls, and the **pitch attitude** (angle of attack plus flight-path angle) rises. The reverse happens in a descent. `081.02.01.03.02`

Three risks follow. `081.02.01.03.03`

- **Descending at constant Mach number**, IAS increases steadily and may exceed **VMO**. Airliners therefore change from Mach to IAS at the crossover altitude in the descent.
- **Climbing at constant IAS**, Mach number increases steadily and may exceed **MMO**. Hence the change from IAS to Mach at the crossover in the climb.
- **Climbing at too low a Mach number** at high altitude, IAS becomes very low, the aeroplane flies at high CL, and it can approach the **low-speed stall**, made worse by compressibility lowering CLMAX.

### 6.3 Compressibility

At low speed the pressure changes around a wing are too small to change the air's density noticeably. From about **Mach 0.4**, in high subsonic flow, they are not: the density changes along a streamline. That is **compressibility**, and it continues through transonic and supersonic flight. `081.02.01.04.01` Mach number is the measure of how much compressibility matters. `081.02.01.04.03`

Compressibility makes the pressure gradients round the wing, especially near the leading edge where the flow accelerates sharply, reach separation sooner at high angles of attack. The syllabus sums this up as compressibility worsening the pressure gradient and **reducing CL overall**; the clearest effect is a **lower CLMAX**. `081.02.01.04.02` Consequently the **stall speed (as an IAS) is higher** and **αCRIT is lower** at high Mach numbers than at low speed. `081.02.01.04.04`

### 6.4 Flow regimes

The flow round an aeroplane is divided into four regimes. `081.02.01.05.01–02`

- **Subsonic, below compressibility** (below about M 0.4): density practically constant; the low-speed theory of the earlier chapters applies.
- **Subsonic, above compressibility** (about M 0.4 up to MCRIT): the flow is subsonic everywhere, but density changes are significant; pressure coefficients and lift-curve slope increase with Mach number.
- **Transonic** (from MCRIT to about M 1.3): the flow is mixed, partly subsonic and partly supersonic, with shock waves on the wing. Lift, drag and pitching moment change rapidly.
- **Supersonic** (above about M 1.3): the flow is supersonic everywhere except in small regions, with shock waves at the nose and leading edges.

Many transport aeroplanes cruise **slightly above MCRIT**. A small, weak shock costs little drag, while the higher speed gives better productivity and, with a supercritical wing, a better specific range. They stay below the Mach number at which drag rises sharply. `081.02.01.05.03`

### 6.5 Shock waves

A **shock wave** is a very thin region, a fraction of a millimetre thick, in which supersonic flow is slowed abruptly and its pressure, temperature and density jump. It forms where supersonic flow has to slow down or turn, and disturbances cannot travel ahead of it to warn the air. `081.02.02.01.01`

Across a **normal shock wave**, one at right angles to the flow, the air changes as follows. `081.02.02.02.01`

> **Key numbers · across a normal shock**
>
> | Quantity | Change |
> | --- | --- |
> | Velocity | decreases (supersonic to subsonic) |
> | Mach number | decreases, to below 1 |
> | Static pressure | increases |
> | Static temperature | increases |
> | Density | increases |
> | Local speed of sound | increases (temperature rises) |
> | Total pressure | decreases (energy lost) |
> | Total temperature | unchanged |

On a wing, the normal shock stands roughly **perpendicular to the surface**, at the back of the region of supersonic flow above the wing. `081.02.02.02.02`

As the Mach number increases at positive lift: `081.02.02.02.03`

- the supersonic region on the upper surface grows and the shock becomes **stronger**;
- the shock moves **aft** towards the trailing edge;
- a **second shock** appears on the **lower surface**, usually at a slightly higher Mach number, and also moves aft, often reaching the trailing edge first.

At a constant Mach number, **increasing the angle of attack** speeds up the flow over the upper surface: the upper shock becomes **stronger** and moves **forward**, while the lower-surface shock weakens. `081.02.02.02.04`

### 6.6 The critical Mach number

The **critical Mach number** (MCRIT) is the free-stream Mach number at which the airflow **somewhere on the aeroplane first reaches the local speed of sound** (local Mach 1). Below it the flow is subsonic everywhere. `081.02.03.01.01`

Anything that accelerates the flow over the wing more lowers MCRIT. `081.02.03.01.02`

- A higher **angle of attack** (higher CL) lowers it.
- A higher **weight** needs more CL at the same speed and lowers it.
- **Manoeuvres** with load factor above 1 need more CL and lower it.
- A **forward CG** needs a larger down-load on the tail, so the wing must give more lift, and lowers it slightly; an aft CG raises it.

### 6.7 Lift above MCRIT

At **constant angle of attack**, CL first **rises** with Mach number above about M 0.4 (compressibility strengthens the suction), then, beyond MCRIT, the shock and shock-induced separation make it **fall**, and in supersonic flow it falls further. `081.02.03.02.01`

Exceeding MCRIT therefore reduces both the CL at a given angle of attack and **CLMAX**, because shock-induced separation occurs at lower angles of attack than low-speed separation. `081.02.03.02.02`

At high altitude a given IAS means a higher Mach number. **CLMAX falls with Mach number**, so the **stall IAS rises with altitude**. At low altitude the stall IAS is constant; at high altitude, the low-speed stall (or buffet) IAS increases, and the margin between the minimum and maximum speeds narrows (Chapter 7). `081.02.03.02.03` Shock-induced separation also makes **αCRIT smaller** at high Mach number. `081.02.03.02.04`

A wing with a **supercritical aerofoil** is designed to fly above MCRIT with a weak shock. Exceeding MCRIT then brings several advantages: a higher cruise speed for little extra drag (a better **speed-to-drag ratio**), a better **specific range** (distance per unit of fuel, which depends on TAS × L/D), and a higher **optimum altitude**. `081.02.03.02.05`

### 6.8 Drag above MCRIT

**Wave drag** is the extra drag caused by shock waves: energy lost in the shock itself, and the separation of the boundary layer it causes behind it (shock-induced separation). `081.02.03.03.01`

At **constant angle of attack**, CD is nearly constant up to MCRIT, then rises slowly, then very steeply from the **drag divergence Mach number** (MDD), reaching a peak near Mach 1 before falling again in supersonic flow. `081.02.03.03.02` On the **polar**, increasing Mach number moves the curve to higher drag, with a lower maximum L/D, once the critical Mach number is exceeded. `081.02.03.03.03`

Beyond **MDD** the aeroplane meets: `081.02.03.03.04`

- a steep **drag rise**, needing much more thrust for little extra speed;
- **instability**, as shock positions on the two wings and the tail change;
- **Mach tuck**, a nose-down pitching moment (Section 6.9);
- a **shock stall**, the loss of lift caused by shock-induced separation, with buffet.

**MDD** is always **higher** than MCRIT: shocks first form at MCRIT, but the drag only rises sharply when they become strong enough to cause separation. For a typical airliner MDD is a few hundredths of a Mach number above MCRIT. `081.02.03.03.05`

### 6.9 Pitching moment and Mach tuck

As the Mach number increases beyond MCRIT the shock moves **aft**, and the region of low pressure behind it grows towards the trailing edge, so the **centre of pressure moves aft**. `081.02.03.04.01` On a swept wing, shock-induced separation tends to begin near the roots, losing lift ahead of the CG while the tips, behind it, keep theirs; and the reduced downwash at the tail raises its angle of attack. Together they give a **nose-down pitching moment** that grows with Mach number: **Mach tuck** or tuck-under. If uncorrected, the aeroplane pitches down, speeds up, and the nose-down moment grows further. `081.02.03.04.02`

To compensate, high-speed aeroplanes have a **Mach trim system**. `081.02.03.04.03` It senses the Mach number and adjusts the elevator or stabiliser trim nose-up automatically, in proportion to Mach number above a threshold, so the stick force gradient with speed stays positive and the pilot does not have to push less to fly faster. `081.02.03.04.04`

If the Mach trim fails, the aeroplane flight manual imposes a **lower maximum Mach number**: the pilot reduces speed and stays below that limit, so that Mach tuck stays within the range where the natural stability is enough. `081.02.03.04.05`

### 6.10 Controls at high Mach number

At high Mach number the shock wave on a wing or tailplane can sit **ahead of the control surface**. Behind the shock the flow is separated and the pressure changes made by deflecting the surface cannot travel forward through the shock. **Control effectiveness is reduced**, the surface can buzz or vibrate, and in extreme cases hinge moments reverse. That is one reason for all-moving tailplanes and spoilers on high-speed aeroplanes. `081.02.03.05.01`

### 6.11 Raising MCRIT

**Sweepback** is the most powerful tool. A swept wing feels mainly the component of the airflow **perpendicular to its quarter-chord line**, V × cos Λ (Λ the sweep angle). The flow along the span does little to accelerate the air over the section. The wing behaves as if the Mach number were reduced by cos Λ, and it also looks **thinner**: the effective thickness-to-chord ratio is reduced because the chord measured along the flow is longer. **MCRIT rises**. `081.02.05.01.01`

> **Worked example · sweep.** At M 0.85 with 30° of sweep, the component perpendicular to the leading edge is 0.85 × cos 30° ≈ **M 0.74**. With 35° of sweep it would be about **M 0.70**.

Sweepback has a price at low speed. `081.02.05.01.02`

- The effective speed over the section is lower, so **CLMAX is lower**, and the lift curve is less steep.
- **High-lift devices** are less efficient on a swept wing, but more are needed: airliners use powerful multiple-slotted flaps and slats.
- Spanwise flow towards the tips thickens the boundary layer there, and the tips tend to **stall first**. The tips are behind the CG, so losing their lift gives a **pitch-up**, which drives the angle of attack further into the stall.

Sweep reduces **wave drag** at high subsonic speed by raising MCRIT and MDD. At low speed it slightly increases induced drag, because swept wings tend to have a lower effective aspect ratio and less efficient lift distribution. `081.02.05.01.03`

**Thin aerofoils with little camber** accelerate the flow less over the upper surface, so the local Mach number stays lower and MCRIT is higher. Their cost is a lower CLMAX and a sharper stall, and less room for structure and fuel. `081.02.05.02.01`

The **supercritical aerofoil** is designed to delay and weaken the shock. Its upper surface is **flattened**, so the flow accelerates only moderately and stays supersonic over a long, nearly constant region that ends in a **weak shock** well aft; much of the lift comes from **aft camber** (rear loading) on the lower surface. `081.02.05.02.02` Its advantages are a higher **MDD** for a given thickness, or a **thicker wing** for the same MDD (more room for fuel and structure, lighter, allowing less sweep or a higher aspect ratio), and less wave drag. Its disadvantages are a large **nose-down pitching moment** from the rear loading, which must be trimmed, a sensitivity to small changes in shape, and more complex manufacturing. `081.02.05.02.04`

**Vortex generators** ahead of the shock position mix energetic air into the boundary layer, so it can withstand the sharp pressure rise across the shock without separating. They reduce shock-induced separation, buffet and control-surface buzz. `081.02.05.03.01`
