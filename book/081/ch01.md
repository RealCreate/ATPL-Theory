## Chapter 1 · Air, Pressure and the Shape of a Wing

Principles of flight starts with the air itself: what it weighs, how its pressure and density change, and how energy is traded between pressure and speed as it flows. From that come the airspeeds a pilot reads and the words used to describe an aerofoil and a wing. Everything later in the subject, lift, drag, stalls and stability, is built on these definitions.

### 1.1 Units, mass and Newton's laws

The exam uses SI units throughout. `081.01.01.01.01`

> **Key numbers · SI units**
>
> | Quantity | SI unit |
> | --- | --- |
> | Mass | kilogram (kg) |
> | Acceleration | metre per second squared (m/s²) |
> | Force, weight | newton (N = kg·m/s²) |
> | Velocity | metre per second (m/s) |
> | Energy | joule (J = N·m) |
> | Power | watt (W = J/s) |
> | Density | kilogram per cubic metre (kg/m³) |
> | Temperature | kelvin (K) |
> | Pressure | pascal (Pa = N/m²) |
> | Wing loading | newton per square metre (N/m²) |

**Mass** is the amount of matter in a body and a measure of its resistance to acceleration (its inertia); it is the same everywhere. A **force** is what changes a body's state of rest or motion. **Acceleration** is the rate of change of velocity, in speed or in direction. **Weight** is the force of gravity on a mass: W = m × g, with g ≈ 9.81 m/s², so a 70 000 kg aeroplane weighs about 687 kN. `081.01.01.01.02`

Newton's three laws describe how forces and motion are linked. `081.01.01.01.03`

1. A body stays at rest, or keeps moving in a straight line at constant speed, unless a resultant force acts on it. An aeroplane in steady, straight and level flight therefore has all its forces in balance.
2. A resultant force produces an acceleration proportional to it and in its direction: **F = m × a**. Any change of speed or direction, including a turn, needs a resultant force.
3. Every action has an equal and opposite reaction. A wing pushes air downwards, and the air pushes the wing upwards; a propeller or jet accelerates air backwards and is pushed forwards.

### 1.2 Density, pressure and temperature

**Air density** (ρ, rho) is the mass of air per unit volume, about 1.225 kg/m³ at sea level in ISA. Aerodynamic forces depend directly on it, because a wing makes its force by changing the momentum of the air it meets. `081.01.01.01.04`

Density is set by **pressure, temperature and humidity**. `081.01.01.01.05` Higher pressure packs more molecules into a volume, so density rises with pressure; higher temperature spreads them out, so density falls with temperature. Humid air is slightly less dense than dry air, because water vapour molecules are lighter than the nitrogen and oxygen they replace. Climbing, pressure falls faster than temperature, so density falls with altitude: at about 22 000 ft it is half its sea-level value. `081.01.01.01.06`

The three are linked by the **equation of state** for a perfect gas, **p = ρ × R × T**, with T in kelvin and R the gas constant for air (287 J/kg/K). At constant pressure, density is inversely proportional to absolute temperature; at constant temperature, it is proportional to pressure. `081.01.01.01.15`

> **Exam trap.** A hot, high, humid day gives the lowest density and the poorest performance. Remember to use kelvin in the gas law: 15 °C is 288 K.

### 1.3 Static, dynamic and total pressure

**Static pressure** (p) is the pressure the air exerts on a surface moving with it, acting equally in all directions; it is the pressure of the still atmosphere. `081.01.01.01.07`

**Dynamic pressure** (q) is the pressure the air would add if it were brought to rest: the kinetic energy of the flow per unit volume. `081.01.01.01.08` Its formula is

**q = ½ ρ V²**

with ρ the density and V the true airspeed. `081.01.01.01.09` Dynamic pressure is what a wing actually feels: lift, drag, control forces and structural loads all scale with it. It is also what the airspeed indicator measures, so **IAS is a measure of dynamic pressure**. The same IAS means the same q, and so the same aerodynamic forces at the same angle of attack, at any altitude; at altitude, where ρ is lower, a higher TAS is needed to make that q. `081.01.01.01.10`

For steady, incompressible flow without friction, **Bernoulli's equation** says that along a streamline

**p + ½ ρ V² = constant = total pressure (pT)**

So where the air speeds up its static pressure falls, and where it slows down its static pressure rises: energy is traded between pressure and motion, but the total stays the same. `081.01.01.01.11`

**Total pressure** is static plus dynamic pressure, the pressure measured where the flow is brought fully to rest, as in a pitot tube. It is constant only within one system of flow. Energy added or removed changes it: total pressure rises through a propeller disc or a compressor, and falls through friction in a boundary layer, in a wake or across a shock wave. So the total pressure behind a propeller is not the same as in the free stream around it. `081.01.01.01.12`

### 1.4 Continuity and the venturi

The **equation of continuity** says that in steady flow the mass passing every cross-section of a stream tube per second is the same: **ρ × A × V = constant**. In incompressible flow ρ is constant, so **A × V = constant**: where the tube narrows the air speeds up, and where it widens it slows down. `081.01.01.01.16`

Combine continuity with Bernoulli in a **venturi**, a tube that narrows to a throat and widens again. At the throat the area is smallest, so the speed is highest and the static pressure lowest; downstream the speed and pressure return to their entry values (ignoring friction). The total pressure is the same all the way through. A wing does something similar: the curved upper surface narrows the stream tubes above it, speeding the air up and lowering its pressure. `081.01.01.01.13`

> **Worked example · continuity.** Air enters a venturi at 50 m/s through 0.2 m². At a throat of 0.1 m² it flows at 50 × 0.2 / 0.1 = **100 m/s**. Dynamic pressure rises from ½ × 1.225 × 50² ≈ 1531 Pa to ½ × 1.225 × 100² ≈ 6125 Pa, so the static pressure at the throat is **about 4594 Pa lower**.

### 1.5 From pitot-static pressures to airspeeds

The **pitot tube** faces the airflow and senses total pressure; the **static ports** sense static pressure. The airspeed indicator subtracts one from the other, pT − p = q, and is calibrated to show the speed that would give that q at ISA sea-level density. That reading is the **indicated airspeed**. `081.01.01.01.14`

The four airspeeds then follow one another. `081.01.01.01.17`

- **IAS** (indicated airspeed): the instrument reading.
- **CAS** (calibrated airspeed): IAS corrected for instrument error and position (pressure) error.
- **EAS** (equivalent airspeed): CAS corrected for compressibility. EAS fixes the dynamic pressure exactly: q = ½ ρ0 EAS². At low speed and altitude CAS ≈ EAS.
- **TAS** (true airspeed): the real speed through the air, EAS × √(ρ0 / ρ). At altitude TAS is well above EAS.

### 1.6 Airflow, streamlines and stream tubes

Flow is **steady** when the speed, pressure and direction at every fixed point stay the same with time; it is **unsteady** when they change, as in a gust, behind a stalled wing, or in a wake. `081.01.01.02.01`

A **streamline** is a line drawn so that it is everywhere parallel to the local flow; in steady flow it is also the path of each particle. Because flow is along streamlines, none crosses them. A bundle of streamlines forms a **stream tube**, whose walls no air crosses either. `081.01.01.02.02` Air in a stream tube behaves as if it were in a pipe: where the streamlines converge the tube narrows and the air speeds up with a fall in static pressure; where they diverge it slows down and the pressure rises (continuity and Bernoulli together). `081.01.01.02.03`

**Two-dimensional flow** is flow around a wing section of infinite span, the same in every slice, with no flow along the span. It describes an aerofoil. **Three-dimensional flow** is flow around a real wing of finite span, where air leaks round the tips, flows spanwise and forms tip vortices; it adds induced drag and changes the lift (Chapter 3). `081.01.01.02.04`

### 1.7 The aerodynamic force on an aerofoil

The air presses on every part of an aerofoil's surface and drags along it. Added up, these pressures and frictions give one **resultant aerodynamic force**, acting at a point on the chord line called the **centre of pressure** (CP). It points upwards and inclined backwards. `081.01.01.03.01`

The resultant is split into two components. **Lift** is the component perpendicular to the relative airflow (the undisturbed flow far ahead of the wing); **drag** is the component parallel to it, in the direction of the flow. `081.01.01.03.02` Lift is therefore not necessarily vertical: it is at right angles to the flight path, and in a climb or a turn it tilts with it. Drag always acts directly opposite to the flight path through the air. `081.01.01.03.03`

The **angle of attack** (α, alpha) of an aerofoil is the angle between its chord line and the relative airflow. `081.01.01.03.08`

Because the CP moves as the angle of attack changes, it is easier to place the lift and drag at a fixed point and add a **pitching (aerodynamic) moment** that does not change with angle of attack. That point is the **aerodynamic centre** (AC), about a quarter of the chord back from the leading edge in subsonic flow. The aerodynamic moment is the twisting moment of the aerodynamic force about the AC, and depends on the camber of the section, the dynamic pressure, and the wing's area and chord: M = CM × q × S × c. `081.01.01.03.04–05`

On a **symmetrical aerofoil** the CP stays at the AC, so the moment about the AC is zero. `081.01.01.03.06` A **positively cambered** aerofoil has a **nose-down** moment about the AC, constant with angle of attack; a negatively cambered one has a nose-up moment. `081.01.01.03.07`

### 1.8 The aerofoil section

The **leading edge** is the front of the section, the first part to meet the air, and the **trailing edge** the sharp rear edge where the upper and lower flows meet. `081.01.01.04.01–02` The **chord line** is the straight line joining the two; its length is the chord. `081.01.01.04.03`

The **thickness-to-chord ratio** (relative thickness) is the maximum thickness divided by the chord, as a percentage: around 12 % for a typical subsonic wing, less for high-speed wings. `081.01.01.04.04` The **location of maximum thickness** is given as a percentage of the chord from the leading edge; further aft favours laminar flow and higher critical Mach numbers. `081.01.01.04.05`

The **camber line** (mean line) runs midway between the upper and lower surfaces from leading to trailing edge. `081.01.01.04.06` **Camber** is the greatest distance between the camber line and the chord line, again as a percentage of chord. `081.01.01.04.07` The **nose radius** (leading-edge radius) describes how blunt the leading edge is: a large radius suits low speed and high lift, a sharp one suits high speed. `081.01.01.04.08`

A **symmetrical** section has no camber: the camber line and chord line coincide, and it gives no lift at zero angle of attack. An **asymmetrical** (cambered) section has a curved camber line and gives lift even at zero angle of attack. `081.01.01.04.09`

### 1.9 The wing

The **span** (b) is the tip-to-tip distance. `081.01.01.05.01` The **root chord** is the chord at the centreline, the **tip chord** the chord at the tip, and the **taper ratio** is tip chord ÷ root chord: 1 for a rectangular wing, 0 for a pointed delta. `081.01.01.05.02–03` The **wing area** (S) is the area of the planform projected onto a flat surface, normally including the part inside the fuselage. `081.01.01.05.04` The **planform** is the shape of the wing seen from above: rectangular, tapered, elliptical, swept or delta. `081.01.01.05.05`

The **mean geometric chord** is simply the area divided by the span, S / b. `081.01.01.05.06` The **mean aerodynamic chord** (MAC) is the chord of an imaginary rectangular wing that would have the same aerodynamic forces and moments as the real one; the CG position and its limits are given as a percentage of the MAC. `081.01.01.05.07`

The **aspect ratio** (A) is span ÷ mean chord, or **b² / S**. A long, slender wing has a high aspect ratio (a glider about 25, an airliner about 9), a short stubby one a low one. `081.01.01.05.08`

The **dihedral angle** is the upward angle of the wing from the root to the tip, seen from the front; a downward angle is **anhedral**. `081.01.01.05.09` The **sweep angle** is the angle between a spanwise line on the wing, usually the quarter-chord line, and the lateral axis. `081.01.01.05.10`

**Wing twist** varies the angle of attack along the span. **Geometric twist** (washout) reduces the angle of incidence of the sections from root to tip; **aerodynamic twist** does the same by changing the section shape, using less camber or a section with a lower zero-lift angle towards the tip. Both make the root stall before the tip (Chapter 7). `081.01.01.05.11`

The **angle of incidence** is the angle between the aeroplane's **longitudinal axis** and the **wing-root chord line**. It is fixed when the aeroplane is built. Some older books use "angle of incidence" for angle of attack; for the EASA exam they are different things. `081.01.01.05.12`

> **Worked example · aspect ratio.** A wing of 34 m span and 122 m² area has A = 34² / 122 = 1156 / 122 ≈ **9.5**. Its mean geometric chord is 122 / 34 ≈ **3.6 m**.

### Check yourself

4. A stream tube narrows from 3 m² to 1 m². What happens to the speed in incompressible flow? *It triples.*

6. Which way does a positively cambered aerofoil pitch about its AC? *Nose-down.*
