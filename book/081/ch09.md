## Chapter 9 · Control

Stability keeps the aeroplane where it is; control makes it go somewhere else. Every primary control works the same way, by changing the camber and angle of attack of a surface so that it makes a force far from the CG. This chapter covers the controls about the three axes, the side effects of rolling and yawing, the tabs and powered systems that keep control forces manageable, fly-by-wire laws, and trim.

### 9.1 Axes, angles and controls

The aeroplane moves about three axes through its CG, each at right angles to the other two. `081.05.01.01.01`

- The **lateral axis** runs from wing tip to wing tip; motion about it is **pitch**.
- The **longitudinal axis** runs from nose to tail; motion about it is **roll**.
- The **normal axis** runs vertically through the CG, perpendicular to the other two; motion about it is **yaw**.

The **pitch angle** is the angle between the longitudinal axis and the horizontal; the **bank angle** (φ) the angle between the lateral axis and the horizontal; the **yaw angle** the angle between the longitudinal axis and a reference direction in the horizontal plane (such as the original heading). `081.05.01.01.02`

Pitching moves the nose up and down, rolling lowers one wing and raises the other, and yawing swings the nose left and right. `081.05.01.01.03` The devices are the **elevator** (or all-moving tailplane) for pitch, the **ailerons** (helped by roll spoilers) for roll, and the **rudder** for yaw. `081.05.01.01.04`

Deflecting a control surface **changes the camber** of the surface it belongs to. Moving a trailing-edge surface down adds positive camber and increases the lift; moving it up reduces or reverses the camber and decreases the lift. `081.05.01.02.01` At the same time it changes the **local angle of attack**: the chord line from leading edge to the deflected trailing edge is tilted, so a downward deflection increases the effective angle of attack of that section and an upward one reduces it. That is also why deflecting an aileron down near the stall can stall that wing. `081.05.01.03.01`

### 9.2 Pitch control

The **elevator** is a hinged surface on the trailing edge of the tailplane. Moving it changes the tailplane's camber and lift; because the tail is far behind the CG, even a small force gives a large pitching moment. Pulling back raises the elevator, reduces the tail's lift (or increases its down-load) and pitches the nose up. An **all-flying tail** (stabilator) moves the whole tailplane, which is more powerful and stays effective at high Mach numbers, where a shock wave ahead of a hinged elevator would reduce its effect. `081.05.02.01.01`

The tailplane sits in the **downwash** from the wing. Its angle of attack is therefore less than that of the aeroplane, by the downwash angle. Anything that changes the downwash, such as flap extension, a change of angle of attack or ground effect, changes the tail's angle of attack and so the pitching moment, which the pilot must trim out. `081.05.02.02.01`

The **CG position** determines how much elevator is needed for a given response. With a **forward CG**, the tail arm is long but the aeroplane is very stable, so a larger elevator deflection (and force) is needed to change the attitude or load factor; with an **aft CG**, less. `081.05.02.04.01` The **forward CG limit** is therefore reached when the elevator, at full deflection, can only just achieve what is needed: rotation at take-off, and the flare at the landing speed with full flap in ground effect, where the downwash change makes the task hardest. `081.05.02.04.02`

**Engine thrust** also pitches the aeroplane if its line of action misses the CG. Engines mounted **low** (under the wing) give a **nose-up** moment when thrust is increased and nose-down when it is reduced; engines mounted **high** (on the rear fuselage or above the wing) give the opposite. The effect is strongest at low speed with large thrust changes, such as a go-around. `081.05.02.05.01`

### 9.3 Yaw control

The **rudder** is a hinged surface on the trailing edge of the fin. Deflecting it changes the fin's camber and side force, which, acting far behind the CG, yaws the aeroplane: the larger the deflection (and the dynamic pressure), the larger the yawing moment. Once the aeroplane yaws, it flies with **sideslip**; the fin, now meeting the air at an angle, produces a side force in the opposite sense, a restoring moment that balances the rudder at a steady sideslip angle. `081.05.03.01.01`

At high speed a full rudder deflection would produce side loads beyond what the fin is built for. Transport aeroplanes therefore **limit rudder travel** as speed increases, with a ratio changer or travel limiter driven by airspeed, so that full pedal gives only a small deflection at high speed. Even so, large alternating rudder inputs (reversals) can overload the fin and must be avoided. `081.05.03.02.01`

### 9.4 Roll control

The **ailerons** are hinged surfaces at the trailing edge of the outer wings, moving in opposite directions. The down aileron increases camber and lift on its wing, the up aileron reduces lift on the other, so the aeroplane rolls towards the up aileron. `081.05.04.01.01`

Aileron deflection has adverse side effects: it causes **adverse yaw** (Section 9.6) and twists the wing, which at high speed can reduce or reverse its effect. `081.05.04.01.02`

Many airliners have **inboard and outboard ailerons**. At low speed both are used for maximum roll power. At high speed the outboard ailerons, near the flexible tips, would produce large twisting loads, so only the stiffer, inboard ailerons are used. `081.05.04.01.03` The outboard ailerons are **locked out** above a given speed (or when the flaps are retracted) to prevent **over-control**, to avoid exceeding **structural limits**, and to avoid **aeroelastic** problems: flutter, divergence, and **aileron reversal**, in which the wing twists so much under the aileron's load that the change in angle of attack cancels or reverses the aileron's effect. `081.05.04.01.04`

Ailerons are used: `081.05.04.01.05`

- in **normal flight**, to enter and leave turns and to hold the wings level;
- in **flight with sideslip**, to hold the bank needed against the rolling moment that sideslip creates through lateral stability;
- in **crosswind landings**, into wind, to stop the drift or to hold the wing down in the wing-low (sideslip) technique;
- in **horizontal turns**, to roll in and out, with a small amount opposite to the turn sometimes needed to stop the bank increasing (overbanking);
- with **one engine inoperative**, to hold a small bank towards the live engine and counter the rolling moment from asymmetric thrust and slipstream.

The **roll rate** is the angular velocity about the longitudinal axis, in degrees per second. `081.05.04.01.06` It depends on the **aileron deflection** and size, the **dynamic pressure** (IAS), the **TAS** (roll damping makes the steady roll rate proportional to TAS for a given aileron deflection), the **span** (a long wing has more roll damping and inertia), and aeroelastic **wing twist**. `081.05.04.01.07`

**Flaperons** are surfaces that act as both flaps (moving down together) and ailerons (moving differentially). **Aileron droop** lowers both ailerons a little when the flaps are extended, so the whole trailing edge contributes extra lift. `081.05.04.01.08`

### 9.5 Roll spoilers

**Roll spoilers** rise on the wing that must go **down**, reducing its lift (and adding drag on that side). They can work together with the ailerons or, at high speed when the outboard ailerons are locked out, partly replace them. They do not twist the wing as an aileron does, and their extra drag on the descending wing helps yaw the aeroplane into the turn. `081.05.04.03.01`

### 9.6 Adverse yaw

The aileron going **down** increases its wing's lift and therefore its **induced drag**; the one going up reduces both. The extra drag on the rising wing pulls the nose **away from the turn**: **adverse yaw**. It is strongest at low speed and high CL. `081.05.04.04.01`

It is reduced by: `081.05.04.05.01`

- **Frise ailerons**: the up-going aileron's nose projects below the wing into the airflow, adding drag on the descending wing to balance the down aileron's drag.
- **Differential aileron deflection**: the up aileron moves further than the down one, increasing drag on the descending wing and limiting the induced drag of the rising wing.
- **Rudder–aileron cross-coupling**: an interconnection that applies some rudder automatically with aileron.
- **Roll spoilers**: they add drag on the descending wing, giving a favourable yaw.

### 9.7 Roll and yaw interaction

Rolling and yawing are coupled. `081.05.05.01.01–02`

- **Secondary effect of roll**: a bank makes the aeroplane slip towards the lower wing, and directional stability then yaws the nose towards the lower wing: rolling produces yaw.
- **Secondary effect of yaw**: a yaw makes the outer wing move faster and gain lift (and the sideslip, through lateral stability, raises the leading wing's lift), so the aeroplane rolls in the direction of the yaw: yawing produces roll.

### 9.8 Reducing control forces: aerodynamic balance

The force on the pilot's controls is set by the **hinge moment** of the surface: its aerodynamic load times the arm about the hinge. On a large or fast aeroplane it would be too heavy to move by hand. **Aerodynamic balance** reduces the hinge moment by placing part of the surface's area or of the force ahead of the hinge, where the air helps the surface move. `081.05.06.01.01`

- A **horn balance** extends part of the surface, usually at its tip, ahead of the hinge line; the air pressure on it helps deflect the surface. `081.05.06.01.02`
- An **internal balance** places a flat plate ahead of the hinge inside a sealed chamber in the wing or tail; the pressure difference between the upper and lower surfaces acts on the plate and helps move the surface. `081.05.06.01.03`

Tabs, small surfaces on the trailing edge of the control surface, also change the hinge moment. `081.05.06.01.04`

- A **balance tab** is geared to move **opposite** to the control surface. Its force helps move the surface and reduces the pilot's force.
- An **anti-balance tab** is geared to move in the **same** direction, increasing the force. It is used to add feel to an over-light control, such as an all-moving tailplane.
- A **servo tab** is moved directly by the pilot; its aerodynamic force moves the surface, which floats freely. The pilot only has to move the tab.
- A **spring tab** is connected through a spring: at low speed the pilot moves the surface almost directly; at high speed, as loads build, the spring deflects and the tab helps more, like a servo tab.

### 9.9 Powered controls

With **power-assisted** controls, the pilot still moves the surface through a mechanical linkage, but hydraulic jacks add force; the pilot feels a fraction of the real aerodynamic load. With **fully powered** controls, the actuators move the surface entirely and the pilot feels **no** aerodynamic load at all: the control is irreversible. `081.05.06.02.01–02`

Because a fully powered control gives no natural feel, an **artificial feel** system is added: springs and a feel unit that increases the force with dynamic pressure (q-feel). It gives the pilot a sense of speed and load, prevents over-controlling and overstressing, and keeps control forces similar to those of a well-designed manual aeroplane. `081.05.06.02.03`

### 9.10 Fly-by-wire

In a **fly-by-wire** (FBW) aeroplane the controls send electrical signals to computers, which move the surfaces according to a **control law**.

In pitch, the side stick or column typically commands a **load factor** or **pitch rate** (or a blend) rather than an elevator deflection. With the stick released the aeroplane holds its **flight path**, and trims itself automatically, adjusting for speed, configuration and thrust changes. `081.05.07.01.01`

The computers know the aeroplane's **CG position** (from fuel and loading data). They can then adjust the control gains so that the handling is the same at every CG, allow the aeroplane to be flown with an aft CG and smaller tail for lower drag (relaxed stability), and use the CG in protections and in fuel transfer to the tail. `081.05.07.01.02`

If sensors or computers fail, the system reverts to **degraded laws**, such as an **alternate law** with fewer protections or a **direct law**, in which stick deflection maps directly to surface deflection, as in a conventional aeroplane, with manual trim. In the last resort a mechanical backup may remain. `081.05.07.01.03`

**Protections** keep the aeroplane inside its envelope (angle of attack, load factor, pitch and bank attitude, overspeed). **Hard protections** cannot be overridden by the pilot: whatever the stick input, the aeroplane will not exceed the limit (the approach used by Airbus). **Soft protections** increase the stick force or give warnings near the limit, but the pilot can exceed the limit with extra force (the approach used by Boeing). `081.05.07.01.04`

### 9.11 Trim

**Trimming** removes the steady control force needed to hold an attitude or speed, so that the pilot does not have to hold it. A change of speed, configuration, power or CG changes the balance of moments and therefore the force needed; trim cancels it, reducing fatigue and freeing the pilot to fly accurately. `081.05.08.01.01`

The difference from balance tabs: a **trim tab** is set independently by the pilot (or autopilot) and stays in position, setting the **zero-force** condition; a **balance tab** moves automatically with the control surface and changes the **force needed for each deflection**, without changing the trimmed condition. `081.05.08.01.02`

A **trim tab** is a small hinged surface on the elevator (or rudder or aileron) trailing edge, set from the cockpit with a wheel or switch. Moving the tab one way makes the control surface float the other way: to hold the elevator **up** (nose-up trim), the trim tab moves **down**. The **flight deck indication** shows the trim setting, for example a pointer marked nose-up and nose-down. `081.05.08.02.01`

On jet transports the whole horizontal **stabiliser** is moved instead: a **trimmable horizontal stabiliser** (THS), driven by a motor and screwjack. The elevator then streamlines with it once trimmed. The stabiliser position appears on a trim indicator, often graduated in units or degrees, with a green band for take-off. `081.05.08.03.01`

Compared with a trim tab, a stabiliser trim: `081.05.08.03.02`

- gives a much **larger trim range**, needed for big CG and speed ranges;
- keeps the **elevator streamlined**, so less drag, and keeps its **full travel available** for control;
- but is heavier and more complex, and a **runaway or jammed stabiliser** is far more serious, because it is a very powerful surface.

The **take-off trim setting** depends on the **CG**. A **forward CG** needs more **nose-up** stabiliser trim, so the rotation force is normal; an aft CG needs less. The setting is calculated from the load sheet's CG (and sometimes thrust and flap), and a take-off configuration warning sounds if it is outside the green band. `081.05.08.03.03`

If the trim is set wrongly for take-off: `081.05.08.03.04`

- **too nose-down** (or the CG further aft than assumed): rotation needs a **heavy pull** and is slow, the lift-off is delayed and the take-off run longer;
- **too nose-up** (or the CG further forward than assumed): the aeroplane rotates **early** or by itself, may **over-rotate**, risks a tail strike and lifts off at too low a speed.

A **runaway** stabiliser moves continuously, uncommanded, producing a rapidly growing out-of-trim force; it must be stopped at once with the trim cut-out switches, then trimmed manually if possible. A **jammed** stabiliser stays at one setting. `081.05.08.03.05` The consequences depend on where it jams. During **take-off**, jammed out of the green band, rotation forces are abnormal. On **landing**, jammed in a cruise setting, very large elevator forces are needed as the aeroplane slows and flaps are extended; the procedure usually calls for a higher approach speed, reduced flap and care in the flare. During a **go-around**, the thrust increase and configuration change produce large pitch changes the elevator alone may struggle to hold. `081.05.08.03.06`
