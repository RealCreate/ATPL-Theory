## Chapter 8 · Stability

An aeroplane is stable if, after a gust or a brief control input disturbs it, it tends to return to where it was without the pilot's help. That tendency is built into the design: the tailplane, the CG position, the fin, dihedral and sweep. This chapter separates the tendency to return (static stability) from the motion that follows (dynamic stability), and works through the three axes: pitch, yaw and roll, and how yaw and roll interact.

### 8.1 Static and dynamic stability

Imagine a ball. In a bowl it rolls back to the bottom when pushed: that is **positive static stability**. On a flat table it stays where it is put: **neutral static stability**. On top of an upturned bowl it rolls away: **negative static stability** (static instability). For an aeroplane, **static stability** is its **initial tendency**, immediately after a disturbance, to return to its original state of equilibrium. Aeroplanes are designed to be statically stable so that they hold their trimmed condition without constant correction and recover from gusts by themselves. `081.04.01.01.01`

**Manoeuvrability** is the ease with which the aeroplane can be made to change its state, for example its attitude, flight path or load factor, and how quickly it responds to the controls. `081.04.01.01.02`

The two pull in opposite directions. A very stable aeroplane resists being disturbed, including by its pilot: it needs large control forces and deflections to manoeuvre. A less stable aeroplane is more manoeuvrable but more demanding to fly steadily. Airliners are designed with moderate stability; aerobatic and fighter aircraft with little or none (fighters use computers to make up for it). `081.04.01.01.03`

**Dynamic stability** describes the **motion over time** after the disturbance. If the motion dies away and the aeroplane settles back, it is **dynamically stable** (positive); if it continues at the same amplitude, **neutral**; if it grows, **unstable** (negative). The motion can be **periodic** (oscillating, swinging back and forth) or **aperiodic** (moving steadily one way, without oscillating). An aeroplane can be statically stable but dynamically unstable: it tends to return, but overshoots by more each time. Static stability is necessary for dynamic stability, but does not guarantee it. `081.04.01.01.04`

### 8.2 Equilibrium

Stability can only be judged starting from **equilibrium**: the condition in which the sum of all forces and the sum of all moments on the aeroplane are zero, so it flies steadily without accelerating or rotating. `081.04.01.02.01`

The forces in equilibrium are **lift, weight, thrust and drag**, plus any side force: in level flight lift balances weight and thrust balances drag. `081.04.01.03.01`

The moments are about the three axes through the CG: **pitching** moments about the lateral axis, **rolling** moments about the longitudinal axis and **yawing** moments about the normal axis. `081.04.01.04.01` If the sum of the moments about an axis is not zero, the aeroplane starts to **rotate** about that axis, with an angular acceleration; trim, a stabiliser or a control deflection is then needed to restore the balance. `081.04.01.04.02`

### 8.3 Balancing in pitch

The wing's lift acts at its centre of pressure (or, with the camber moment, at its aerodynamic centre), the weight at the CG. They are not at the same point, and the cambered wing has its own nose-down moment. The **tailplane** (stabiliser) provides the force that makes the sum of the pitching moments about the CG **zero**. `081.04.03.01.01`

On a conventional aeroplane the CG is usually **ahead of** the wing's centre of pressure, so the wing's lift and camber moment pitch the nose down and the tailplane must push **down** (a negative tail load). The further the CP lies behind the CG, the larger that down-load. If the CG were behind the CP, the tail would need to lift upwards. `081.04.03.01.02`

The **IAS** changes the balancing force. At low speed the wing works at a high CL, its centre of pressure is further forward and the tail needs less down-load; at high speed the CP moves aft and the camber moment, which grows with dynamic pressure, needs more. The balancing force is varied by changing the tail's angle of attack, through elevator deflection or by changing the **stabiliser** incidence (trim). The pilot or the trim system sets the deflection that gives the force needed for each speed and CG, and the tail force can be down or up. `081.04.03.01.03–04`

**Thrust** also gives a pitching moment if its line of action does not pass through the CG. Engines **below** the CG, typically under-wing engines, pitch the nose **up** when thrust increases, so the elevator (or trim) must move nose-down; engines **above** the CG, such as tail-mounted engines high on the fuselage, pitch the nose **down** with more thrust and need nose-up elevator. `081.04.03.01.05`

### 8.4 Static longitudinal stability and the CG

If a gust raises the nose, the angle of attack of both wing and tailplane increases. The wing's extra lift acts at its aerodynamic centre; the tail's extra lift acts far behind the CG with a long arm and pitches the nose back down. If the stabilising tail moment is larger than the destabilising wing moment, the aeroplane is statically stable in pitch.

That balance depends on the **CG position**. A **forward CG** gives a short wing arm and a long tail arm: **more stability**, but **less manoeuvrability** in pitch, with heavy forces and large elevator deflections needed. An **aft CG** gives **less stability** and more manoeuvrability, with light forces, until stability disappears. `081.04.03.02.01`

### 8.5 The neutral point and static margin

The **neutral point** (NP) is the CG position at which the aeroplane would have **neutral** static longitudinal stability: the aerodynamic centre of the whole aeroplane, the point about which the total pitching moment does not change with angle of attack. `081.04.03.03.01`

Its position depends only on the **aerodynamic design**: the wing's aerodynamic centre, the tail's size and arm, its lift-curve slope and the downwash, and the fuselage. Moving the load does not change it; moving the CG changes stability by changing the CG's distance from it. `081.04.03.03.02`

The neutral point lies **between** the aerodynamic centre of the wing and that of the tailplane, normally a little behind the wing's AC. A larger tail or a longer tail arm moves it further aft. `081.04.03.04.01`

So the aeroplane is **stable** as long as the CG is **ahead of** the neutral point; the further ahead, the more stable. With the CG at the neutral point it is neutrally stable, and behind it unstable. `081.04.03.05.01`

The CG limits are set accordingly. `081.04.03.05.02`

- The **forward limit** is set by **control**: with the CG too far forward, the elevator may not have the power to rotate for take-off or to flare for landing, especially with flaps out and close to the ground, and control forces become very heavy. Elevator effectiveness, not stability, sets this limit. Stall speed and drag also rise.
- The **aft limit** is set by **stability**: the CG must stay sufficiently ahead of the neutral point to keep positive stability and a minimum stick force per g, so that control forces do not become dangerously light.

The **static margin** is the distance between the CG and the neutral point, usually expressed as a percentage of the MAC. The larger it is, the more stable the aeroplane. `081.04.03.05.03`

> **Exam trap.** Forward CG limit: elevator authority (rotation and flare). Aft CG limit: stability. The neutral point does not move with loading; the CG does.

### 8.6 The Cm–α graph

The **pitching-moment coefficient** Cm (nose-up positive) plotted against angle of attack shows static stability directly. Where the line crosses Cm = 0 the aeroplane is trimmed. If the slope is **negative**, an increase in angle of attack gives a nose-down moment that restores it: **stable**. A **steeper** negative slope means more stability; a zero slope is neutral; a positive slope unstable. `081.04.03.06.01`

The graph changes with: `081.04.03.07.01`

- **CG shift**: moving the CG **aft** makes the slope **less negative** (less stable), and at the neutral point the line is flat; moving it **forward** makes it steeper.
- **Elevator**: moving the elevator **up** adds a nose-up moment and moves the whole line **upwards** without changing its slope, so it trims at a **higher** angle of attack (lower speed); elevator down moves it downwards.
- **Trim**: moving the trim does the same as the elevator: it shifts the line up or down, setting the trimmed angle of attack, with no change of slope.
- **Wing contribution**: with the CG ahead of the wing's AC, the wing is stabilising; behind it, destabilising. Its camber adds a constant nose-down moment, which moves the line down.
- **Tail contribution**: strongly stabilising, a steep negative slope; it is what makes a conventional aeroplane stable.

### 8.7 Stick force stability

The pilot feels longitudinal stability through the control forces. In a stable aeroplane trimmed at a speed, flying **slower** needs a steady **pull** and flying **faster** a steady **push**, and releasing the column returns the aeroplane towards its trim speed. That is positive stick-force stability with **speed**. `081.04.03.10.01`

The gradient of stick force with speed is **steeper** with a forward CG and shallower with an aft CG; at the same IAS it is broadly similar at different **altitudes**, because forces depend on dynamic pressure, though aeroelastic and Mach effects change it at high altitude. Changing the **mass distribution** so that the CG moves aft makes the forces lighter and the aeroplane feel less stable.

### 8.8 Stick force per g

The **stick force per g** is the pull needed to increase the load factor by 1 g, in a pull-up or a turn. In a stable aeroplane the stick force **increases linearly with load factor**. `081.04.03.12.01`

It has a **prescribed minimum and maximum**. Too small a value lets the pilot overstress the aeroplane with a light pull; too large a value makes manoeuvring tiring and the aeroplane unresponsive. The stick force per g **decreases with pressure altitude**, because the aeroplane's natural pitch damping, which depends on air density, falls. `081.04.03.12.02`

An **aft CG** reduces the stick force per g; at the **manoeuvre point** (slightly aft of the neutral point) it becomes zero. **Trim** does not change the stick force per g: the trimmed condition sets where the force is zero, but the gradient with g depends on the CG and the design. `081.04.03.14.01`

### 8.9 Dynamic longitudinal stability

A disturbed aeroplane in pitch moves in two ways at once. `081.04.03.16.01`

- The **phugoid** (long-period oscillation): a slow exchange of speed and height at almost **constant angle of attack**. The nose drops, the aeroplane speeds up and descends, the extra lift makes it climb, it slows down, and the cycle repeats, with a period of around a minute or more. It is usually **lightly damped**, but so slow that the pilot corrects it easily.
- The **short-period oscillation**: a rapid pitching, with large changes of **angle of attack** and load factor at nearly **constant speed** and height, with a period of a second or two. It is normally **heavily damped** and dies out within one or two cycles.

The short period is the more **hazardous** one, because it is fast. Its period is close to the pilot's reaction time, so control inputs can come too late and reinforce it instead of damping it, and its large changes of angle of attack and load factor can overstress the structure or stall the wing. `081.04.03.16.02`

That reinforcement is a **pilot-induced oscillation** (PIO): oscillations in pitch (or roll) sustained or amplified by the pilot's own inputs, because each correction arrives too late and adds to the motion. The cure is to relax on the controls, or freeze them, and let the aeroplane's damping stop the motion. `081.04.03.16.03`

At **high altitude**, the air's lower density reduces the aerodynamic damping that resists pitching (and rolling and yawing), so all the oscillations are **less damped**: dynamic stability is reduced. `081.04.03.16.04`

Moving the CG **aft** reduces static stability and therefore the damping of the short-period motion, and makes the phugoid more lightly damped or unstable; at or behind the neutral point the motion diverges. A forward CG makes both better damped. `081.04.03.16.05`

### 8.10 Directional stability

**Static directional stability** is the tendency of the aeroplane, after a disturbance in yaw, to return to flying with its nose into the relative airflow: it is weathercock stability. `081.04.04.01.01`

Too **weak**, and the aeroplane wanders in yaw, is hard to keep straight and slips in turbulence; it may also be prone to Dutch roll. Too **strong**, combined with weak lateral stability, and it tends to the spiral dive (Section 8.13); it also makes crosswind landings harder, since the aeroplane wants to turn into wind. `081.04.04.01.02`

The **sideslip angle** is the angle between the longitudinal axis and the relative airflow, seen from above: the aeroplane is flying partly sideways. `081.04.04.02.01` Its symbol is **β** (beta). `081.04.04.02.02`

The **yawing-moment coefficient Cn** is the yawing moment about the normal axis, divided by the dynamic pressure, wing area and span. `081.04.04.03.01` For a directionally stable aeroplane, a sideslip produces a yawing moment that turns the nose **into** the relative wind, reducing the sideslip: Cn grows with β in the restoring sense. `081.04.04.03.02`

On a **Cn–β graph**, Cn depends on β because a sideslip presents the fin and fuselage at an angle to the flow. Cn is zero at the β where the aeroplane is in equilibrium about the normal axis, and with no asymmetric thrust, control deflection or loading that β is zero. `081.04.04.04.01` The **slope** of the line measures static directional stability: the steeper the restoring slope, the more stable. `081.04.04.04.02` The slope is reduced at high altitude in the same IAS sense: at high Mach number the fin's lift-curve slope falls, and lower density reduces damping, so directional stability is less. `081.04.04.04.03`

Many parts of the aeroplane contribute. `081.04.04.05.01`

- **Wing**: a small contribution; **sweepback** is stabilising, because in a sideslip the forward wing has more drag and pulls the nose back.
- **Fin**: the main stabilising surface. In a sideslip it meets the air at an angle and produces a side force well behind the CG.
- **Dorsal fin**: a fillet ahead of the fin that delays fin stall at large sideslip angles and adds side area aft.
- **Ventral fin**: a fin under the rear fuselage that adds side area behind the CG, especially useful at high angles of attack when the main fin is blanketed.
- **Sweep of the fin**: increases its arm behind the CG and its stalling sideslip angle.
- **Fuselage**: the forward fuselage is destabilising; at high angles of attack, the forebody vortices can strongly change its contribution.
- **Strakes**: on the forward fuselage or tail cone, they control the forebody vortices or add side area aft.

Moving the **CG aft** shortens the fin's arm and so **reduces** the directional stability (and the rudder's power). `081.04.04.05.02`

### 8.11 Lateral stability

**Static lateral stability** is the tendency of the aeroplane, after a disturbance in roll, to return to wings level. `081.04.05.01.01` Strictly the aeroplane responds to the **sideslip** a bank causes, not to the bank itself.

Too **weak**, and the aeroplane drifts into a bank and turn after every gust. Too **strong**, and it rolls strongly in response to every sideslip, which makes crosswind handling harder, reduces roll response and promotes **Dutch roll**. `081.04.05.01.02`

The **bank angle** φ (phi) is the angle between the lateral axis and the horizontal, measured about the longitudinal axis. `081.04.05.02.01`

The **rolling-moment coefficient Cl** is the rolling moment about the longitudinal axis divided by the dynamic pressure, wing area and span. `081.04.05.03.01`

Without coordination, a **bank** tilts the lift: its horizontal component pulls the aeroplane sideways towards the lower wing, so it **slips** sideways and a sideslip angle β appears, with the relative wind coming from the low side. `081.04.05.04.01`

On the **Cl–β graph**, a laterally stable aeroplane produces, in a sideslip, a rolling moment that raises the lower (leading) wing. The curve passes through zero at zero sideslip, and its **slope** measures lateral stability: the steeper, the more stable. `081.04.05.05.01–02` As with directional stability, the effective slope falls at high altitude, because damping is lower and the aeroplane responds differently. `081.04.05.05.03`

The contributions are: `081.04.05.06.01`

- **Dihedral**: in a sideslip, the forward (lower) wing meets the air at a higher angle of attack, gains lift and rolls the aeroplane level. **Anhedral** does the opposite and is destabilising.
- **High wing**: in a sideslip, the fuselage blocks the crossflow, increasing the angle of attack of the forward wing's root, which is stabilising; together with a low CG, a high wing gives strong lateral stability (often balanced with anhedral). A **low wing** is destabilising and needs more dihedral.
- **Sweepback**: in a sideslip the forward wing is effectively less swept, so it gives more lift, rolling the aeroplane level. Sweepback is strongly stabilising, more so at high CL; it often needs anhedral to stop it being too strong.
- **Ventral fin**: lies below the roll axis, so its side force in a sideslip rolls the aeroplane the wrong way: destabilising.
- **Vertical tail** (fin): lies above the roll axis, so its side force in a sideslip rolls the aeroplane level: stabilising.

### 8.12 Roll and yaw coupled

Lateral and directional stability are **coupled**. Any bank produces sideslip, and any sideslip produces both yaw (directional stability turns the nose into it) and roll (lateral stability rolls away from it). A yaw produces a speed difference between the wings and so roll; a roll produces sideslip and yaw. The two motions never happen alone. `081.04.06.02.01`

### 8.13 Spiral divergence

If **directional stability is strong** and **lateral stability weak**, a small bank leads to a sideslip; the strong fin yaws the nose into it (into the turn) faster than the weak lateral stability can roll the wings level. The yaw speeds up the outer wing, which gains lift and steepens the bank. The bank and turn tighten, the nose drops, the speed increases and the aeroplane descends in a tightening spiral: **spiral divergence** (an unstable spiral mode). With the two in better balance the spiral is **neutral** (the bank stays constant) or **stable** (the wings slowly return level). `081.04.06.02.02`

In an unstable **spiral dive** the **speed increases**, the **bank angle increases**, the **nose drops** further below the horizon and the aeroplane **loses height** at an increasing rate. It develops slowly, so it is dangerous mainly when the pilot is distracted or in cloud. Recovery: reduce power, roll the wings level first, then ease out of the dive. `081.04.06.02.03`

### 8.14 Dutch roll

**Dutch roll** is a combined, oscillating yaw and roll, with the wing tips tracing ellipses as seen from behind: the aeroplane yaws one way and rolls the other way, repeatedly, with a period of a few seconds. `081.04.06.03.01`

It happens when **lateral stability is strong compared with directional stability**. A yaw to the right makes the left wing go faster and gain lift, and the sideslip from the left rolls the aeroplane right strongly through the lateral stability. The sideslip then reverses, the fin yaws the aeroplane back, it overshoots, and the strong rolling reaction repeats. Swept wings, with strong lateral stability at high CL and weak fin damping at altitude, are especially prone. More directional stability and damping makes the oscillation **stable** (dying out); with less it becomes **neutral** or **unstable** (growing). `081.04.06.03.02`

Airliners have a **yaw damper**: a system that senses the yaw rate and moves the rudder to oppose it automatically, damping the Dutch roll before it builds up. If the **yaw damper is unavailable**, the flight manual normally requires flying at a lower altitude and speed where natural damping is better. If a Dutch roll develops, the usual action is to damp it with small, well-timed **aileron** inputs against the roll (the rudder is generally not used, as mistimed inputs make it worse), or follow the type's procedure. `081.04.06.03.02`

At **high Mach numbers**, a sideslip changes the effective sweep of the two wings: the shock wave is stronger on one wing than the other, changing the lift and drag on each side. That asymmetry adds a rolling and yawing moment that can start or worsen a Dutch roll. `081.04.06.03.03`

### 8.15 Altitude and dynamic stability

Higher **pressure altitude** means lower air density and therefore less aerodynamic **damping**, while the aeroplane's inertia stays the same. The dynamic lateral and directional modes, especially Dutch roll, are therefore less damped at high altitude. `081.04.06.04.01`
