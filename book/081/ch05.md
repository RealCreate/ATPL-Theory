## Chapter 5 · High-Lift Devices, Spoilers and Contamination

A wing designed for efficient cruise has a modest CLMAX, which would make take-off and landing speeds unacceptably high. Flaps and slats raise CLMAX for the slow phases of flight; spoilers and speed brakes do the opposite, destroying lift or adding drag when the pilot needs to slow down or descend. This chapter also covers what ice, dirt and age do to a wing.

### 5.1 Trailing-edge flaps

There are four basic types of trailing-edge flap. `081.01.09.01.01`

- **Plain flap**: the rear of the wing hinges down. Simple, but it gives the smallest CLMAX increase and much drag at large deflections.
- **Split flap**: only a plate on the lower surface hinges down, leaving the upper surface unchanged. A little more lift than a plain flap, and a lot of drag.
- **Slotted flap**: the flap moves down and leaves a gap (slot) between it and the wing. High-energy air from below flows through the slot over the flap's upper surface, re-energising the boundary layer so the flow stays attached at larger deflections. Double and triple slotted flaps repeat the idea.
- **Fowler flap**: the flap first slides back, increasing the wing area and chord, and then rotates down, usually with one or more slots. It gives the largest increase in CLMAX, with relatively little drag in the first stages, which suits take-off.

Lowering a flap increases the **effective camber** of the wing (and, for a Fowler flap, its area). More camber gives more lift at every angle of attack and a higher CLMAX, so the aeroplane can fly slower; it also gives **more drag**, which helps steepen the approach and slow the aeroplane down. Small flap settings give much lift for little drag (take-off); large settings give much drag for little extra lift (landing). `081.01.09.01.02`

Flaps move the **centre of pressure aft**, because the extra lift is generated over the rear of the wing. On their own that produces a **nose-down pitching moment**; on a real aeroplane the change of downwash on the tailplane can reduce or reverse it, so the actual trim change depends on the type. Flaps reduce the **stall speed**, because CLMAX is higher. `081.01.09.01.03`

On the **lift curve**, trailing-edge flaps move the curve **up and to the left**: at any angle of attack CL is higher; **CLMAX is higher**; **αCRIT is a little lower**; and a given CL is reached at a **lower angle of attack**. `081.01.09.01.04`

On the **polar**, flaps move the curve to higher CL and higher CD; the maximum lift-to-drag ratio is **lower** than for the clean wing. `081.01.09.01.05` Because the glide angle is set by the L/D ratio, flaps make the **glide steeper**: useful on approach to come down steeply without gaining speed. `081.01.09.01.06`

**Flap asymmetry**, one flap extended and the other not, or extended further, creates a strong **rolling moment** towards the side with less flap, plus some yaw. It may exceed the aileron's ability to correct, especially at low speed. Systems therefore compare left and right flap positions and stop the flaps if they disagree. `081.01.09.01.07`

For take-off and landing, flaps give a **lower stall speed**, so lower take-off and landing speeds and shorter distances. Because they reach the needed CL at a lower angle of attack, the **nose attitude is lower**, which improves the pilot's view on approach and reduces the risk of a tail strike. `081.01.09.01.08`

**Flap-setting errors** are dangerous. `081.01.09.01.09`

- Taking off with **less flap** than planned raises the real stall and rotation speeds above those used for planning: the take-off run is longer, rotation at the planned speed may not give lift-off, and the stall margin at V2 is reduced.
- Taking off with **more flap** than planned shortens the run but reduces climb performance, which may not meet the obstacle gradient.
- **Retracting flaps too early** or at too low a speed reduces CLMAX while the aeroplane is still slow: it may sink or meet the stall buffet. Retract on schedule at the minimum flap-retraction speeds.
- **Extending flaps late** on approach leaves the aeroplane fast and high; **extending too early**, above VFE, risks structural damage.

### 5.2 Leading-edge devices

Leading-edge devices raise CLMAX mainly by letting the wing reach a **higher angle of attack** before the flow separates. `081.01.09.02.01`

- **Slats** are small aerofoils on the leading edge that move forward and down, opening a **slot** between them and the wing. They can be fixed, automatic (pulled out by the low pressure at high angle of attack) or powered.
- **Krueger flaps** hinge down and forward from the lower surface of the leading edge, increasing camber and nose radius; they are often used on the inboard wing of swept-wing airliners.
- **Variable-camber flaps** are leading-edge sections that flex or curve down, increasing the camber of the front of the wing.

The **slot** works by feeding high-energy air from the high-pressure area under the wing through a narrow, accelerating gap onto the upper surface. This energises the boundary layer and smooths the pressure distribution behind the leading edge, so the flow stays attached to a much higher angle of attack. `081.01.09.02.02`

Leading-edge flaps (Krueger and variable camber) increase the **effective camber** at the front of the wing and its nose radius, which lets the air turn round the leading edge at high angles of attack without separating. `081.01.09.02.03`

Leading-edge devices **lower the stall speed**, like trailing-edge flaps, but they do it by raising αCRIT rather than by raising the lift at a given angle of attack. Used together, as on almost every airliner, the two give the largest reduction in stall speed. `081.01.09.02.04`

On the **lift curve**, slats **extend** the curve: the straight part continues to a **higher CLMAX** at a **higher αCRIT**; at normal angles of attack the curve is almost unchanged (slightly lower for a slat). Trailing-edge flaps, by contrast, shift the whole curve up and lower αCRIT. `081.01.09.02.05`

On the **polar**, leading-edge devices add a little drag at low CL but extend the curve to higher CL; at high CL they can give less drag than the clean wing near its stall. `081.01.09.02.06`

**Slat asymmetry** means one wing stalls at a lower angle of attack than the other. Near the stall the wing without slats stalls first and drops, creating a sharp **roll** and possibly a spin entry. `081.01.09.02.07`

For take-off and landing, slats reduce speeds and distances because CLMAX is higher. Their disadvantage is that the high CL is reached at a **high angle of attack**, so the **nose attitude is high**: the pilot's view is worse and the tail is closer to the runway. That is why slats are combined with trailing-edge flaps. `081.01.09.02.08`

> **Exam trap.** Trailing-edge flaps: CLMAX up, αCRIT down, nose lower. Slats: CLMAX up, αCRIT up, nose higher.

### 5.3 Vortex generators

**Vortex generators** are rows of small vanes standing up from the surface, set at an angle to the flow. `081.01.09.03.01` Each one makes a small, strong vortex that mixes fast-moving air from outside the boundary layer down into it. The boundary layer gains energy and can then survive a stronger adverse pressure gradient before it separates. `081.01.09.03.02`

They delay separation at high angles of attack, keep control surfaces effective, and, on high-speed wings, reduce shock-induced separation. Their cost is a small increase in parasite drag in cruise, and some extra weight and maintenance. `081.01.09.03.03`

### 5.4 Spoilers

**Spoilers** are panels on the upper surface of the wing, ahead of the flaps, that hinge up into the airflow. They destroy part of the lift over the wing behind them and add drag. `081.01.10.01.01`

- **Roll spoilers** rise on one wing only, in step with the aileron on that side, to help roll the aeroplane (Chapter 9).
- **Flight spoilers** (speed brakes) rise symmetrically in flight to increase drag and allow a faster descent or deceleration.
- **Ground spoilers** (lift dumpers) rise fully on both wings after touchdown or in a rejected take-off. They dump the lift, putting the weight on the wheels so the brakes work, and add drag.

Spoilers move the **lift curve down**: at any angle of attack CL is lower, and **CLMAX is reduced**, so the **stall speed rises**. Using speed brakes close to the stall is therefore dangerous. `081.01.10.01.02` On the **polar**, spoilers reduce the lift and increase the drag, so the **lift-drag ratio falls**. `081.01.10.01.03`

### 5.5 Speed brakes

**Speed brakes** are surfaces that add drag when needed: spoiler panels on the wing, or panels on the fuselage or tail cone. They are used to descend steeply without gaining speed, to slow down quickly (for example to meet an ATC speed restriction), and to manage energy on approach. `081.01.10.02.01`

They **increase parasite drag** by adding form drag and causing separated flow behind them. `081.01.10.02.03` On the polar they move the curve to the right (more CD at every CL), so (L/D)max is lower. `081.01.10.02.02` Because parasite drag rises while induced drag is unchanged, the speed at which the two are equal, **VMD, decreases**. `081.01.10.02.04`

With the extra drag, the aeroplane can **descend at a steeper angle and a higher rate** at the same speed, or descend at the same rate at a lower speed. `081.01.10.02.05`

### 5.6 Ice and other contamination

In flight, ice builds up on parts that meet the airflow head-on and have small radii: the **leading edges** of the wings, tailplane and fin; **engine intakes** and propellers; **probes, antennas** and wipers; and on the windscreen. Thin, sharp surfaces such as the tailplane collect ice faster than the thick wing roots. `081.01.12.01.01`

Even a thin layer of ice, frost or snow, with the roughness of coarse sandpaper, can change the aerodynamics drastically. `081.01.12.01.02`

- **Lift**: CLMAX falls, by up to about 30 % for frost or rough ice on the leading edge, more for large ridges.
- **Drag**: increases, through roughness and the disturbed shape.
- **Stall speed**: rises with the lower CLMAX, often by 15–20 % or more.
- **αCRIT**: falls; the wing may stall **before** the stall warning, which is set for a clean wing, activates.
- **Stability and control**: the tailplane may stall (Chapter 7); ailerons may become heavy or snatch; the aeroplane may become unstable.

At **take-off** these effects are worst, because the aeroplane is at high angle of attack close to the ground with no height to recover. Rotating at the normal speed with a contaminated wing can mean a stall, a wing drop or an inability to climb out of ground effect. That is why the **clean wing concept** applies: no take-off with frost, ice or snow adhering to critical surfaces. `081.01.12.01.03`

### 5.7 Ageing aeroplanes

Over its life an airframe changes. Dents, distortion, poorly fitting panels, repairs with doublers and protruding fasteners, worn seals, mis-rigged control surfaces and extra antennas all add **parasite drag** and can produce small asymmetries that need constant trim. The result is higher fuel burn and lower performance, which operators allow for with performance (degradation) factors. `081.01.12.02.01`

Roughness and steps on an older surface also make the **boundary layer turn turbulent earlier** and thicken, adding friction drag and making separation more likely, especially on laminar-flow surfaces. `081.01.12.02.02`
