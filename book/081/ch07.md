## Chapter 7 · The Stall, Buffet and Upset Prevention

A wing stalls when its angle of attack exceeds the critical angle, whatever the speed, attitude or altitude. This chapter starts with the boundary layer, where separation begins, and then covers stall speeds and load factor, how different wings stall, stall warning, the special cases (deep stall, tailplane stall, contamination, spins), the buffet boundaries of high-altitude flight, and the situations in which airline crews meet stalls in practice.

### 7.1 The boundary layer

The **boundary layer** is the thin layer of air next to the surface in which the speed rises from zero at the surface (the air sticks to it) to the speed of the flow outside. It is where friction acts. `081.03.01.01.01`

It starts thin and **laminar** at the leading edge: the air moves in smooth layers sliding over one another. Further back it becomes **turbulent**: the layers mix in random eddies. A laminar boundary layer is very thin, typically a millimetre or so; a turbulent one is several times thicker, growing to a few centimetres on a large wing. `081.03.01.01.02`

- A **laminar** boundary layer has **low skin friction**, which is its advantage. But its air near the surface moves slowly and has little energy, so it **separates easily** in an adverse pressure gradient. `081.03.01.01.03`
- A **turbulent** boundary layer has **higher skin friction**, but its mixing brings fast air down close to the surface. It has more **energy** near the wall and **resists separation** much better. `081.03.01.01.04`

The **transition point** is where the boundary layer changes from laminar to turbulent. It moves forward with higher speed, roughness, and adverse pressure gradients. `081.03.01.01.05`

The laminar layer separates more easily because its slow-moving lower layers have too little momentum to push on against rising pressure; in the turbulent layer, the constant mixing keeps feeding momentum from the outer flow down to the surface. `081.03.01.01.06`

### 7.2 Separation and the critical angle

Behind the suction peak, the air over the upper surface has to flow from low pressure back up to the higher pressure at the trailing edge: an **adverse pressure gradient**. As the angle of attack increases, the suction peak becomes stronger, so the pressure rise the air must climb over the rear of the wing becomes steeper, and the air there is slowed down more. `081.03.01.01.07`

Eventually the boundary layer near the surface stops and reverses: it **separates**. The **separation point** is where this happens. At small angles of attack it lies at or near the trailing edge; as the angle of attack increases, it moves **forward**, and behind it is a region of turbulent, reversed flow. `081.03.01.01.08`

The **critical angle of attack** (αCRIT) is the angle of attack at which CL reaches its maximum. Beyond it, separation spreads forward so fast that lift falls: the wing has stalled. `081.03.01.01.09`

Approaching the stall in straight flight, as the angle of attack increases: `081.03.01.01.10`

- the **stagnation point** moves down and back under the leading edge;
- the **pressure distribution** develops a sharp suction peak near the leading edge, then collapses as separation spreads;
- the **centre of pressure** of a straight wing moves forward up to the stall, then moves back sharply; on a **swept wing**, where the tips stall first, it moves **forward** at the stall;
- **CL** rises to CLMAX and then falls;
- **CD** rises steeply, and total **drag** rises sharply;
- the **pitching moment** becomes nose-down at the stall on a straight wing, but nose-up on a swept wing (pitch-up);
- **buffet** begins as the separated wake shakes the wing and tail;
- at high Mach number a clean wing may meet a **deterrent buffet**, so strong that it acts as the limit;
- the pilot finds a **lack of pitch authority**, the aeroplane may **pitch down** uncommanded, and a wing may drop in an **uncommanded roll**.

The natural **pre-stall buffet** is caused by the turbulent, separated wake hitting the rear of the wing and the tailplane, which shakes the structure and the controls. `081.03.01.01.11`

Near the stall the controls become less effective. The ailerons work in disturbed, low-energy air and feel soft, the elevator may lose authority, and in some aeroplanes the controls feel light. `081.03.01.01.12`

### 7.3 Behaviour at and after the stall

A **straight-wing** aeroplane with a well-designed wing stalls at the root first. The lift lost near the root, ahead of the tail, and the separated wake reducing downwash at the tail, give a **nose-down pitch**. If the stall is symmetrical, the nose drops and the aeroplane recovers itself as the angle of attack falls. `081.03.01.01.13`

Using the controls carelessly close to the stall is dangerous. Pulling harder increases the angle of attack further. Using the **ailerons** to lift a dropping wing increases that wing's angle of attack (the down aileron increases its camber), which can stall it completely and drive the aeroplane into a spin. Near the stall, keep the wings level with the **rudder** where the type allows, and above all reduce the angle of attack. `081.03.01.01.14`

The **deterrent buffet** is buffeting strong enough to discourage the pilot unmistakably from going further. `081.03.01.01.15` In some aeroplanes, especially at high Mach number, it appears before CLMAX is reached and makes further increase in angle of attack unacceptable, so it is treated as the stall limit and used to define the stall speed. `081.03.01.01.16`

### 7.4 Stall speeds

Several stall speeds are defined. `081.03.01.02.01`

- **VS0**: the stall speed or minimum steady flight speed in the **landing configuration**.
- **VS1**: the stall speed or minimum steady flight speed in a **specified configuration**.
- **VS1G**: the **one-g stall speed**, the minimum speed at which the aeroplane can produce lift equal to its weight, at CLMAX in 1-g flight.
- **VSR**: the **reference stall speed** used in CS-25, a calibrated airspeed defined by the manufacturer that may not be less than VS1G. Take-off and landing speeds are set as multiples of it.

From the lift formula, at CLMAX in 1-g flight: **VS1G = √(2W / (ρ S CLMAX))**. A higher CL gives a lower stall speed, and stall speed is inversely proportional to the square root of CLMAX. `081.03.01.02.02`

> **Worked example · stall speed from CL.** An aeroplane stalls at 120 kt clean with CLMAX 1.5. With flaps CLMAX is 2.4. VS = 120 × √(1.5/2.4) = 120 × 0.79 ≈ **95 kt**.

Many factors change the stall speed. `081.03.01.02.03`

- **CG**: a **forward** CG needs a larger down-load on the tail, so the wing must lift more than the weight and the stall speed is **higher**; an aft CG lowers it.
- **Thrust component**: in a nose-high attitude part of the thrust acts upwards and supports some of the weight, so the stall speed **with power** is lower.
- **Slipstream**: on a propeller aeroplane, the slipstream over the inner wing energises the flow and increases its lift, also lowering the stall speed with power.
- **Wing loading** and **mass**: stall speed rises with the square root of W/S.
- **Contamination**: frost, ice, snow, dirt or rain reduce CLMAX and raise the stall speed.
- **Sweep**: reduces CLMAX, so a swept wing has a higher stall speed than an equivalent straight wing.
- **Altitude**: at low altitude the stall IAS does not change; at high altitude compressibility lowers CLMAX and the stall IAS rises (Chapter 6).

### 7.5 Load factor and accelerated stalls

The **load factor** n is the ratio of lift to weight: **n = L / W**, given in g. In level flight n = 1. `081.03.01.02.04`

In a level **turn**, the lift is tilted by the bank angle φ. Its vertical part must still equal the weight, so the total lift must increase: **n = 1 / cos φ**. At 60° of bank, n = 2. `081.03.01.02.05`

In a **pull-up**, the aeroplane follows a curved path upwards; lift must support the weight and also provide the force towards the centre of the curve, so n > 1. In a **push-over**, the path curves downwards, gravity provides part of the curving force, and n < 1, down to zero or negative. `081.03.01.02.06`

At a given CLMAX the stall speed rises with the square root of the load factor: **VS(n) = VS1G × √n**. `081.03.01.02.07` A stall at a load factor above 1, in a turn or pull-up, is an **accelerated stall**: it happens at a speed higher than the 1-g stall speed, and can be more abrupt. (It is sometimes wrongly called a high-speed stall.) `081.03.01.02.08`

> **Key numbers · stall speed in a turn**
>
> | Bank | Load factor | Stall speed × |
> | --- | --- | --- |
> | 0° | 1.00 | 1.00 |
> | 30° | 1.15 | 1.07 |
> | 45° | 1.41 | 1.19 |
> | 60° | 2.00 | 1.41 |
> | 75° | 3.86 | 1.97 |

> **Worked example · load factor.** VS1G = 110 kt. At n = 2.5, VS = 110 × √2.5 ≈ **174 kt**. `081.03.01.02.09`

> **Worked example · bank angle.** VS1G = 100 kt. In a 45° level turn, VS = 100 × √(1/cos 45°) = 100 × √1.414 ≈ **119 kt**. `081.03.01.02.10`

Stall speed also changes with the square root of **mass**: VS2 = VS1 × √(m2 / m1). `081.03.01.02.11`

> **Worked example · mass.** VS = 120 kt at 60 t. At 48 t: 120 × √(48/60) = 120 × 0.894 ≈ **107 kt**.

### 7.6 Where the stall starts

The stall should start near the **root** and spread outwards. Then the ailerons keep working, the buffet from the root shakes the tail as a warning, and the nose drops. If it starts at the tips, the ailerons are lost, one tip usually stalls first and the aeroplane rolls, and on a swept wing it pitches up. `081.03.01.03.01`

- An **elliptical** wing has uniform lift and stalls along the whole span at once, with little warning and poor lateral control.
- A **rectangular** wing stalls first at the **root**, where the local lift coefficient is highest: the most benign pattern.
- A **moderately tapered** wing stalls first part-way out; a **highly tapered** wing stalls near the **tips**.
- A **swept** or **delta** wing tends to stall at the **tips** first, because spanwise flow thickens the boundary layer outboard, and this gives pitch-up.

![Where the stall starts on rectangular, tapered, elliptical and swept planforms](img/p-stallpattern.jpg)

**Washout** makes the tip sections meet the air at a lower angle of attack than the root (geometric twist), or gives them a section with a higher stalling angle (aerodynamic twist). Either way the root reaches its critical angle first and the ailerons stay effective into the stall. `081.03.01.03.02`

Swept wings need other devices to control spanwise flow and tip stall. `081.03.01.03.04`

- **Wing fences** are thin plates along the chord on the upper surface that block the outward flow of the boundary layer.
- **Vortilons** are small plates under the leading edge whose vortex, at high angle of attack, flows over the upper surface and acts as an aerodynamic fence.
- A **saw tooth** (dog-tooth) is a step in the leading edge that creates a vortex doing the same job.
- **Vortex generators** re-energise the boundary layer, delaying separation.
- **Nacelle strakes** on the engine nacelles of wing-mounted engines shed a vortex over the wing at high angle of attack, restoring flow that the nacelle and pylon would otherwise disturb.

### 7.7 Stall warning

The stall must be avoided, and the pilot must know when it is close. Natural warning alone is not always enough: some wings give little buffet, and at high altitude or with contamination the warning may come late. `081.03.01.04.01` **Aerodynamic** warning (natural buffet) is accepted when it is clear and comes early enough; otherwise **artificial** warning, a stall-warning horn, light or stick shaker, is required, and on most airliners it is fitted anyway. `081.03.01.04.02`

The certification rules require operating speeds to stay a margin above the stall: in CS-25 the reference landing speed must be at least **1.23 VSR0** and the take-off safety speed V2 at least **1.13 VSR**; in CS-23 the approach speed is at least **1.3 VS0** (VS1 in the landing configuration). The margin covers gusts, turbulence, manoeuvres and errors. Stall warning must begin at least **5 kt (or 5 %) before** the stall. `081.03.01.04.03`

The warning devices are: `081.03.01.04.04`

- **Buffet**: the natural shaking from separated flow.
- **Stall strip**: a small wedge on the leading edge of the inner wing that forces the flow to separate there first, giving root stall and early buffet.
- **Flapper switch** (leading-edge stall-warning vane): a small tab at the leading edge pushed up by the stagnation point as it moves down at high angle of attack, closing a switch for the horn.
- **Angle-of-attack vane**: a small weathervane on the fuselage side that aligns with the local flow and measures the angle of attack directly.
- **Angle-of-attack probe**: a cone or probe with pressure ports that senses the angle of attack from the pressure difference.
- **Stick shaker**: a motor that shakes the control column when the angle of attack approaches the stall, a warning the pilot cannot miss.

The recovery is the same in principle at every stage. `081.03.01.04.05`

- At the **stall warning**: reduce the angle of attack (lower the nose or relax the back pressure) until the warning stops, keep the wings level, and adjust thrust as needed.
- At the **stall**: push forward to **reduce the angle of attack** first, until the stall indications disappear, even if this costs height; level the wings; then increase thrust as needed (on aeroplanes with engines below the wing, thrust also pitches the nose up, so apply it carefully); finally recover to the desired flight path, avoiding a secondary stall.
- After a **stick-pusher** actuation: do not fight it. Let it lower the nose, then recover as from the stall.

### 7.8 Special stalls

**Power-on and power-off stalls** differ. With power on, the thrust component and slipstream lower the stall speed and the nose is higher; the slipstream and torque can make one wing stall first, so a wing drop and spin entry are more likely, and the stall can be more abrupt. Recovery: reduce the angle of attack first, then use power. With power off, the stall speed is higher, the nose lower and the stall usually gentler. `081.03.01.05.02`

In a **climbing turn** the outer wing follows a shallower spiral path and meets the air at a higher angle of attack, so it tends to stall first and the aeroplane rolls outwards. In a **descending turn** the inner wing has the higher angle of attack and tends to stall first, rolling the aeroplane inwards. In both cases recover by reducing the angle of attack first, then rolling the wings level. `081.03.01.05.03`

**Pitch-up** affects swept wings: the tips, which lie aft of the CG, stall first, so the remaining lift moves forward and the nose pitches **up**, increasing the angle of attack further. A **T-tail** aeroplane adds to it: at high angles of attack the tailplane can enter the wake of the stalled wing and the rear-mounted engines, losing effectiveness just when it is needed. `081.03.01.05.04`

That can lead to a **deep stall** (super stall): the aeroplane settles at a very high angle of attack in a stable stalled condition, with the tailplane immersed in the wake and the elevator unable to lower the nose. It may be impossible to recover. `081.03.01.05.05` Aeroplanes prone to it are fitted with a **stick pusher**, which pushes the control column forward forcefully when the angle of attack reaches a set value, so the aeroplane never reaches the deep-stall region. The philosophy is to prevent the stall rather than rely on recovering from it. `081.03.01.05.06`

The **stall warning can be absent** or late when the wing is contaminated (it stalls at a lower angle of attack than the warning system assumes), when an angle-of-attack sensor fails or freezes, when the system is unpowered or inhibited, or at high Mach number where the stall characteristics differ. The pilot may then meet a stall with no warning at all, so speed and attitude must be monitored and contamination never accepted. `081.03.01.05.07`

### 7.9 Ice and the tailplane stall

The tailplane normally produces a **down-load** at a **negative** angle of attack. It is thin and collects ice faster than the wing. Ice can then make it stall at a smaller negative angle of attack: a **negative tail stall**. `081.03.01.05.08`

Extending flaps increases the downwash behind the wing, making the tailplane's angle of attack **more negative**, so it is most likely just after **flap extension** on approach, and with high power or high speed. The tail stops producing its down-load and the nose pitches **down** suddenly. Signs include a light or buffeting control column, pitch oscillations, and difficulty trimming. The recovery is the opposite of a wing stall: **retract the flaps** to the previous setting, **pull back** on the column, and reduce power if the type's procedures say so. Retracting high-lift devices reduces the downwash and so the tail's negative angle of attack. `081.03.01.05.10`

**In-flight icing** is to be expected in visible moisture (cloud, rain, drizzle) at outside air temperatures from about 0 °C down to −20 °C, and in cumulonimbus down to −40 °C, and in freezing rain or drizzle below a warm layer. It is most severe near 0 to −10 °C with large drops. `081.03.01.05.09`

A contaminated wing has a **higher stall speed** and a **lower αCRIT** (Chapter 5). `081.03.01.05.12`

On the **ground in winter**, frost, snow and ice settle on parked aeroplanes; slush and spray from the runway are thrown onto the wings and gear during taxi. Taking off with any contamination on the wing risks a **stall at or just after rotation**, a wing drop, reduced climb performance and higher drag. That is why the aeroplane must be de-iced and protected with anti-icing fluid. `081.03.01.05.13`

The **holdover time** is the estimated time for which the anti-icing fluid will keep frost and ice from forming on the treated surfaces. It depends on the fluid type and concentration, the temperature and the type and intensity of precipitation. Once it expires, the fluid may be saturated and contamination may be building on the wings, so a pre-take-off contamination check or a new treatment is needed; taking off after it has expired risks all the effects of a contaminated wing. `081.03.01.05.14`

**Heavy tropical rain** puts a film of water on the wing that roughens the surface, thickens the boundary layer and adds mass. It increases drag and can reduce CLMAX by several per cent, raising the stall speed. Avoid the heaviest cells, add speed margin and be ready for extra thrust. `081.03.01.05.15`

### 7.10 Spins

A spin is an autorotation: one wing more deeply stalled than the other, with the aeroplane rolling, yawing and descending steeply. It needs a **stall** plus **yaw**. Avoid it by not stalling, and by keeping the aeroplane coordinated (ball centred) near the stall. `081.03.01.06.01`

Factors that start a spin are: stalling with **sideslip** or yaw (uncoordinated rudder or adverse yaw from aileron), using **aileron** to pick up a wing near the stall, **power** asymmetries and propeller effects, and **aft CG**. `081.03.01.06.02`

The **incipient spin** is the first phase, from the stall to about two turns: the motion is unsteady and still developing, and recovery is quickest. In a **developed spin** the rotation rate, airspeed and rate of descent are steady and the aerodynamic and inertial forces are balanced. To recover (unless the flight manual says otherwise): **power** to idle, **ailerons** neutral, full **rudder** opposite to the rotation, then **elevator** forward to unstall the wing; when the rotation stops, centralise the rudder, level the wings and recover from the dive. `081.03.01.06.03`

With a **forward CG** the spin is steeper and nose-down, and recovery is easier. With an **aft CG** it is **flatter**, faster rotating, and recovery may be slow or impossible because the elevator and rudder are less effective. `081.03.01.06.04`

### 7.11 Mach buffet and the buffet boundaries

At high Mach number the shock wave on the upper surface becomes strong enough to make the boundary layer behind it **separate**: **shock-induced separation**. The separated, unsteady flow shakes the wing and tail as **Mach buffet** (high-speed buffet). The same separation moves the centre of pressure and changes the downwash, giving **Mach tuck**. `081.03.02.01.01`

At high altitude an airliner is therefore boxed in between two buffet boundaries: **low-speed buffet**, where the angle of attack is too high, and **high-speed buffet**, where the Mach number is too high. The **buffet margin** is the distance to them, expressed as the extra load factor available before buffet begins. It is reduced by: `081.03.02.02.01`

- a higher **angle of attack** (lower speed);
- a higher **Mach number** (towards high-speed buffet);
- a higher **pressure altitude**, where a given Mach number gives a lower IAS and needs a higher CL;
- a higher **mass**;
- a higher **load factor**, for example in turbulence or a turn, and so a higher **bank angle**;
- a more **forward CG**, because the tail's down-load adds to the lift the wing must provide.

The **buffet onset boundary chart** in the flight manual gives, for a given mass, CG and altitude, the Mach numbers at which low- and high-speed buffet begin at a given load factor. The pilot uses it to find how much **manoeuvre** (load factor or bank) is available at the planned cruise level, and the **buffet margin**. `081.03.02.02.02`

![Buffet onset boundaries: low- and high-speed buffet against Mach number, the 1.3 g margin, the aerodynamic ceiling and the coffin corner](img/p-buffet.jpg)

Exceeding **MMO** brings the aeroplane into the region of shock-induced separation: first **light buffet**, then stronger **buffet onset**, together with Mach tuck and an overspeed warning. `081.03.02.02.03`

As altitude increases at a given mass, the low-speed buffet Mach number rises and the high-speed buffet Mach number falls. The altitude where they meet at 1 g is the **aerodynamic ceiling**: there the aeroplane can only fly at one Mach number, with no margin for manoeuvre or gust. The region near it, where the two speeds are very close, is the **coffin corner**. `081.03.02.02.04`

Operators require a margin against buffet in cruise, usually **1.3 g**: the cruise level must allow a load factor of 1.3 before buffet onset, equal to a level turn at about **40° of bank** or a strong gust, at the cruise Mach number. The **1.3 g buffet margin altitude** is the highest level at which that margin exists, for the current mass. `081.03.02.02.05`

On the chart, the pilot can find: `081.03.02.02.06`

- the **buffet-free range** of Mach numbers at a given mass, altitude and load factor;
- the **aerodynamic ceiling** at a given mass (where the 1-g range shrinks to a point);
- the **load factor and bank angle** at which buffet begins at a given mass, Mach number and altitude (bank = arccos(1/n)).

**Descending** increases the buffet-free range: lower down, the density is higher, so at a given Mach number the IAS is higher and the CL needed lower, which moves both buffet boundaries away. That is why the response to severe turbulence or a buffet warning at high altitude is often to descend. `081.03.02.02.07`

### 7.12 Situations that lead to a stall

Some stalls are **pilot-induced**. `081.03.03.01.01`

- **Inappropriate take-off configuration**: with the slats or leading-edge flaps not extended, αCRIT is much lower and a normal rotation can stall the wing just after lift-off. Mitigation: configuration checks, take-off configuration warnings, and correct speeds for the actual configuration.
- **Steep turns**: the load factor raises the stall speed with √n; mitigation: limit bank, keep speed up.
- **Go-around with TOGA thrust** on an aeroplane with **underslung engines**: the thrust gives a strong nose-up pitching moment. If the pitch is not controlled the nose rises, speed decays and the aeroplane can approach the stall. Mitigation: positive pitch control to the target attitude, trim, and follow the go-around procedure.

At **low altitude**, the weather can cause stalls. `081.03.03.01.02`

- **Thunderstorms, wind shear and microbursts**: a sudden loss of headwind or a strong downdraft drops the airspeed and raises the angle of attack. Avoid them; if caught, follow the wind-shear escape procedure (maximum thrust, pitch to the stick-shaker or the guidance, no configuration change).
- **Turbulence**: vertical gusts change the angle of attack; fly the turbulence speed, and keep an adequate margin.
- **Wake turbulence**: induced rolls and sinks at low speed; keep the separation minima and fly above the leader's path.
- **Icing**: higher stall speed, lower αCRIT, possible tail stall; use anti-ice, avoid icing, add speed margins.

At **high altitude**, the margins are small. `081.03.03.01.03`

- **Thunderstorms in the ITCZ**: violent updrafts and turbulence at high altitude, often embedded and hard to see, and ice crystals that can block pitot probes. Avoid them by a wide margin on radar.
- **Jet streams**: strong wind shear can change the airspeed suddenly by tens of knots, towards either buffet boundary.
- **Clear-air turbulence**: gusts with no visual warning. Mitigation for all three: fly at a level with a good buffet margin, use the turbulence penetration speed and Mach, and descend if needed.

Other situations: `081.03.03.01.04`

- An **inappropriate autopilot climb mode**, especially **vertical speed** mode at high altitude, holds the rate of climb regardless of speed; as the thrust available falls, the speed decays towards the stall. Mitigation: use a speed-on-pitch mode (level change, OPEN CLIMB) and monitor speed.
- **Loss of, or unreliable, airspeed** (blocked pitot or static ports, ice crystals) can lead the crew or automation to fly the aeroplane slower or faster than they think. Mitigation: recognise it, fly the memorised **pitch and thrust** settings, and use the unreliable airspeed procedure.

### 7.13 Recognising a stall

A wing stalls at the critical angle of attack, so a stall can happen **at any airspeed, attitude or altitude**: in a steep turn at high speed, in a pull-out from a dive, nose-low in a descent, or close to the ground. The angle of attack, not the speed, is what matters. `081.03.04.01.01`

A stall is recognised by **continuous stall-warning activation** together with **at least one** of: `081.03.04.01.02`

- **buffet**, which can be heavy;
- **lack of pitch authority**;
- an **uncommanded pitch down** or an **uncommanded roll**;
- **inability to arrest the rate of descent**.

A **stall warning** is a natural or synthetic indication given when approaching the stall. It may include: aerodynamic buffeting; reduced roll stability and aileron effectiveness; visual or aural cues and warnings; reduced elevator (pitch) authority; inability to maintain altitude or arrest a rate of descent; and stick-shaker activation. `081.03.04.01.03`
