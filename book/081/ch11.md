## Chapter 11 · Propellers

A propeller is a set of rotating wings. Each blade section meets an airflow made of its own rotation and the aeroplane's forward speed, and its lift and drag become thrust and torque. This chapter builds that velocity triangle, explains fixed-pitch and constant-speed propellers, efficiency, what happens when an engine fails, and the side effects of a big spinning disc on the front of an aeroplane.

### 11.1 Thrust and torque

A blade element meets a **relative airflow** that combines the rotational speed of the blade (2π × radius × rpm) and the aeroplane's TAS. Its aerodynamic force can be split in two ways. Relative to that airflow, it has **lift** (perpendicular) and **drag** (parallel). Relative to the aeroplane, it has **thrust**, along the propeller axis, and a **torque force** in the plane of rotation, opposing the rotation; summed over all the blades, the torque force times its radius is the **aerodynamic torque** the engine must supply. `081.07.01.01.01`

For a **fixed-pitch** propeller at constant rpm, as **IAS increases** the relative airflow comes more from ahead, the blade angle of attack falls and so do both **thrust** and **aerodynamic torque**; the engine then speeds up unless the throttle is reduced. As speed falls, thrust and torque rise, with the greatest static thrust at zero airspeed. `081.07.01.01.02`

### 11.2 Blade geometry

At the reference section (often 75 % of the radius) the parts of the velocity triangle are: `081.07.01.02.01`

- the **blade chord line**, from the leading to the trailing edge of the section;
- the **rotational velocity** vector, in the plane of rotation;
- the **TAS** vector, along the axis;
- the **helix angle** (advance angle), between the resulting relative airflow and the plane of rotation;
- the **blade angle** (pitch angle), between the chord line and the plane of rotation;
- the **blade angle of attack**, between the chord line and the relative airflow: **blade angle − helix angle**.

**Geometric pitch** is the distance the propeller would advance in one revolution at zero blade angle of attack (if it moved like a screw in a solid). **Effective pitch** is the distance it actually advances in one revolution. The difference is the **propeller slip**: it is what produces the blade angle of attack and the thrust.

A **fine pitch** means a **small** blade angle: good for take-off and climb at low speed and high rpm. A **coarse pitch** means a **large** blade angle: good for cruise at high speed and lower rpm. `081.07.01.02.02`

### 11.3 Blade twist

**Blade twist** is the decrease of the blade angle from root to tip. `081.07.01.03.01` It is needed because the rotational speed increases with radius while the forward speed is the same, so the helix angle is large near the root and small near the tip. To give every section about the same, efficient angle of attack, the blade angle must be large at the root and small at the tip. `081.07.01.03.02`

### 11.4 Types of propeller

`081.07.01.04.01`

- **Fixed pitch**: the blade angle is fixed in manufacture.
- **Adjustable pitch** or **variable pitch (non-governing)**: the blade angle can be changed, on the ground or by the pilot in flight, but is not automatically controlled.
- **Variable pitch (governing)**, the **constant-speed** propeller: a governor changes the blade angle automatically to keep the rpm selected by the pilot.

A **fixed-pitch** propeller is simple, light, cheap and reliable, but efficient only near one speed; elsewhere its blade angle of attack is too high or too low. A **constant-speed** propeller keeps an efficient angle of attack over a wide range of speeds and lets the pilot choose rpm and power separately, at the price of weight, cost, complexity and maintenance, plus extra pilot workload. `081.07.01.04.02`

A fixed-pitch propeller is chosen for its mission: a **climb propeller** has a fine pitch, giving good take-off and climb performance but over-speeding and poor efficiency at high cruise speed; a **cruise propeller** has a coarse pitch, giving good efficiency at cruise but poor take-off and climb. `081.07.01.04.03`

For a **fixed-pitch** propeller at constant rpm, the blade angle of attack **falls** as airspeed rises, because the helix angle increases while the blade angle stays fixed. A **variable-pitch** propeller increases its blade angle as airspeed rises, keeping the angle of attack near its best value. `081.07.01.04.04`

The forces on a rotating blade differ with the mode of operation. `081.07.01.04.05`

- **Normal**: positive blade angle of attack; the force gives forward thrust and a torque opposing rotation, which the engine overcomes.
- **Windmilling**: the airflow drives the propeller. The blade angle of attack is **negative**, the aerodynamic force points backwards (drag) and its torque component **drives** the propeller round, turning a failed engine. This produces a large drag.
- **Feathered**: the blade angle is about 90°, edge-on to the airflow. The blades produce almost no force, the propeller stops, and drag is minimal.
- **Reverse**: the blade angle is negative. The blades push air forwards, giving reverse thrust for braking after landing.

At **constant IAS**, making the pitch **coarser** (a larger blade angle) increases the blade angle of attack, so thrust and torque increase; if the engine power is unchanged, the rpm falls. Making it finer does the opposite. A constant-speed unit uses this to hold rpm: if the rpm rises, it coarsens the pitch to absorb more torque. `081.07.01.04.06`

### 11.5 Efficiency

**Propeller efficiency** is the useful thrust power divided by the shaft power from the engine: **η = (thrust × TAS) / shaft power**. `081.07.01.05.01`

It is **zero** at zero TAS, because no work is done on the aeroplane even though there is plenty of static thrust. It rises with speed to a maximum, about 80–85 %, and falls again at high speed as the blade angle of attack becomes too small and the tips approach the speed of sound (compressibility losses). A **fixed-pitch** propeller reaches its peak at one speed only and falls off on either side; a **constant-speed** propeller follows the envelope of many fixed-pitch curves and stays near its peak over a wide range. `081.07.01.05.02`

At a given TAS and rpm, increasing the **blade angle** increases the blade angle of attack and the **thrust**, up to the point where the blade stalls; beyond it thrust falls and drag rises. `081.07.01.05.03`

### 11.6 Ice on the propeller

Ice on the blades changes their shape and roughness, reducing **thrust and efficiency** and increasing the power needed. Ice usually builds and sheds unevenly, causing **imbalance and vibration**; ice thrown off the blades can damage the fuselage, which is why fuselage panels next to propellers are reinforced. Propellers are protected by electrical de-icing boots or fluid systems. `081.07.01.06.01`

### 11.7 Engine failure: windmilling and feathering

When an engine fails, its **thrust is lost**, and if its propeller **windmills**, it adds a large **drag**. On a twin, the remaining thrust on one side and the drag on the other produce a strong **yawing moment** towards the dead engine, which must be held with rudder, and the climb performance falls by much more than half. `081.07.02.01.01`

**Feathering** turns the blades edge-on so the propeller stops and its drag is minimal. That reduces the **yawing moment** (so less rudder is needed and VMC is lower), improves **performance** (less drag, better climb), improves **controllability**, and prevents further damage to a failing engine. `081.07.02.02.01`

### 11.8 Absorbing more power

A propeller must absorb the engine's power without excessive tip speed. Its ability to absorb power increases with: **diameter**, **number of blades**, **blade chord** (width), **blade angle** and camber, and **rpm**. `081.07.03.01.01`

The **diameter** is limited by **tip speed**: the tips move fastest, and must stay well below the speed of sound to avoid compressibility losses and noise; and by **ground clearance**, especially at rotation and with a deflated tyre or compressed oleo, and clearance from the fuselage and the other propeller. `081.07.03.02.01`

**Solidity** is the ratio of the total blade area to the area of the propeller disc. `081.07.03.03.01`

More blades increase solidity and allow more power to be absorbed with a **smaller diameter** (lower tip speed and noise, better ground clearance), and give smoother running and less vibration. The disadvantages are more weight, cost and complexity, and a small loss of efficiency because each blade works in the disturbed air of the one ahead. `081.07.03.03.02`

Propeller **noise** is reduced by lowering the **tip speed** (lower rpm or smaller diameter), using **more blades**, using **swept or scimitar** blade tips that delay compressibility at the tips, and synchronising or synchrophasing the propellers of multi-engine aeroplanes. `081.07.03.04.01`

### 11.9 Torque, precession, slipstream and P-factor

Below, the propeller is assumed to turn **clockwise as seen from the cockpit** (from behind), as on most Western single-engine aeroplanes.

**Torque reaction**: by Newton's third law, as the engine turns the propeller clockwise, the propeller turns the aeroplane anticlockwise: it **rolls to the left**. On the ground this puts more load on the left wheel, adding to the swing; in the air it is trimmed out or held with aileron. `081.07.04.01.01`

It can be cancelled by: `081.07.04.01.02`

- **counter-rotating propellers**: on a twin, the two engines turn in opposite directions, so their torques (and P-factors) cancel and there is no critical engine;
- **contra-rotating propellers**: two propellers on the same axis turning in opposite directions, which cancels torque, gyroscopic effects and slipstream swirl.

**Gyroscopic precession**: a spinning propeller is a gyroscope. A force applied to its rim acts as if it were applied **90° further round in the direction of rotation**. `081.07.04.02.01` Pitching or yawing the aeroplane therefore produces a moment about the other axis. With a clockwise propeller, **pitching down** (such as raising the tail of a tailwheel aeroplane on the take-off run) gives a **yaw to the left**; pitching up gives a yaw to the right; yawing left gives a pitch up, and yawing right a pitch down. The effect is strongest with large, heavy propellers and rapid attitude changes. `081.07.04.02.02`

**Slipstream**: the air behind the propeller rotates in the same direction as the propeller, spiralling round the fuselage. With a clockwise propeller it strikes the **left** side of the fin and yaws the aeroplane to the **left**. The slipstream also blows faster air over the tail and inner wing, improving control and lift at low speed and with high power, and can cause asymmetric lift between the wings. `081.07.04.03.01`

**Asymmetric blade effect (P-factor)**: when the propeller axis is inclined to the flight path, at a high angle of attack in a climb or at low speed, the **descending** blade meets the air at a larger angle of attack and a higher speed than the ascending blade, so it produces more thrust. The centre of thrust moves towards the descending blade's side: with a clockwise propeller, to the right, yawing the aeroplane to the **left**. `081.07.04.04.01`

On a twin with both propellers turning the same way (clockwise from behind), the descending blades are on the **right** of each engine. The right engine's thrust line therefore lies further from the centreline than the left's. If the **left** engine fails, the right engine's thrust acts with the longer arm and gives the greater yawing moment: the left engine is the **critical engine**. Slipstream effects over the fin can reinforce it. `081.07.04.04.02`

### 11.10 Propeller effects in practice

With a clockwise propeller during the **take-off run**, torque loads the left wheel and the slipstream hits the left of the fin, both swinging the aeroplane **left**; on a tailwheel aeroplane, raising the tail adds a gyroscopic yaw to the left. At **rotation** and in the **initial climb**, P-factor at the high angle of attack adds further left yaw. The pilot holds the aeroplane straight with **right rudder**, more at high power and low speed. `081.07.04.05.01`

In a **go-around**, full power is applied suddenly at low speed with the nose rising: torque, slipstream and P-factor all strengthen at once, producing a strong yaw and roll to the left (for a clockwise propeller) and a nose-up pitch, while the controls are least effective. Anticipate it with rudder and pitch control. `081.07.04.05.02`

The go-around effects are made worse by: `081.07.04.05.03`

- **high engine performance** (low altitude, low temperature, high density): more thrust means more yaw, and on a multi-engine aeroplane **higher VMC speeds**;
- **loss of the critical engine** during the go-around, which adds the largest asymmetric moment;
- a **crosswind**, which, depending on its side, adds to or reduces the yaw through weathercocking;
- a **high flap setting**, which leaves the aeroplane slow, with more drag and more slipstream effect over the flaps, while the rudder and ailerons are less effective.
