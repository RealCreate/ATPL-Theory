## Chapter 5 · Precipitation

A cloud is not a rain shower. Cloud droplets are about a hundredth of a millimetre across and float in the faintest updraft; a raindrop holds about a million of them. This chapter explains how droplets grow big enough to fall, what kinds of precipitation result and how they are coded, and why freezing rain and hail are so dangerous to aircraft.

### 5.1 How precipitation forms

Cloud droplets, at about 10–20 µm, are far too small to fall. To become rain, they must grow about a hundredfold in diameter, and there are two ways for that to happen: the **ice-crystal process**, named after Wegener, Bergeron and Findeisen (the WBF process), in mixed clouds below 0 °C; and **coalescence** (collision and merging), mainly in warm clouds. In mid and high latitudes most precipitation comes from the WBF process, so most rain there starts as snow; in the tropics much rain falls from warm cloud by coalescence. `050.05.01.01.01`

In a mixed cloud below 0 °C, ice crystals and supercooled droplets sit side by side. As Chapter 3 showed, the saturation vapour pressure is lower over ice than over water, so air that is just saturated for the droplets is **supersaturated for the ice**. Vapour therefore deposits on the crystals while the droplets around them evaporate, and the crystals grow fast at the droplets' expense. They join into snowflakes and fall, melting into rain if they pass below the freezing level. The process works best between about −10 and −20 °C. A cloud that is too warm to contain ice, or too thin, cannot use it. Mixed clouds are therefore both rain-makers and icing hazards. `050.05.01.01.02`

In **coalescence**, larger droplets fall faster than small ones, collide with them and merge, growing further. It works best in deep cloud with strong updrafts, plenty of liquid water and a broad range of droplet sizes, as in maritime cloud with large sea-salt nuclei. It works above and below 0 °C. Coalescence inside a supercooled cloud produces **supercooled large drops** (SLD), which fall as freezing drizzle or freezing rain without any warm layer aloft. `050.05.01.01.03`

Each kind of precipitation has its own origin:

- **Snow** forms when ice crystals grow by the WBF process and stick together into flakes. It reaches the ground when the air stays near or below 0 °C; in dry air snow can survive to the surface at +2 or +3 °C.
- **Rain** is either melted snow or drops grown by coalescence, 0.5 mm or more across.
- **Drizzle** is fine drops under 0.5 mm, formed by coalescence in shallow St or Sc with weak updrafts. It signals stratus and stable air: low ceilings, poor visibility, and the possibility of freezing drizzle.
- **Hail** grows when small ice embryos (graupel or frozen drops) are carried up and down again and again by strong Cb updrafts through regions of supercooled water. Each pass adds a layer of clear or opaque ice, until the stone is too heavy for the updraft and falls. Hail needs a Cb with a strong updraft and a deep supercooled layer, and the stronger the updraft, the larger the stones.

### 5.2 Types of precipitation and their codes

> **Key numbers · precipitation in METAR and TAF**
>
> | Code | Name | Description |
> | --- | --- | --- |
> | DZ | Drizzle | Fine drops under 0.5 mm, close together; from St |
> | RA | Rain | Drops 0.5 mm or more, or smaller and widely spaced |
> | SN | Snow | Ice crystals, mostly branched stars, often joined into flakes |
> | SG | Snow grains | Very small white opaque grains under 1 mm; from St or fog; the solid form of drizzle |
> | PL | Ice pellets | Transparent or translucent pellets under 5 mm (frozen raindrops, or melted and refrozen snow); bounce on hard ground |
> | GR | Hail | Ice balls 5 mm or more; from Cb |
> | GS | Small hail and/or snow pellets (graupel) | Under 5 mm, white and opaque, soft or with a thin ice coat; from convective cloud |
> | IC | Ice crystals (diamond dust) | Tiny crystals in very cold, clear air |
> | FZDZ, FZRA | Freezing drizzle, freezing rain | Supercooled drops that freeze on impact |
>
> They are combined with SH (showers, from convective cloud) and TS (thunderstorm), and with intensity: − light, no sign moderate, + heavy. `050.05.02.01.01`

> **Exam trap.** GR and GS are separated at 5 mm. Ice pellets (PL) are a warning sign: they mean a warm layer aloft over a cold one, so freezing rain may be nearby.

The sizes are worth knowing: **cloud droplets** are about 0.01–0.02 mm (10–20 µm, up to about 50 µm); **drizzle** drops are under 0.5 mm, typically 0.2–0.5 mm; **raindrops** are 0.5 mm or more, typically 1–2 mm, and at most about 5–6 mm, because larger drops break up as they fall. Drop size matters for icing: large supercooled drops strike further back on the wing and freeze more slowly, so the water runs back and freezes as clear ice beyond the protected areas. `050.05.02.01.02`

### 5.3 Hail

Hailstones can be more than 5 cm across, and at aircraft speeds their impact energy is enormous. They crack windscreens, smash radomes, dent leading edges, damage engine fan blades and cores, and the ingestion of hail and water can flame out an engine. Hail can fall from the anvil and outside the visible precipitation core, several miles from the storm, and it can be met at high altitude above the freezing level, where it has not melted. Keep at least 10–20 nm from Cb and never fly under an anvil. `050.05.02.01.03`

### 5.4 Freezing precipitation

Freezing precipitation forms in two ways. In the **classical (melting) mechanism**, snow falls into a warm layer above 0 °C aloft and melts into rain, then falls into a sub-zero layer near the ground. The drops become supercooled and freeze the instant they hit anything. If that cold layer is deep enough, the drops refreeze in the air and arrive as ice pellets instead. In the **warm-rain (supercooled coalescence) mechanism**, drops in shallow supercooled St or Sc grow by coalescence without ever freezing, giving freezing drizzle with no warm layer aloft at all. `050.05.02.01.04`

![Ahead of a winter warm front: rain, freezing rain, ice pellets and snow depending on the warm layer and the cold layer below](img/m-freezing.jpg)

The signature of freezing rain is an **inversion**, warm air over cold. If its position is known and you are allowed to, climbing into the warm layer can be an escape; descending into the colder air below may turn freezing rain into ice pellets.

The classic setting is ahead of a **warm front or warm occlusion in winter**, when mild air rides over a shallow layer of sub-zero continental air. It also happens in pools of cold air in valleys and basins with warmer air above, and freezing drizzle falls from stratiform cloud in stagnant cold air under a high. In continental Europe and North America in winter, freezing rain ahead of a warm front can close airports, because de-icing is ineffective in it. `050.05.02.01.05`

### 5.5 Precipitation and cloud type

The kind of cloud decides the character of the precipitation. **Convective** cloud (Cu, TCU, Cb) gives **showers**: they start and stop suddenly, change intensity quickly, have large drops and possibly hail or graupel, last a short time, and make visibility vary sharply. **Stratiform** cloud (Ns, As, St, Sc) gives **continuous or intermittent** precipitation of uniform intensity, light to moderate, lasting a long time, with smaller drops or drizzle. So SHRA means Cu or Cb, continuous RA means Ns or As, and DZ means St. `050.05.02.01.06`

>
> | Cloud | Typical precipitation | Intensity |

> | Cb | SHRA, SHSN, GR, GS, TS | Moderate to heavy |
> | Cu / TCU | SHRA, SHSN, GS | Light to moderate |

### 5.6 Winter precipitation and visibility

Visibility in precipitation depends more on the number and size of the falling particles than on how much water they contain. Snow cuts visibility far more than rain carrying the same amount of water. Large, wet flakes near 0 °C fall in clumps with gaps between them; small, dry flakes at lower temperatures are far more numerous and, for the same precipitation rate, can reduce visibility more. Heavy snow can bring visibility below 500 m, and moderate snow to about 500–1000 m. Wet snow near 0 °C also builds up heavily on airframes and runways, gives poor braking and shortens holdover times. Blowing snow (Chapter 9) reduces visibility further. `050.05.02.01.08`
