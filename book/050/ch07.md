## Chapter 7 · Pressure Systems

Chapter 6 followed the frontal depression through its life. This chapter steps back to the other pressure systems: the great permanent and seasonal highs and lows, the different kinds of anticyclone and non-frontal low, and the most violent low of all, the tropical revolving storm.

### 7.1 The principal pressure areas

Some pressure systems are permanent features of the map. The **subtropical highs** sit over the oceans at about 30°: the Azores (or Bermuda) high, the North Pacific (Hawaiian) high, and in the southern hemisphere the South Atlantic (St Helena), South Indian (Mascarene) and South Pacific highs. The **subpolar lows**, the Icelandic and Aleutian lows, are deepest in winter. The Icelandic low is not a single stationary low but the statistical average of the depressions that keep passing through. Near the equator lies the **equatorial trough**. `050.07.01.01.01`

Other systems come and go with the seasons, as the continents heat and cool:

- In **January** (northern winter) the **Siberian high** is the strongest pressure system on Earth, often above 1035 hPa, with the North American (Canadian) high alongside it. The Icelandic and Aleutian lows are deep, and thermal lows sit over southern Africa, Australia and South America, where it is summer.
- In **July** (northern summer) a large thermal low covers South Asia (the Pakistan or Persian Gulf low), with smaller ones over the south-western USA, Iberia and the Sahara. The Azores and Pacific highs are strong and displaced north, the Icelandic low is weak, and a high covers Australia in its winter.

On a map, the Siberian high belongs only to winter and the Asian low only to summer.

The systems form in two different ways. **Dynamic** systems are made by the circulation: the subtropical highs by the sinking branch of the Hadley cell, where air converges aloft at about 30°, and the subpolar lows by convergence and ascent at the polar front and the constant passage of depressions. **Thermal** systems are made by the surface: continental winter highs by intense radiative cooling of dense air, so they are shallow and cold-cored; continental summer lows by strong surface heating, so they are shallow and warm-cored. The equatorial trough comes from heating and from the convergence of the trade winds. As Chapter 1 showed, thermal systems are shallow and reverse with height, while the dynamic subtropical highs are deep, warm highs. `050.07.01.01.02`

The whole pattern follows the sun, moving poleward in the summer hemisphere and equatorward in winter, about one to two months behind the sun. It moves further over the continents than over the oceans, and the continental thermal highs and lows reverse between the seasons. Spain shows this clearly: in summer it lies under the Azores ridge with a thermal low over the Iberian interior, bringing heat and afternoon thunderstorms inland; in winter it lies in the path of Atlantic depressions, with occasional outbreaks of continental polar air. `050.07.01.01.03`

### 7.2 Anticyclones

There are four kinds of anticyclone. **Warm anticyclones** are dynamic, deep and warm-cored, like the subtropical highs. **Cold anticyclones** are thermal, shallow and cold-cored, like the continental winter highs and polar highs. **Ridges** are wedges of high pressure between lows. **Blocking highs** are persistent warm highs at middle and high latitudes. Depth is what tells warm and cold highs apart: a warm high reaches the tropopause, a cold high disappears aloft. `050.07.02.01.01`

A surface high forms where air **converges aloft**: behind upper troughs, at certain quadrants of jet streams, and in the descending branch of the Hadley cell. Air accumulates in the column, and if the upper convergence exceeds the divergence at the surface, the surface pressure rises. The extra air sinks and spreads out at the surface. A surface high therefore means divergence at low level, subsidence through the column and convergence aloft. `050.07.02.01.02`

**Subsiding** air warms at the DALR and dries. The top of a subsiding layer sinks further than its bottom, so it warms more, the lapse rate becomes stable, and a **subsidence inversion** forms, typically at 3000–6000 ft and lowering with time. Above it there is little cloud and light wind; beneath it haze, smoke and Sc collect, or fog and stratus in winter. Subsidence explains both the clear skies of highs and their persistent poor visibility and anticyclonic gloom. `050.07.02.01.03`

A **warm anticyclone** is built dynamically, by convergence aloft and subsidence. Its column is warm, so it strengthens with height; subtropical and blocking highs are of this type. A **cold anticyclone** is built thermally, by intense radiative cooling of air over snow-covered continents in winter. Its column is cold and dense, so pressure is high at the surface but the system weakens with height and becomes a low aloft. The Siberian high is cold and shallow; the Azores high is warm and deep. `050.07.02.01.04`

A **ridge** is an elongated extension of a high, typically lying between two depressions in the westerlies, in the cold air behind one and ahead of the next. It brings subsidence and surface divergence and moves along with the family of depressions. Its isobars are U-shaped around the axis, and in the northern hemisphere the wind backs as a ridge passes, after veering in the trough before it. `050.07.02.01.05`

The two kinds of anticyclone bring different weather. A **warm high** gives fine, settled weather, light winds and little cloud, or Sc under the inversion. In summer that means heat, haze and possibly isolated thunderstorms at its edges; in winter over land, fog, stratus and anticyclonic gloom. A **cold high** gives very cold, dry air, clear skies, severe frost, radiation fog or ice fog, poor visibility in smoke haze, and low stratus under its inversion. In winter, fog and stratus at dawn under a high can close many aerodromes at once, while above the inversion flying conditions are good. `050.07.02.01.06`

A ridge brings a **fair-weather interval** between fronts: subsidence clears the cloud, winds are light and visibility good in the fresh polar air, though radiation fog can form at night in winter. It is short-lived, and increasing cirrus on its western side announces the next front. Treat a ridge as a good VFR window, with the next frontal system due within 12–24 hours. `050.07.02.01.07`

A **blocking anticyclone** is a persistent warm high at middle or high latitudes, over Scandinavia or the British Isles for example, that interrupts the westerly flow. Seen on the upper chart, the flow around it can take the shape of the Greek letter omega (an *omega block*). Depressions are deflected north and south of it and slow down. A block lasts from days to weeks. Under it the weather stays settled, with heatwaves in summer and cold spells, fog and smog in winter; at its flanks depressions stall and bring persistent rain; and unusual easterly winds blow over Europe. Atlantic tracks shift to route around it. `050.07.02.01.08`

### 7.3 Non-frontal depressions

A surface low forms where air **diverges aloft**: ahead of upper troughs and at certain quadrants of jet streams. Air is removed from the column faster than low-level convergence replaces it, so the surface pressure falls and the low deepens, while air rises through the column making cloud and precipitation. Deepening lows are therefore found downstream of upper troughs, under upper divergence. `050.07.03.01.01`

Four kinds of low form without fronts, or apart from them:

- A **thermal low** forms over land heated strongly in summer, as over Iberia, the Sahara and northern India. It is shallow and warm-cored, with a weak circulation and a high above it, and brings afternoon thunderstorms if the air is moist enough.
- An **orographic** or **lee low** forms when air flows across a mountain range: the air descending the lee side is stretched vertically and the pressure falls. The Genoa low south of the Alps in northerly flow, and lows in the lee of the Rockies, are examples. They can grow into active depressions with severe weather. Genoa lows bring severe weather to the western Mediterranean, Italy and the Alps, often with the Mistral or Bora (Chapter 8).
- A **polar low** is a small (about 200–500 km), intense low that forms in very cold arctic air over a relatively warm sea in winter. It brings heavy snow showers, gales and Cb, forms and deepens quickly, and is hard to forecast.
- A **secondary low** forms within the circulation of a main depression, often on its trailing cold front or at the triple point. It can deepen rapidly, take over from the primary, and bring worse weather than it.

`050.07.03.01.02`

A **trough** is an elongated area of low pressure, with V-shaped isobars, extending from a low. Air converges and rises along it. Frontal troughs contain fronts; non-frontal troughs, such as troughs in the polar air behind a cold front or troughs in the upper air, bring bands of showers and thunderstorms. In the northern hemisphere the wind veers as a trough passes, and pressure falls ahead of it and rises behind it. Behind a cold front, a non-frontal trough may bring a second line of showers, and upper troughs at 500 hPa drive surface development and convection. `050.07.03.01.03`

### 7.4 Tropical revolving storms

A **tropical revolving storm** (TRS) needs five ingredients:

1. A sea-surface temperature of at least 26–27 °C through a deep layer of about 50 m.
2. A latitude of at least 5°, because the Coriolis force is needed to start the rotation. A TRS never forms on the equator.
3. High humidity through the troposphere, in conditionally unstable air.
4. Weak vertical wind shear; strong shear tears the warm core apart.
5. A disturbance to start it, such as an easterly wave or a disturbance in the ITCZ, with divergence aloft.

Because of cold currents and shear, tropical revolving storms do not form in the South Atlantic or south-east Pacific, and none form within about 5° of the equator. Their energy comes from the latent heat of the warm sea, so they weaken quickly over land or cold water. `050.07.04.01.01`

A TRS is steered by the flow around it. In the northern hemisphere it first moves west or west-north-west with the trade winds on the equatorward side of the subtropical high, at about 10–15 kt. It then **recurves** poleward and north-eastward around the western edge of the high into the westerlies, speeding up to 20–30 kt or more. In the southern hemisphere the track is a mirror image: west-south-west, then recurving to the south-east. After recurving the storm often becomes an extra-tropical low, which can still be dangerous at mid-latitudes. `050.07.04.01.02`

The stages are defined by the maximum sustained wind:

> **Key numbers · stages of a tropical revolving storm**
>
> | Stage | Maximum sustained wind |
> | --- | --- |
> | Tropical disturbance | Organised convection, no closed circulation |
> | Tropical depression | Closed circulation, under 34 kt |
> | Tropical storm (named) | 34–47 kt |
> | Severe tropical storm | 48–63 kt |
> | Tropical revolving storm (hurricane, typhoon, cyclone) | 64 kt or more (hurricane force, Beaufort 12) |
>
> `050.07.04.01.03`

Inside the storm:

- The **eye**, about 10–40 nm across, has light winds, little cloud, subsiding warm air and the lowest pressure, which can fall below 920 hPa.
- The **eyewall**, a ring of Cb reaching the tropopause around the eye, has the strongest winds (64 kt and up to more than 150 kt), torrential rain, and severe turbulence and icing.
- **Spiral rain bands** of Cb and thunderstorms curl in towards the centre, with gusts and squalls.
- Aloft, a **cirrus shield** spreads out from the anticyclonic outflow. At the coast, the storm surge brings flooding.

The strongest winds and worst weather are in the eyewall, often on the right-hand side of the track (poleward and forward) in the northern hemisphere. Aerodromes close, and aircraft must be flown out or secured. `050.07.04.01.04`

The circulation of a TRS is about 200–500 nm across, with gale-force winds over about 100–300 nm, an eye of about 10–40 nm, and cloud reaching the tropopause at about FL 500 or higher. Avoid it by hundreds of miles, not tens. `050.07.04.01.05`

A TRS's track can stall, loop or recurve unexpectedly, and its strength can grow very fast (rapid intensification). Its movement can only rarely be forecast exactly, and forecast cones widen with lead time, so take the utmost care near one: use the latest advisories from the Tropical Cyclone Advisory Centres and SIGMETs (Chapter 10), and carry generous fuel and alternates. `050.07.04.01.06`

### 7.5 Where and when tropical revolving storms occur

The storms have different names in different oceans:

- **Hurricane** in the North Atlantic, the Caribbean, the Gulf of Mexico and the north-east Pacific off Mexico.
- **Typhoon** in the north-west Pacific (the Philippines, China and Japan).
- **Cyclone** or **tropical cyclone** in the North Indian Ocean (the Bay of Bengal and Arabian Sea), the South Indian Ocean (Madagascar and Mauritius), around Australia and in the south-west Pacific. Australia historically also used the name *willy-willy*.

There are none in the South Atlantic (with rare exceptions) or the south-east Pacific. `050.07.04.02.01`

> **Key numbers · tropical revolving storm seasons**
>
> | Area | Season | Frequency |
> | --- | --- | --- |
> | North Atlantic | June–November, peak August–October | ≈ 10–15 named storms a year, ≈ 6 hurricanes |
> | North-east Pacific | May/June–November | — |
> | North-west Pacific | All year, peak July–October | Most frequent basin, ≈ 25–30 storms a year |
> | North Indian Ocean | April–June and October–December | ≈ 5 a year |
> | South Indian Ocean, Australia, south-west Pacific | November–April, peak January–March | — |
>
> The Bay of Bengal has two seasons, before and after the south-west monsoon, with little activity during the monsoon itself because of strong shear. `050.07.04.02.02`

### Check yourself

1. Why does the Siberian high not appear on a 300 hPa chart? *It is a cold, thermal high: its dense, cold column makes it shallow, and it becomes a low aloft.*
2. Where does a subsidence inversion form, and what weather does it bring? *In an anticyclone, typically at 3000–6000 ft. Clear skies above; haze, smoke, Sc, or fog and stratus in winter, trapped beneath.*
3. What sets a blocking high apart from an ordinary ridge? *It is a persistent warm high that interrupts the westerlies for days or weeks, deflecting depressions around it; a ridge is a transient wedge between two depressions.*
4. Why does a tropical revolving storm never form at the equator? *The Coriolis force there is zero, so no rotation can develop. A latitude of at least 5° is needed.*
5. A tropical storm has maximum sustained winds of 55 kt. What stage is it? *Severe tropical storm (48–63 kt).*
6. In which season do cyclones affect the Bay of Bengal? *April–June and October–December, before and after the south-west monsoon.*
7. Where is a Genoa low, and what forms it? *South of the Alps, in the lee of the mountains, when northerly flow crosses the Alps.*
