## Chapter 2 · Wind

Wind is air moving from high towards low pressure, bent by the Earth's rotation, slowed by friction and curved around pressure systems. This chapter builds the wind up from those forces, then follows it down into the friction layer, out into the global circulation, into local winds and mountain waves, and up to the jet streams and the clear-air turbulence they carry.

### 2.1 What wind is and how it is reported

**Wind** is the horizontal movement of air relative to the Earth's surface. It is always given by the direction it blows *from* and its speed, so 270/20 is a 20 kt wind from the west. Vertical movements are called updrafts and downdrafts, not wind. The **surface wind** is the wind measured 10 m above open, level ground. It decides the runway in use, the crosswind and tailwind components, and the take-off and landing distances. `050.02.01.01.01`

Wind directions are given in **degrees true** in METARs, TAFs, SIGMETs, upper-wind charts and other written reports, and in **degrees magnetic** when the tower or ATIS gives a wind for runway use. Speeds are in knots (KT) or metres per second (MPS); 1 m/s is about 2 kt, and some charts use km/h. To work out a crosswind from a METAR, first convert its true direction to magnetic with the local variation, then compare it with the runway's magnetic heading. `050.02.01.01.02`

> **Exam trap.** The ATIS and tower wind is magnetic, not true.

The reported wind is an average from an anemometer and wind vane at 10 m. The tower (and any ATS unit or local report) averages over **2 minutes**; METARs and SPECIs average over **10 minutes**, unless there was a marked discontinuity, in which case only the period after it is used. A **gust** is the highest 3-second mean wind, reported when it exceeds the mean by 10 kt or more. The direction is reported as VRB when the mean speed is 3 kt or less (or the direction varies widely), and in the form "dddVddd" when it varies by 60° or more with a mean of 3 kt or more. The 2-minute tower wind describes conditions at landing better than the 10-minute METAR wind. `050.02.01.01.03`

### 2.2 The pressure gradient force

The **horizontal pressure gradient** is how fast pressure changes with horizontal distance, measured at right angles to the isobars from high to low (in hPa per 100 km, say). Closely spaced isobars mean a strong gradient. `050.02.02.01.01`

The gradient produces the **pressure gradient force** (PGF), which per unit mass is (1/ρ) × ΔP/Δn. It acts from high to low pressure, at right angles to the isobars, and it is what sets the air moving. Because density appears underneath, the same pressure difference produces a larger force where the air is thinner; that, plus the absence of friction, is why the same isobar spacing gives a stronger wind aloft than at the surface. `050.02.02.01.02`

> **Exam trap.** The pressure gradient force acts at right angles to the isobars, never along them.

### 2.3 The Coriolis force

As soon as air moves, the Earth turns beneath it, and to an observer on the ground the air appears deflected. This apparent force is the **Coriolis force**, CF = 2 Ω V sin φ, where Ω is the Earth's rate of rotation, V the wind speed and φ the latitude. It acts at right angles to the motion: to the **right in the northern hemisphere** and to the left in the southern. It is zero at the equator, greatest at the poles, and proportional to the wind speed. It changes the direction of the air, never its speed, and it cannot start air moving: with no wind there is no Coriolis force. `050.02.02.01.03`

> **Exam trap.** Coriolis is zero at the equator and maximum at the poles, not the other way round.

### 2.4 The geostrophic wind

Imagine air at rest between a high and a low. The pressure gradient force starts it moving towards the low. As it gains speed, the Coriolis force grows and turns it to the right (northern hemisphere), until the Coriolis force exactly balances the pressure gradient force. The air then flows steadily, without acceleration, **parallel to the straight isobars**. This is the **geostrophic wind**, with speed Vg = (ΔP/Δn) ÷ (ρ × 2Ω sin φ). It exists only above the friction layer, with straight, parallel isobars, in steady flow, and more than about 15° from the equator. `050.02.02.01.04`

The geostrophic wind blows along the isobars on surface charts and along the contours on upper-air charts. **Buys Ballot's law** puts this in a sentence: in the northern hemisphere, stand with your back to the wind and low pressure is on your left; in the southern hemisphere it is on your right. Air therefore circulates anticlockwise round lows and clockwise round highs in the northern hemisphere, and the opposite way in the southern. To find the wind direction at a point on a northern-hemisphere chart, draw the tangent to the isobar with the low on your left. One consequence for altimetry: in the northern hemisphere, a drift to the right means the wind is from the left, so you are flying towards lower pressure and the altimeter over-reads. `050.02.02.01.05`

![Geostrophic wind in both hemispheres: PGF towards the LOW, Coriolis equal and opposite towards the HIGH, wind parallel to the isobars with the LOW on its left (NH) or right (SH); below, the full circulation: anticlockwise round a LOW and clockwise round a HIGH in the NH, reversed in the SH](img/m-geostrophic.jpg)

> **Exam trap.** In a figure showing geostrophic balance, the PGF and Coriolis arrows point the same way in both hemispheres: PGF at the LOW, Coriolis at the HIGH. Only the wind arrow tells you which hemisphere it is. If the LOW is on the wind's right, it is the southern hemisphere.

Latitude changes the geostrophic wind even when the isobar spacing does not. The pressure gradient force depends only on the spacing (and density), but the Coriolis force, 2Ω V sin φ, produces less force per knot of wind the closer you are to the equator, because sin φ shrinks. The wind therefore has to blow **faster at low latitudes** before its Coriolis force grows big enough to balance the same pressure gradient force: Vg ∝ 1/sin φ. With the spacing that gives 20 kt at 60°, you get about 24 kt at 45° and 35 kt at 30° (sin 60° ÷ sin 30° ≈ 1.7). Turned round, to give the same wind, isobars at low latitude must be further apart, which is why a chart's geostrophic wind scale is drawn for one latitude. Within about 15° of the equator sin φ is so small that the Coriolis force can never catch up: the balance never forms, the air flows straight across the isobars from high to low and fills the pressure difference. Tropical pressure gradients are therefore weak, and tropical charts use streamlines instead of isobars. `050.02.02.01.06`

![Globe: at 60°N the ground turns faster about the vertical than at 30°N, so the same pressure gradient is balanced at a lower wind speed; none at the equator](img/m-latitude2.jpg)

> **Exam trap.** The same isobar spacing does not give the same wind at all latitudes. Compare spacing only at the same latitude.

### 2.5 The gradient wind

When the isobars are curved, the air is following a curved path, and that needs a net force towards the centre of the curve. Round a **low**, the pressure gradient force points inward, while the Coriolis and centrifugal forces point outward: PGF = Coriolis + centrifugal, so the Coriolis force, and with it the wind speed, is smaller than geostrophic. The gradient wind round a low is **sub-geostrophic**. Round a **high**, the Coriolis force points inward and balances both the outward pressure gradient force and the centrifugal force, so it must be larger than geostrophic, and the gradient wind round a high is **super-geostrophic**. For identical spacing and latitude, then, the wind is strongest round a high, middling with straight isobars and weakest round a low. In practice highs usually have weak gradients, so their winds are lighter anyway. `050.02.02.01.07`

![Gradient wind: force balance round a low, along straight isobars and round a high, with the resulting wind speeds](img/m-gradient.jpg)

### 2.6 Wind in the friction layer

Near the ground, friction slows the air. The Coriolis force, which depends on speed, weakens with it, so the pressure gradient force is no longer fully balanced and the wind turns across the isobars **towards low pressure**. Going up, friction decreases, so the wind speeds up and turns until, at the top of the friction layer around 2000–3000 ft, it is geostrophic. In the northern hemisphere the wind therefore **veers and increases** with height through the friction layer; in the southern hemisphere it backs and increases. On a northern-hemisphere climb-out the wind veers and strengthens; on the descent it backs and weakens, so expect the crosswind and headwind to change on final. `050.02.02.02.01`

The surface wind has a daily cycle. By day, surface heating creates turbulence that mixes the faster, veered air from higher up down to the surface, so the surface wind becomes **stronger, veered and gusty**, peaking in the early afternoon. At night a surface inversion cuts the surface layer off from the air above, so the surface wind becomes **lighter and backed**, while the wind just above the inversion may strengthen into a low-level jet (section 2.12). A calm night at the surface with a strong wind at 1000–2000 ft means low-level wind shear on the approach. Over the sea the daily cycle is small. `050.02.02.02.02`

How deep the friction layer is depends on three things: **terrain roughness, wind speed and stability**. Rough ground, strong wind and unstable air mix momentum higher and give a deeper layer; a smooth surface such as the sea, light wind and stable air (a night inversion) give a shallower one. Typical depths run from about 1000 ft over a calm sea at night to 3000 ft or more over rough ground on an unstable day. `050.02.02.02.03`

### 2.7 Convergence and divergence

**Convergence** is a net horizontal inflow of air into a region, so that air piles up; **divergence** is a net outflow. Air cannot pile up indefinitely, so low-level convergence must be balanced by rising air and divergence aloft, and low-level divergence by sinking air and convergence aloft. Low-level convergence in troughs, the ITCZ or along a sea-breeze front therefore means ascent, cloud and showers. `050.02.02.03.01`

![Convergence at the surface and divergence aloft over a low; the reverse over a high](img/m-convergence.jpg)

This links the surface to the upper air. In a **surface low**, friction makes the surface wind cross the isobars inward, so air converges, rises and diverges aloft. If the divergence aloft removes air faster than the surface convergence brings it in, the surface pressure falls and the low **deepens**. In a **surface high**, air diverges at the surface, subsides and converges aloft; if the upper convergence exceeds the surface divergence, the high **builds**. Changes of wind speed along the upper flow, especially at the entrances and exits of jet streams, are what create this upper divergence and convergence, and so develop the surface systems. The weather follows: lows and troughs bring cloud and precipitation; highs bring subsidence, clear skies or a layer of stratiform cloud under an inversion, and haze or fog. `050.02.02.03.02`

> **Exam trap.** A surface low deepens because upper-level divergence exceeds low-level convergence, not because of more surface convergence alone.

### 2.8 The general circulation

The equator receives more energy than it radiates away and the poles less, so heat must be carried towards the poles. On a non-rotating Earth one giant convection cell per hemisphere would do it. The Earth's rotation breaks it into **three cells in each hemisphere**:

- The **Hadley cell**, from 0° to 30°. Air rises at the equator in the ITCZ, flows poleward aloft, sinks at about 30° into the subtropical highs and returns towards the equator at the surface as the **trade winds**. The Coriolis force turns that equatorward flow to the right in the northern hemisphere and to the left in the southern, so the trades blow from the **north-east** in the northern hemisphere and from the **south-east** in the southern. It is thermally direct: warm air rises and cool air sinks.
- The **Ferrel cell**, from 30° to 60°. At the surface the flow is poleward and deflected into the **westerlies** (south-westerly in the northern hemisphere, north-westerly in the southern); the air rises at about 60°, at the subpolar lows and the polar front. It is thermally indirect: it rises over colder air and sinks over warmer air.
- The **polar cell**, from 60° to 90°. Air sinks at the pole into the polar high, flows out at the surface as the **polar easterlies** and rises at about 60°.

> **Exam trap.** To get any surface wind belt's direction, start from where the air is going (towards the equator for the trades, towards the pole for the westerlies) and turn it with Coriolis: right in the northern hemisphere, left in the southern. Equatorward air turned right comes from the NE; poleward air turned right comes from the SW.

The result is a set of surface pressure belts: the equatorial low, the subtropical highs (the "horse latitudes"), the subpolar lows and the polar highs. Aloft, the flow is mostly westerly from about 20° poleward, with the subtropical and polar front jets embedded in it. `050.02.03.01.01`

![Three-cell model, NH: Hadley, Ferrel and polar cells, jets and pressure belts](img/gcirc.jpg)

The real surface pattern follows the model: the equatorial trough and ITCZ; the north-east trades in the northern hemisphere and south-east trades in the southern; the subtropical highs (Azores, North Pacific, St Helena, Mascarene, South Pacific); the westerlies; the subpolar lows (Icelandic, Aleutian); and the polar easterlies. The whole pattern moves with the sun. In **January** the belts are shifted south, strong cold highs sit over the continents (the Siberian and North American highs), the Icelandic and Aleutian lows are deep, and the north-east monsoon blows over South Asia. In **July** the belts are shifted north, a thermal low sits over South Asia (the Pakistan low), the south-west monsoon blows, and the Azores high extends towards Europe. On map questions, the Siberian high belongs only to winter and the Asian low only to summer. Chapter 8 develops this into climatology, including the seasonal movement of the ITCZ. `050.02.03.01.02`

Aloft, westerlies dominate from about 20–30° poleward in both hemispheres and are strongest in winter, when the temperature contrast between pole and equator is greatest. Easterlies blow aloft over the tropics, reaching further from the equator in the summer hemisphere, and in the northern summer the Tropical Easterly Jet blows at about FL 450–500 over South Asia and Africa. Eastbound North Atlantic flights use the westerly tailwind, especially in winter; westbound tracks avoid the jet core. `050.02.03.01.03`

> **Exam trap.** The mid-latitude upper westerlies do not turn easterly in summer. They persist, only weaker.

### 2.9 Slope and valley winds

On a sunny day a mountain slope heats the air in contact with it, which becomes less dense than air at the same level over the valley and flows **up the slope**. This is the **anabatic wind**: weak, about 5–10 kt, and often marked by Cu over the ridges. At night the slope cools by radiation, the air touching it becomes denser and drains **down the slope** under gravity: the **katabatic wind**. Katabatic winds can be strong where a large cold plateau or ice sheet feeds them, as in Greenland and Antarctica, and the Bora is partly katabatic. Katabatic flow fills valleys with cold air, giving valley fog, frost, an inversion and wind shear at the valley rim. Remember *ana* = up. `050.02.04.01.01`

![Anabatic wind up the slopes by day, katabatic wind down the slopes by night](img/m-slopewinds.jpg)

The same effect on the scale of a whole valley gives the **valley wind**, blowing up the valley towards the mountains by day, and the **mountain wind**, blowing down the valley at night. Both are strongest in summer, under clear skies and with a weak pressure gradient. At a valley aerodrome the runway in use may change between morning and afternoon. `050.02.04.01.02`

### 2.10 Venturi effect

Air forced through a gap or a narrowing valley must speed up to get through (continuity), and as it speeds up its static pressure falls (Bernoulli). The Mistral down the Rhône valley, the Tramontane, and the Levante through the Strait of Gibraltar are examples. Expect much stronger local wind and turbulence in passes than the pressure gradient suggests, and remember from Chapter 1 that the altimeter over-reads in this low-pressure, accelerated flow. `050.02.04.01.03`

![Venturi effect in a gap: streamlines crowd together, wind speeds up and pressure falls](img/m-venturi.jpg)

### 2.11 Sea and land breezes

By day the land heats faster than the sea. Air rises over the land, a shallow thermal low forms at the surface, and air flows **onshore** at low level, with a return flow offshore aloft. This **sea breeze** is typically 10–15 kt and 1000–3000 ft deep, and pushes 10–30 nm inland, more in the tropics. It starts in mid-morning, peaks in mid-afternoon and, in the northern hemisphere, veers through the day as the Coriolis force acts on it. At night the land cools faster and a weaker **land breeze** of about 5 kt blows offshore. Both need strong heating and a weak synoptic pressure gradient, which is why they are common under a high. `050.02.04.01.04`

The leading edge of the cool marine air is the **sea-breeze front**, a convergence line that can produce a line of Cu, sometimes showers or thunderstorms, wind shear and turbulence, and a drop in visibility from haze or smoke. At a coastal aerodrome the runway wind can reverse in the late morning, and sea fog can be carried inland.

![Sea breeze by day, land breeze by night: shallow thermal circulation, sea-breeze front with Cu, return flow aloft](img/m-seabreeze.jpg)

### 2.12 The evening low-level jet

After sunset a surface radiation inversion separates the air above from the friction of the ground. Freed from friction, the wind just above the inversion accelerates and can become super-geostrophic, through an effect called inertial oscillation. It peaks around midnight to early morning, a few hundred to about 2000 ft above the ground. For a night or early-morning approach with a calm surface wind, expect strong shear between about 300 and 1500 ft and be ready for airspeed fluctuations. `050.02.04.01.05`

### 2.13 Mountain waves

When **stable** air is forced up over a ridge, it is lifted away from the level where it would naturally rest. On the lee side it sinks back past that level, becomes warmer than its surroundings, bounces back up, and keeps oscillating as it travels downstream. The wind holds this oscillation in place relative to the ground, so the waves stand still: **standing waves**, or **lee waves**. Their energy can also travel upward to great heights. In unstable air the same lifting produces convection instead. `050.02.05.01.01`

Mountain waves need three conditions:

1. A wind blowing within about 30° of perpendicular to the ridge.
2. A wind at ridge-top level of at least about 15 kt for small ridges, or 25 kt for large ranges, **increasing with height** with little change of direction.
3. A **stable layer** near ridge-top level, with less stable air above and below it.

`050.02.05.01.02`

> **Exam trap.** Distractors offer "unstable air at ridge level" or "wind decreasing with height". Both are wrong.

The waves typically have a wavelength of 2–30 km, longer with stronger wind and weaker stability. Their amplitude can reach several thousand feet, and vertical speeds can exceed 2000 ft/min. The strongest **downdraft** is on the immediate lee slope. Beneath the wave crests, at or below ridge-top height, **rotors** form: rolling eddies with severe turbulence. Above the rotor the flow in the wave itself is smooth and laminar, but an aircraft holding a constant attitude can still gain or lose several thousand feet. The waves can extend into the stratosphere. The most severe turbulence is in the rotor, below the crests, on the lee side, roughly at ridge height. `050.02.05.01.03`

![Mountain waves: cap cloud, lenticulars at the crests, lee downdraft and rotor](img/mwave.jpg)

Mountain waves show themselves through cloud, when there is moisture:

- **Lenticular clouds** (ACSL, CCSL, SCSL) are smooth, lens-shaped and stationary. They sit at the wave crests, sometimes stacked like a pile of plates. They do not drift with the wind: air flows through them, condensing on the way up and evaporating on the way down.
- **Rotor** (roll) clouds are ragged lines of Cu or Sc parallel to the ridge, at low level under the crests.
- A **cap cloud**, or Föhn wall, drapes over the ridge on the windward side, with a clear Föhn gap in the lee.

Without moisture the waves are invisible, so a clear sky does not mean there are no waves. `050.02.05.01.04`

Mountain-wave downdrafts of 2000 ft/min or more can exceed the climb performance of light aircraft, and of heavy aircraft at high altitude; rotor turbulence can exceed structural limits; and the altimeter over-reads in the accelerated, low-pressure flow, which adds to the danger near terrain. In strong winds, cross ridges with a margin of at least half the ridge height and at about 45°, so that you can turn away; fly at turbulence penetration speed; and avoid the lee side at low level. `050.02.05.01.05`

With favourable profiles of wind and stability, wave energy climbs to the tropopause and into the stratosphere, where the waves can break and produce clear-air turbulence at cruise levels. Well-known areas are Greenland, the Rockies, the Andes, the Alps, the Himalaya and Norway; on North Atlantic tracks, turbulence over Greenland at FL 300 and above is often mountain-wave turbulence. SWC charts mark areas of severe mountain wave (SEV MTW), and a SIGMET is issued for it. `050.02.05.01.06`

### 2.14 Turbulence

**Turbulence** is irregular, random eddying motion of the air in all directions, which makes an aircraft's attitude, altitude and airspeed change rapidly and varies its load factor. **Gustiness** is the rapid fluctuation of the surface wind's speed and direction about its mean, caused by the same eddies; it is reported as a gust (G) when the peak exceeds the mean by 10 kt or more. On a gusty day, add the gust factor to the approach speed as your procedures require. The ICAO intensity scale (light, moderate, severe) is in Chapter 9. `050.02.06.01.01`

There are five meteorological types of turbulence: **convective** (thermal), **mechanical** (friction with the ground and obstacles), **orographic** (mountains, waves and rotors), **frontal** (shear and lifting at fronts) and **clear-air turbulence** (high-level shear). Wake turbulence is aircraft-made, not meteorological. Each type has its forecast: convective turbulence goes with CB areas on the SWC chart, CAT with the CAT areas, and orographic turbulence with mountain-wave markings. `050.02.06.01.02`

Each type forms in its own way:

- **Convective turbulence** comes from uneven heating: thermals rise and compensating downdrafts sink. It is strongest on sunny afternoons over land, in and below Cu and Cb, so expect a bumpy approach below Cu bases on a summer afternoon. Above the cloud tops or an inversion the air is smoother.
- **Mechanical turbulence** comes from wind blowing over rough ground, trees and buildings. It grows with wind speed, roughness and instability and stays within the friction layer. Expect it downwind of hangars and buildings on short final.
- **Orographic turbulence** comes from air flowing over and around mountains: lee eddies, waves and rotors.
- **Frontal turbulence** comes from the vertical shear and lifting across a frontal surface, and is strongest with fast-moving, active cold fronts and squall lines.

`050.02.06.02.01`

Turbulence is normally found near rough ground and relief (in the friction layer and the lee of hills); at inversions, where air layers slide over each other; in and near Cb and thunderstorms, up to 20 nm away and above their tops; in unstable layers; and near jet streams and tropopause breaks. Avoid Cb by at least 10 nm, or 20 nm when severe or at high level, and never fly under the anvil. Expect shear when crossing a low inversion on climb or descent, especially at night. `050.02.06.02.02`

### 2.15 Clear-air turbulence

**Clear-air turbulence** (CAT) is turbulence at high level, usually above FL 200, in cloud-free air and not linked to convection. It is caused by strong wind shear. It is patchy, typically 50–100 nm long, 10–20 nm wide and 2000–5000 ft deep, and often lies in bands parallel to a jet stream. There is no visual warning and no radar return, which is why seat belts stay fastened while seated. `050.02.06.03.01`

CAT forms where strong vertical (and horizontal) wind shear acts across a stable layer. Waves form on the boundary between the layers, grow and break into turbulent eddies, a process called **Kelvin–Helmholtz instability**. Turbulence appears when the shear is strong enough to overcome the stability, which is the idea behind the Richardson number. As a rule of thumb, forecasters expect CAT where the vertical shear reaches 5 kt per 1000 ft or the horizontal shear 20 kt per 100 nm, and severe CAT is likely above about 6 kt per 1000 ft. Shear comes from horizontal temperature gradients (the thermal wind, section 2.16), so tightly packed isotherms on a chart flag CAT. `050.02.06.03.02`

CAT is found:

- near **jet streams**, mainly on the cold (polar, low-pressure) side just below the core, and above the core near the tropopause break;
- in sharply curved **upper troughs** and ridges, and wherever the jet curves;
- at jet **entrance and exit** regions, and where streams of air converge or spread apart;
- near **tropopause breaks** and in breaking mountain waves.

`050.02.06.03.03`

> **Exam trap.** CAT is usually worst on the cold (low-pressure) side of the jet, not the warm side.

Clear air has no moisture or particles, so CAT cannot be seen by satellites or weather radar. Forecasts use indices of shear and deformation computed from numerical models (such as the Ellrod index), and they are probabilistic: a forecast CAT area means turbulence is possible there, and it can still occur outside. `050.02.06.03.04`

Since nothing can measure CAT remotely, **pilot reports** (special air-reports, and automated reports of eddy dissipation rate) are the only direct observations. They feed SIGMETs and warn other crews, so report moderate or severe turbulence promptly, with position, flight level, time and aircraft type. `050.02.06.03.05`

### 2.16 Jet streams

A **jet stream** is a strong, narrow, nearly horizontal current of air with its axis near the tropopause, with strong vertical and lateral wind shear and one or more speed maxima. Jets are a product of the **thermal wind**: where there is a strong horizontal temperature contrast, pressure surfaces slope more and more steeply with height (because cold columns are thinner than warm ones), so the geostrophic wind increases with height through that layer. In the northern hemisphere the thermal wind blows with cold air on its left, so the jets are mostly westerly. A jet can give a tailwind or headwind of 100–200 kt, and brings CAT with it; flight planning uses jets eastbound and avoids them westbound. `050.02.07.01.01`

By the WMO definition a jet stream has a core speed of at least **60 kt**. SWC charts only draw jets of **80 kt** or more, as arrows with wind barbs and the flight level of the core, and in some formats with the vertical extent of the 80 kt isotach. `050.02.07.01.02`

> **Exam trap.** The definition is 60 kt. The SWC chart threshold is 80 kt. Distractors offer 50, 80 or 100 kt for the definition.

Jets are thousands of nautical miles long (in segments of often 1000–3000 nm), about 100–300 nm (200–500 km) wide and about 10 000–15 000 ft (3–5 km) deep. Core speeds are typically 100–200 kt and reach 250–300 kt over Japan in winter. Typical shear is 5–10 kt per 1000 ft vertically and 20–50 kt per 100 nm horizontally on the cold side. Because a jet is far wider than it is deep, changing level can take you out of the core faster than a sideways diversion. `050.02.07.01.03`

There are four named jets:

> **Key numbers · jet streams**
>
> | Jet | Level | Latitude | Season and character |
> | --- | --- | --- | --- |
> | Arctic (front) | ≈ FL 200–250 | ≈ 60–70° | Mainly winter, along the arctic front |
> | Polar front | ≈ FL 300–350 | ≈ 40–65° | Follows the polar front; further south and stronger in winter (≈ 40°, 150+ kt), further north and weaker in summer; variable, meanders with the long waves |
> | Subtropical | ≈ FL 400 (≈ 200 hPa) | ≈ 25–30° in winter, ≈ 40° in summer | Persistent, at the poleward edge of the Hadley cell; strongest in winter, maximum over Japan |
> | Tropical easterly | ≈ FL 450–500 (≈ 100–150 hPa) | ≈ 10–20° N | Northern summer only (June–September), over southern India, the Arabian Sea and Africa; **easterly**, ≈ 50–100 kt; linked to the SW monsoon |
>
> `050.02.07.02.01`

The subtropical jet is higher and more persistent; the polar front jet is more variable and goes with fronts and CAT.

> **Exam trap.** The tropical jet is easterly, and it exists only in the northern summer.

### 2.17 Where the jet sits, and where the CAT is

The polar front jet lies **in the warm air**, just below the higher, warm-side tropopause, at the tropopause break. Because the frontal surface slopes upward towards the cold side, the jet core lies roughly **300–600 km poleward of the surface front**, above air that is cold at the surface. Its maximum wind is below the tropopause, near FL 300–350. The arctic jet lies the same way relative to the arctic front, at a lower level. On satellite images the jet axis often runs along the sharp poleward edge of the frontal cirrus shield. `050.02.07.03.01`

![Polar front jet cross-section: core at the tropopause break, worst CAT on the cold side below the core](img/jet.jpg)

> **Exam trap.** The jet core is not above the surface front, and not in the cold air. It is in the warm air, displaced poleward of the surface front.

The worst CAT and shear lie on the **cold (polar, low-pressure) side** of the jet just below the core, and in the stable layer above the core near the tropopause break; also where the jet curves sharply round upper troughs, and at jet exits. The greatest vertical shear is above and below the core; the greatest horizontal shear is on the cold side. To escape jet CAT, change level (often descending on the cold side or climbing above the core) or move sideways towards the warm side. A rapid change of outside air temperature at constant level suggests you are crossing the frontal zone near the jet: expect CAT. `050.02.07.03.02`

### Check yourself

1. Why is the gradient wind round a low weaker than the geostrophic wind for the same isobar spacing? *Round a low the pressure gradient force must supply the centripetal force as well as balance the Coriolis force, so the Coriolis force, and with it the wind speed, is smaller than geostrophic.*
2. In the southern hemisphere, the wind at 2000 ft over land is 180/30. Roughly what is the surface wind? *Veered by about 30° and halved: about 210/15.*
3. Isobars are equally spaced at 40° N and 60° N. Where is the geostrophic wind stronger? *At 40° N, because Vg is proportional to 1/sin φ.*
4. Name the three conditions for mountain waves. *Wind within about 30° of perpendicular to the ridge; wind of at least 15–25 kt at ridge level, increasing with height; a stable layer near ridge level with less stable air above and below.*
5. Where is the most severe turbulence in a mountain-wave system? *In the rotor zone on the lee side, below the wave crests, roughly at ridge height.*
6. What is the minimum speed of a jet stream, and from what speed are jets drawn on SWC charts? *60 kt by definition; 80 kt on SWC charts.*
7. On which side of a northern-hemisphere westerly jet is CAT usually worst? *On the cold, polar side (the left side looking downwind), just below the core.*
8. What makes a surface depression deepen? *Upper-level divergence exceeding low-level convergence.*
