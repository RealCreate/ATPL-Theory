## Chapter 3 · Thermodynamics

This chapter is about water and vertical motion. Water vapour is only a small part of the air, but when it condenses it makes cloud and releases the heat that drives storms. Whether rising air keeps rising or sinks back, which is the question of stability, decides what kind of cloud forms. Almost every cloud, fog, icing and thunderstorm question in later chapters comes back to the ideas here.

### 3.1 Water vapour in the air

At the same pressure and temperature, equal volumes of gas hold the same number of molecules. A water vapour molecule (molar mass 18) is lighter than the nitrogen (28) and oxygen (32) it replaces, so **moist air is less dense than dry air**. Meteorologists allow for this with the *virtual temperature*, the temperature dry air would need to have the same density. In practice humidity slightly raises the density altitude, adding to the long take-off runs of hot, humid days. `050.03.01.01.01`

> **Exam trap.** Humid air is lighter than dry air, not heavier, however "heavy" a humid day feels.

Water vapour matters to meteorology out of all proportion to its amount. It is the source of all cloud, fog and precipitation; it stores and carries latent heat, the energy of Cb and tropical revolving storms; it is the main greenhouse gas, absorbing long-wave radiation; and its content sets the saturated adiabatic lapse rate, and so the stability of the air. More moisture means more energy for convection and more supercooled water for icing. `050.03.01.01.02`

Most of it comes from **evaporation from the oceans**, with smaller amounts from lakes, rivers, wet ground and snow and ice (by sublimation), and from plants (transpiration; together with evaporation, *evapotranspiration*). Evaporation is faster when the water is warmer, the wind stronger and the air drier. Air masses formed over the sea are therefore moist, and those formed over land dry. `050.03.01.01.03`

Air is **saturated** when it holds the most water vapour it can at its temperature, so that over a flat water surface evaporation and condensation are in balance; its relative humidity is then 100 %. The amount needed to saturate air, measured as the *saturation vapour pressure*, rises steeply with temperature, roughly doubling for every 10 °C. At temperatures below zero it is **lower over ice than over water**, which is the key to how most mid-latitude rain begins (Chapter 5). Air reaches saturation either by being cooled, which is by far the most common way, or by having moisture added. `050.03.01.01.04`

### 3.2 Dew point and relative humidity

The **dew point** (Td) is the temperature to which air must be cooled, at constant pressure and without adding or removing water vapour, for it to become saturated. It measures how much water the air actually contains: the dew point changes only if moisture is added or removed (or the pressure changes). The equivalent for saturation over ice is the *frost point*. In the METAR group 08/06 the temperature is 8 °C and the dew point 6 °C, a spread of 2 °C: if the air keeps cooling, fog or low cloud is near. `050.03.01.03.01`

**Relative humidity** (RH) is the actual vapour content (or pressure) divided by the saturation value at the same temperature, as a percentage. It tells you how close the air is to saturation, not how much water it holds: a cold night at 100 % RH can contain far less water than tropical air at 80 %. `050.03.01.03.02`

At constant pressure, two things change the relative humidity. **Temperature**: cooling lowers the saturation content, so RH rises, and warming lowers RH. **Moisture**: adding vapour, by evaporation or by advection of moister air, raises RH. With the moisture content unchanged, RH and temperature move in opposite directions. `050.03.01.03.03`

Over a typical day the moisture content stays roughly constant, so RH follows the temperature inversely. It peaks around **sunrise**, when the temperature is lowest, and is lowest in the **early afternoon**, when the temperature peaks. That is why mist, fog and low stratus are most likely around dawn and clear as the day warms. `050.03.01.03.04`

The dew point can never be higher than the temperature, and the two are equal at saturation. The gap between them, the **spread**, closes as the air cools or gains moisture. In rising unsaturated air the temperature falls at 1 °C per 100 m while the dew point falls only about 0.2 °C per 100 m, so the spread closes at about 0.8 °C per 100 m, or 2.5 °C per 1000 ft. That gives the rule for the base of convective cloud: **cloud base ≈ spread × 400 ft** (or × 125 m). In a series of METARs, temperature and dew point converging warn of deteriorating visibility. `050.03.01.03.05`

> **Worked example · cloud base.** Surface temperature 22 °C, dew point 14 °C. Spread = 8 °C. Cumulus base ≈ 8 × 400 = **3200 ft** above the surface.

The spread also gives a quick estimate of relative humidity, for temperatures around 10–20 °C:

> **Key numbers · spread and relative humidity**
>
> | Spread T − Td | Approximate RH |
> | --- | --- |
> | 0 °C | 100 % |
> | 1 °C | ≈ 94 % |
> | 2 °C | ≈ 88 % |
> | 5 °C | ≈ 70 % |
> | 10 °C | ≈ 50 % |
> | 20 °C | ≈ 25 % |
>
> For small spreads RH falls by roughly 5–6 % per degree. `050.03.01.03.06`

A spread of 2–3 °C or less with a clear, calm night ahead means fog. A large spread means high cloud bases and, under high-based Cb, the possibility of virga and dry microbursts (Chapter 9).

### 3.3 Changes of state and latent heat

Water exists as vapour, liquid and ice, and every change between them absorbs or releases **latent heat**: energy taken in or given out without any change in temperature.

> **Key numbers · changes of state**
>
> | Change | Name | Latent heat |
> | --- | --- | --- |
> | Vapour → liquid | Condensation | Releases ≈ 2500 J/g |
> | Liquid → vapour | Evaporation | Absorbs ≈ 2500 J/g |
> | Liquid → ice | Freezing | Releases ≈ 334 J/g |
> | Ice → liquid | Melting | Absorbs ≈ 334 J/g |
> | Vapour → ice | Deposition (often called sublimation) | Releases ≈ 2834 J/g |
> | Ice → vapour | Sublimation | Absorbs ≈ 2834 J/g |
>
> Moving towards more energetic states (ice → water → vapour) absorbs heat; moving the other way releases it. Deposition releases condensation plus freezing: 2500 + 334 ≈ 2834 J/g. EASA texts often use "sublimation" for both directions between vapour and ice, so read the context. `050.03.02.01.01` `050.03.02.01.13`

**Condensation** needs air at, or very slightly beyond, saturation, and particles to condense on. **Evaporation** needs unsaturated air and a supply of energy; it is faster when the air is warmer, windier and drier (a larger spread). Rain falling into dry air evaporates and cools it, which produces virga, downdrafts and microbursts. `050.03.02.01.02`

The air reaches its dew point in four ways: by **adiabatic expansion** as it is lifted (by convection, by mountains, at fronts or where air converges), by **radiation** cooling the surface beneath it, by **advection** over a cold surface, and by **mixing** of two air masses. The vapour then condenses on nuclei into droplets of about 10–20 µm, which we see as cloud or fog. Most cloud forms by adiabatic cooling of rising air; fog forms by cooling at the surface. `050.03.02.01.03`

Pure air would need to be supersaturated by several hundred per cent before droplets could form on their own, because surface tension makes very small droplets evaporate again. In reality the lower atmosphere is full of **condensation nuclei**: hygroscopic particles of sea salt, sulphates, combustion products and dust that attract water. On these, condensation starts at around 100 % RH, and on the most hygroscopic even below it, which is why haze and mist appear at 75–95 %. In polluted industrial areas fog forms earlier, is thicker and lasts longer. `050.03.02.01.04`

Condensation does two things to the weather. It makes cloud and fog, and it releases latent heat into the air. That heat slows the cooling of rising saturated air, so the saturated lapse rate is smaller than the dry one; it warms the cloud relative to the air around it and drives further ascent, which is the engine of instability, Cb and tropical revolving storms. The same heat, left behind when cloud rains out over a mountain, is why the lee side of a Föhn ends up warmer (section 3.7). `050.03.02.01.05`

Ice melts above 0 °C. Liquid water freezes at or below 0 °C only if it has a **freezing nucleus** or a surface to freeze on; otherwise it stays liquid as **supercooled** water. Very small, pure droplets freeze spontaneously only at about −40 °C. That is why clouds between 0 and −20 °C hold so much supercooled water, and why that band is the main airframe-icing zone. `050.03.02.01.06`

When water freezes, its molecules lock into an ice lattice, starting from a nucleus or a surface. The latent heat released warms the remaining water back towards 0 °C and slows further freezing. A large drop striking a wing therefore does not freeze at once: it spreads and runs back before it freezes, building clear ice (Chapter 9). `050.03.02.01.07`

Freezing nuclei are particles whose crystal structure resembles ice, such as some clay minerals, silver iodide and certain bacteria. They are scarce and mostly start working only below about −10 to −15 °C, with more becoming active the colder it gets. Their scarcity is why supercooled water is so common between 0 and −20 °C. `050.03.02.01.08`

**Supercooled water** is liquid water below 0 °C. It is unstable and freezes the instant it touches a nucleus or a surface, such as an airframe. Small droplets can stay liquid down to about −40 °C, while large drops usually freeze above about −15 to −20 °C. The most dangerous icing band is 0 to −10 °C, where supercooled water is most plentiful and the drops largest. Below about −40 °C no supercooled droplets remain, so there is no icing from droplets. `050.03.02.01.09`

**Deposition** (vapour to ice) needs air saturated with respect to ice at below-zero temperatures, plus deposition nuclei or a surface below zero. **Sublimation** (ice to vapour) needs ice exposed to air that is dry relative to ice, and a supply of energy. Frost forms whenever a surface falls below the frost point, even if the air at screen height is above 0 °C. `050.03.02.01.10`

In deposition, vapour builds directly into ice crystals on a nucleus or surface, never passing through the liquid stage, and grows feathery, six-sided crystals while releasing about 2834 J/g. Sublimation is the reverse: ice turning straight into vapour in dry air. Hoar frost on a parked aircraft after a clear night is deposition, and so is the frost that forms in flight when a cold-soaked aircraft descends into warm, moist air. `050.03.02.01.11`

Deposition nuclei are even rarer than freezing nuclei and work mainly below about −15 to −20 °C, many only below −30 °C. That is why clouds made purely of ice crystals (Ci, Cs, Cc) appear mainly at very low temperatures and high levels. `050.03.02.01.12`

The latent heat of these changes shows up in the weather. Melting snow absorbs heat and can create an isothermal layer at 0 °C near the freezing level, and in heavy precipitation it can even lower the freezing level. Evaporating rain cools the air beneath a storm and powers its downbursts. `050.03.02.01.13`

Every change of state has an example you will meet:

- **Condensation**: cloud, fog and dew.
- **Evaporation**: a runway drying, virga, fog dispersed by daytime heating.
- **Freezing**: freezing-rain glaze and clear ice.
- **Melting**: snow turning to rain below the freezing level.
- **Deposition**: hoar frost and cirrus.
- **Sublimation**: snow cover shrinking in a cold, dry wind.

Frost is deposition, not frozen dew; frozen dew is a separate case, dew that formed above 0 °C and then froze. `050.03.02.01.14`

### 3.4 Adiabatic cooling and warming

A process is **adiabatic** when temperature changes because air is compressed or expanded, with no heat exchanged with the surroundings. A parcel of air that rises meets lower pressure, expands, does work on the air around it and cools; a parcel that sinks is compressed and warms. Air moves vertically fast enough, compared with how slowly it exchanges heat, for this to be a good description. *Adiabatic* does not mean constant temperature; that is *isothermal*. `050.03.03.01.01`

An unsaturated parcel cools as it rises, and warms as it sinks, at the **dry adiabatic lapse rate**, DALR = 1 °C per 100 m = 3 °C per 1000 ft (9.8 °C per km), for as long as it stays unsaturated. A parcel lifted dry from 20 °C through 3000 ft arrives at 20 − 9 = 11 °C. `050.03.03.01.02`

The amount of water vapour in the parcel, measured as the *mixing ratio* (grams of vapour per kilogram of dry air), stays the same as it moves. As it rises, its temperature falls quickly and its dew point slowly (about 0.2 °C per 100 m), so its relative humidity rises until it saturates at the **condensation level**, which is the cloud base. Sinking air does the opposite and dries out, which is why air subsiding in a high gives clear skies, with haze trapped under the subsidence inversion. `050.03.03.01.03`

A **saturated** parcel also cools as it rises, but condensation releases latent heat inside it, offsetting part of the cooling. Saturated air sinking inside a cloud warms at the same reduced rate, because evaporating droplets take heat back. Once the moisture has rained out, sinking air warms at the full dry rate, which is the heart of the Föhn effect. `050.03.03.01.04`

Saturated air changes temperature at the **saturated adiabatic lapse rate** (SALR). The exam value is **0.6 °C per 100 m, or 1.8 °C per 1000 ft**, at low levels. The SALR is not constant. In warm, moist air, where a lot of vapour condenses, it is small, about 0.4 °C per 100 m; in cold air and at high altitude, where little vapour is left to condense, it approaches the DALR. `050.03.03.01.05`

> **Exam trap.** The SALR is not 1.8 °C per 1000 ft at all levels. It increases with height and with falling temperature, towards the DALR.

### 3.5 Stability

**Stability** asks what happens to a parcel of air pushed upward. Compare the parcel's own lapse rate (DALR if it is dry, SALR if saturated) with the **environmental lapse rate** of the air around it, taken from a sounding. If the displaced parcel ends up colder and denser than its surroundings, it sinks back: the air is **stable**. If it ends up warmer, it keeps rising: the air is **unstable**. If it ends up at the same temperature, it stays where it is: the air is **neutral**. The faster the environment cools with height, the less stable it is. Stability is a property of the environment relative to the parcel, not of the parcel alone. `050.03.03.01.06`

The weather follows. Stable air gives stratiform cloud, steady precipitation, poor visibility and smooth air. Unstable air gives Cu and Cb, showers, good visibility and turbulence.

There are four cases to know:

- **Absolutely stable**: ELR is less than the SALR. This includes isothermal layers and inversions.
- **Conditionally unstable**: ELR lies between the SALR and the DALR. The air is stable while it is dry and becomes unstable once it is saturated.
- **Absolutely unstable**: ELR is greater than the DALR. This happens only just above a strongly heated surface.
- **Neutral** (indifferent): ELR equals the DALR for dry air, or the SALR for saturated air.

`050.03.03.01.07`

![Stability: ELR compared with DALR 3 °C and SALR 1.8 °C per 1000 ft](img/stability.jpg)

> **Exam trap.** An ELR of 0.8 °C per 100 m lies between 0.6 and 1.0, so the air is **conditionally unstable**. So is the ISA lapse rate of 2 °C per 1000 ft. Absolutely unstable lapse rates are rare above the lowest few hundred metres.

> **Worked example · stability.** A sounding shows the temperature falling from 14 °C at the surface to 2 °C at 5000 ft. ELR = 12 °C in 5000 ft = 2.4 °C per 1000 ft. That lies between the SALR (1.8) and the DALR (3): **conditionally unstable**. If surface heating or lifting saturates the air, Cu will build, and with enough depth, Cb.

### 3.6 Advection and stability

Stability changes whenever the lapse rate changes:

- Air warmed from below, or cooled aloft, becomes **less stable**. Cold air flowing over a warm surface, or cold air advected in at upper levels, steepens the ELR and brings showers and Cb. Polar maritime air crossing a warm sea in winter is unstable, showery and has good visibility.
- Air cooled from below, or warmed aloft, becomes **more stable**. Warm air flowing over a cold surface, or warm air advected in aloft, makes the ELR shallower and can create an inversion, bringing stratus, fog and drizzle. Tropical maritime air over cold land or sea is stable, with stratus, drizzle and advection fog.

In the exam, use DALR = 1 °C per 100 m (3 °C per 1000 ft) and SALR = 0.6 °C per 100 m (1.8 °C per 1000 ft). `050.03.03.01.09`

### 3.7 The Föhn effect

When moist air is forced over a mountain range, it climbs the windward side unsaturated, cooling at the DALR, until it reaches its condensation level. From there it climbs inside cloud at the SALR to the crest, and much of its moisture falls as rain or snow, while the latent heat that condensation released stays in the air. On the lee side it descends with no cloud, warming at the DALR all the way down. Because it cooled at the slower saturated rate for part of the climb but warms at the faster dry rate for the whole descent, it arrives **warmer and drier** than it was at the same level on the windward side, and its cloud base is higher because its dew point has fallen. `050.03.03.01.08`

![Foehn worked example: 20 °C windward becomes 28.2 °C in the lee](img/foehn.jpg)

> **Worked example · Föhn.** At sea level on the windward side the temperature is 20 °C and the dew point 12 °C. The ridge crest is at 8000 ft.
>
> 1. Cloud base: spread 8 °C × 400 = **3200 ft**.
> 2. Up to cloud base, dry: 20 − 3 × 3.2 = **10.4 °C**.
> 3. Base to crest in cloud, 4800 ft at 1.8 °C per 1000 ft: 10.4 − 8.6 = **about 1.8 °C** at the crest.
> 4. Crest to sea level on the lee side, dry: 1.8 + 3 × 8 = **about 25.8 °C**.
>
> The air has gained about 6 °C. The gain is always (3 − 1.8) × the depth of cloud climbed, in thousands of feet: 1.2 × 4.8 ≈ 5.8 °C. The figure above uses different starting values and the same method.

The weather on the two sides is very different. The windward side has low cloud, rain, icing, hill fog and hidden terrain. The lee side is clear, warm, dry and gusty, with mountain waves, rotors and lenticular cloud (Chapter 2), and the warm wind can melt snow rapidly. The Föhn of the Alps, the Chinook of the Rockies and the Zonda of the Andes are examples.
