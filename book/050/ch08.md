## Chapter 8 · Climatology

Climatology is the weather you can expect at a place and season before you look at any forecast. It follows from the global circulation of Chapter 2 and its seasonal march with the sun. This chapter covers the climate zones, the tropics (trade winds, the ITCZ, monsoons, squall lines, easterly waves and sandstorms), the typical weather situations of the mid-latitudes, and the best-known local winds.

### 8.1 The general circulation again

The circulation of Chapter 2 sets the climate. At the surface there are the Hadley cells, with ascent in the ITCZ and subsidence in the subtropics; the mid-latitude westerlies, with travelling depressions along the polar front; and the polar easterlies. In the upper troposphere the flow is westerly, with the subtropical and polar front jets, and easterly over the tropics in summer. In the lower stratosphere the winds are westerly in winter, with the strong polar night jet at high levels, and easterly in summer. Climatology questions combine this pattern with its seasonal shift, so learn which belt controls a region in January and in July. `050.08.01.01.01`

### 8.2 Climate zones

There are five main climates:

- The **tropical rain climate** (A) is warm all year, with the coldest month above 18 °C, high humidity and heavy convective rain. It includes rainforest, and savanna and monsoon climates with distinct wet and dry seasons.
- The **dry climate** (B) is where evaporation exceeds precipitation: deserts and steppes, with a large daily temperature range, dust and strong heating.
- The **mid-latitude**, or warm temperate rain, climate (C) has mild winters (coldest month between −3 and 18 °C) and frontal precipitation. It includes the Mediterranean climate, with a dry summer, and the maritime west-coast climate.
- The **subarctic**, or cold snow-forest, climate (D) has cold winters with snow cover (coldest month below −3 °C) and short, warm summers. It covers the continental interiors of North America and Eurasia (the taiga).
- The **snow**, or polar, climate (E) has its warmest month below 10 °C. It includes tundra and ice caps.

Spain is mostly Mediterranean (C, dry summer), with semi-arid (B) areas in the south-east. `050.08.01.02.01`

Because the pressure and wind belts follow the sun, some regions lie in one belt in summer and another in winter, and these form **transitional climates**. The savanna gets ITCZ rains in summer and dry trade winds in winter. The Mediterranean lies under the subtropical high in summer, which makes it dry, and in the westerlies in winter, when polar-front depressions bring rain. Monsoon regions see their wind reverse. `050.08.01.02.02`

### 8.3 Tropical showers and thunderstorms

Tropical showers and thunderstorms need very warm, humid air at the surface, deep conditional instability, a high tropopause (so Cb tops reach FL 500–600), and something to lift the air: convergence (the ITCZ, easterly waves, sea-breeze fronts), mountains or daytime heating. The convection organises into **cloud clusters** hundreds of kilometres across and into **mesoscale convective complexes** (MCCs): long-lived (more than 6 hours), large (a cold cloud shield of over 100 000 km²) and often at their most active at night. Over land the maximum is in the afternoon and evening, over the sea at night and early morning. Cb embedded in huge cloud shields make weather radar essential, and bring a risk of ice-crystal icing (Chapter 9). `050.08.02.01.01`

A **tropical squall line** is an organised line of Cb several hundred kilometres long, moving faster than the mean wind (in West Africa often westward at about 20–30 kt). It arrives with a gust front: a sharp wind shift, gusts of 40–60 kt or more and a sudden drop in temperature, then heavy rain for about an hour, followed by lighter rain from stratiform cloud behind. The "line squalls" of the West African wet season are the classic example. They bring severe turbulence, wind shear, heavy rain and low visibility at aerodromes, cannot be overflown, and are not easy to fly around. `050.08.02.01.02`

The **Intertropical Convergence Zone** (ITCZ) is where the north-east and south-east trade winds meet. The converging moist, unstable air is forced to rise, building bands and clusters of Cu and Cb rather than a continuous wall. Activity is strongest over land in summer and where the convergence is strongest, and weak or broken over some oceans. The ITCZ is not a front: there is no temperature contrast across it, only convergence. `050.08.02.01.03`

In the tropics the surface temperature is typically 25–30 °C (higher inland in the dry season), the dew point 20–25 °C and the relative humidity often above 70–80 %. The **freezing level** stays at about 15 000–16 000 ft (FL 150–160) all year. Airframe icing is therefore confined to higher levels, though tropical Cb carry supercooled water and ice crystals far above that. The hot, humid surface air also means a high density altitude. `050.08.02.01.04`

### 8.4 Tropical winds and pressure belts

The **trade winds** are the tropical easterlies, blowing steadily from the north-east in the northern hemisphere and the south-east in the southern, from the subtropical highs towards the ITCZ, at about 10–20 kt. A **trade-wind inversion**, formed by subsidence at about 5000–8000 ft, caps the convection. Under it lie fair-weather Cu with flat tops and good visibility, and showers fall mainly on the windward slopes of islands. The inversion rises and weakens towards the ITCZ. Below about FL 100 the trades usually give good flying weather, except near windward mountains. `050.08.02.02.01`

The **doldrums** are the equatorial belt of low pressure, mainly over the oceans, within the ITCZ zone. Winds are light and variable, or calm; the air is hot and humid; and Cb with heavy showers and thunderstorms develop, especially in the afternoon and at night. They bring all the hazards of the ITCZ. `050.08.02.02.02`

The **subtropical highs**, at about 25–35° (the *horse latitudes*), lie under the sinking branch of the Hadley cell. Subsidence gives light, variable winds and clear skies, or low Sc under the inversion over the eastern oceans; the air is dry, deserts lie beneath on land, and there is haze and dust. Along coasts with cold currents under these highs (California, Morocco, Namibia, Peru), fog and stratus are common. `050.08.02.02.03`

The major **monsoons** are:

- **South Asia**: the south-west monsoon in June–September (wet) and the north-east monsoon in December–February (dry).
- **West Africa**: the south-west monsoon in summer (wet) and the north-east trade, the Harmattan, in winter (dry and dusty).
- **East Asia**: the south or south-east summer monsoon (wet) and the north or north-west winter monsoon (cold and dry, from the Siberian high).
- **Northern Australia**: the north-west monsoon in December–March (wet) and the south-east trades in winter (dry).

On a July map, the south-west monsoon over India is the one to identify. `050.08.02.02.04`

Over the eastern Atlantic and eastern Pacific the ITCZ stays in the northern hemisphere all year, and its seasonal swing is largest over Africa and Asia. It follows the zone of greatest heating, under the overhead sun, about one to two months behind it, and moves much further over the continents, with their low heat capacity, than over the oceans. Its position decides the wet and dry seasons in the tropics. In West Africa, for example, the ITCZ lies north in July, bringing the wet season and line squalls, and south in January, bringing the Harmattan. `050.08.02.03.02`

At the ITCZ the winds are light and variable where the trades meet. Cu and Cb form lines and clusters, with heavy showers and thunderstorms and tops at FL 450–600. Activity varies along its length, peaking over land in the afternoon and evening and over the sea at night. Its width varies from a narrow line to a broad zone hundreds of kilometres wide. Because it is not continuous, crews choose a crossing point with less activity using satellite pictures and radar. `050.08.02.03.03`

The ITCZ's flight hazards are severe turbulence in Cb; icing above the freezing level (about FL 150), including high concentrations of ice crystals (ice-crystal icing) in and near Cb anvils; hail; lightning; heavy rain, with engine water ingestion and poor visibility; wind shear; and sand and dust in squalls over Africa. Signs of ice-crystal icing include rain on the windscreen at sub-zero temperatures, odd total air temperature readings, and only light radar returns in heavy cloud. `050.08.02.03.04`

### 8.6 Monsoons

A **monsoon** is a seasonal reversal of the prevailing wind, by at least 120°, caused by the different heating of continents and oceans, and bringing wet and dry seasons with it. The main regions are South and South-East Asia, East Asia, West Africa and northern Australia. "Monsoon" means the wind regime, not just the rain. `050.08.02.04.01`

Monsoon winds are trade winds that have crossed the equator. When the ITCZ moves far into the summer hemisphere, the trades of the winter hemisphere follow it across the equator. There the Coriolis force changes direction, so the south-east trades are turned to the right and become the **south-west monsoon** of India and West Africa, and the north-east trades crossing into the southern hemisphere become the **north-west monsoon** of northern Australia. The long sea track makes them very moist and unstable. That is why the Indian summer monsoon is south-westerly. `050.08.02.04.03`

The **wet** (summer) monsoon brings deep, moist, unstable air: extensive Cu, Cb and Ns; heavy, prolonged rain and thunderstorms; low ceilings and visibility; embedded Cb; and flooding. Its onset is often violent. The **dry** (winter) monsoon is a dry offshore flow, with fair weather, haze, dust and, in some areas such as northern India, fog. In the wet monsoon expect contaminated runways, wind shear, engine water ingestion in heavy rain and poor visibility; in the dry monsoon, haze and fog (the winter fog of Delhi, for example). `050.08.02.04.04`

Over **West Africa**, from about June to September the ITCZ lies at about 15–20° N and a moist south-west monsoon flows in from the Gulf of Guinea. This is the rainy season, with westward-moving line squalls and thunderstorms; south of the ITCZ there is low cloud and drizzle near the coast. From about November to March the ITCZ lies near the Guinea coast and the dry north-east Harmattan blows from the Sahara, bringing dust haze, visibility often below 1000 m and little cloud. The hazard is visibility in winter and convection in summer. `050.08.02.04.05`

Over **India**, the south-west monsoon (June–September) is drawn in by the Asian thermal low and the northward ITCZ, bringing moist air from the Arabian Sea and Bay of Bengal. It reaches Kerala in early June and covers India by mid-July, with heavy rain on the west coast (the Western Ghats) and in the north-east, Cb, thunderstorms and monsoon depressions; cyclones form in the Bay of Bengal before and after it. The north-east monsoon (December–February) is a dry outflow from the Siberian high, with fair weather and fog and haze in the north (the Ganges plain); only south-east India and Sri Lanka get rain, from the flow crossing the Bay of Bengal. Delhi therefore sees thunderstorms in the south-west monsoon, dust storms just before it, and dense fog in winter. `050.08.02.04.06`

Over the **Far East**, the summer monsoon blows from the south or south-east and is wet, with the Baiu or Meiyu frontal rains over China and Japan in June and July, and typhoons from July to October. The winter monsoon blows cold and dry from the north or north-west out of the Siberian high; after crossing the Sea of Japan it brings snow to Japan's west coast. The strong winter contrast between continent and ocean is also why the strongest jet streams lie over Japan in winter. Over **northern Australia** the north-west monsoon of December to March is the wet season, with thunderstorms and cyclones, and the south-east trades of April to October bring the dry season. `050.08.02.04.07`

### 8.7 Sandstorms and cold outbreaks

A **sandstorm** or dust storm happens when strong surface winds, above about 15–20 kt, blow over dry, loose sand or dust in unstable, well-mixed air. The wind may come from a Cb outflow (the gust front of a *haboob*), a cold front, a strong pressure gradient, or a local wind such as the Harmattan, Khamsin, Shamal or Ghibli. Dust can rise to FL 100–150 and travel thousands of kilometres. METARs report DS (dust storm), SS (sandstorm), BLSA or BLDU (blowing sand or dust), and +DS or +SS when visibility is below 200 m. The hazards are visibility below 1000 m, abrasion, engine erosion, blocked pitot and static ports, and walls of dust arriving without the warning of lightning. `050.08.02.04.08`

In winter, cold polar air can surge a long way towards the equator behind strong cold fronts, especially along the eastern sides of continents and across open plains. Such **outbreaks** reach the Gulf of Mexico and Central America (the "northers"), the South American pampas, the South China Sea and northern India. They bring sharp falls in temperature, strong winds and sometimes snow at low latitudes, with gusty winds, low-level turbulence, icing and snow where pilots do not normally expect them. `050.08.02.04.09`

Two outbreaks have names. The **blizzard** is a North American winter storm behind a deep low or cold front, with strong winds (30–35 kt or more), heavy falling or blowing snow, visibility of 400 m or less, and severe cold. The **pampero** is a cold, strong south-westerly wind over the pampas of Argentina and Uruguay behind a cold front, often arriving as a squall line with thunderstorms and a sharp drop in temperature. Pampero is South America; blizzard is North America. `050.08.02.04.10`

### 8.8 Easterly waves

**Easterly waves** are wave-shaped troughs in the trade-wind easterlies. They move westward at about 10–15 kt, with a wavelength of about 2000–4000 km and a period of 3–4 days. Ahead of the trough axis (to its west) the air diverges and the weather is fair. Behind it (to its east) the air converges, the trade inversion lifts, and deep Cb, showers and thunderstorms develop. Many North Atlantic hurricanes grow from easterly waves that leave Africa. Note that the worst weather lies behind the axis, the opposite of what you would expect from a mid-latitude trough. `050.08.02.05.01`

### 8.9 Typical mid-latitude situations

In a **westerly situation** a strong west-to-east flow crosses the mid-latitudes, and a family of frontal depressions forms on the polar front and runs quickly east at about 25–40 kt, separated by short-lived ridges. The surface chart shows closely spaced isobars running roughly west to east, with several lows in a row from the Atlantic to Scandinavia. The weather changes every day or two (frontal rain, then showers, then a brief ridge), winds are strong, and eastbound flights get good tailwinds. `050.08.03.01.01`

**High-pressure** situations include the Azores high extending ridges over Europe, continental winter highs, and blocking highs. All bring subsidence, light winds and dry air: in summer, sun, heat, haze and isolated thunderstorms at the edges; in winter, fog, low stratus, frost and inversions trapping pollution (Chapter 7 describes warm and cold highs in detail). `050.08.03.02.01`

On a chart, a high appears as closed isobars with pressure rising towards the centre (marked H), widely spaced isobars, U-shaped ridges extending from it, clockwise circulation in the northern hemisphere, and usually no fronts near its centre. Combine the chart with station reports: a small temperature–dew point spread and calm wind under a winter high mean a fog forecast. `050.08.03.02.02`

### 8.10 The cold-air drop

A **cold-air drop**, also called a cold pool or cut-off low (in Spain, a DANA, *depresión aislada en niveles altos*), is an isolated pool of cold air in the middle and upper troposphere, with a closed cyclonic circulation aloft, cut off from the main westerly flow. It often has little or no sign on the surface chart. `050.08.03.04.01`

It forms when an upper trough in a strongly meandering westerly flow deepens and stretches towards the equator, and its southern end is cut off as the flow to the north reconnects. The cold-cored low then drifts slowly and erratically, often for several days, sometimes even westward, until it is reabsorbed or fills. Its movement is slow and hard to forecast. `050.08.03.04.02`

On upper-air charts (500 or 300 hPa) a cold-air drop shows as closed low contours with a cold centre (closed isotherms), separated from the westerly belt. On the surface chart it may show only as a weak low, a trough, or even slack or high pressure. On satellite images it appears as a comma or spiral of convective cloud. Always check the upper charts, because the surface chart alone can look harmless. `050.08.03.04.03`

Cold air aloft over a warm surface, especially a warm sea such as the Mediterranean in autumn, makes the atmosphere strongly unstable. The result is severe Cb, hail, heavy and persistent rain (the autumn flash floods of Spain's Mediterranean coast), icing and turbulence, in an area of bad weather that moves slowly and unpredictably. Forecasts are unreliable, and severe weather can occur with no front at all, so carry extra fuel and choose alternates outside the area. `050.08.03.04.04`

### 8.11 Local winds

The **Föhn** mechanism was worked through in Chapter 3. Moist air climbs the windward side, cooling at the DALR and then at the SALR in cloud while precipitation falls, and descends the lee side dry at the DALR, arriving warmer and drier than at the same level to windward. A second mechanism contributes: when the low-level air on the windward side is blocked by the mountains, the air descending in the lee comes from higher up, where it was potentially warmer. The **Chinook** is the Föhn east of the Rocky Mountains; the **Zonda** (Andes) and the **berg wind** (South Africa) are others. A Chinook can raise the temperature by 20 °C in a few hours and melt snow rapidly, earning its name "snow eater". `050.08.04.01.01`

On the windward side of a Föhn there is extensive cloud and precipitation and the mountains are hidden behind the Föhn wall. In the lee the wind is warm, dry and gusty, the humidity low and the visibility excellent, with a clear Föhn gap in the cloud and lenticular clouds above. Lee aerodromes get strong, gusty crosswinds, wind shear and severe turbulence from mountain waves and rotors, despite clear skies. `050.08.04.01.02`

The **Mistral** is a cold, dry, strong north or north-west wind that blows down the Rhône valley into the Gulf of Lion. It is funnelled between the Alps and the Massif Central (a Venturi effect) and is partly katabatic. It blows when cold air pours in behind a cold front, with high pressure over France or Biscay and low pressure over the Gulf of Genoa. Speeds are often 30–50 kt with gusts of 60–80 kt or more; skies are clear, visibility excellent, and it extends well out to sea. The **Tramontane** of Languedoc and Catalonia is a related wind. The **Bora** is a cold, dry, very gusty north-east katabatic wind that falls from the Dinaric Alps onto the Adriatic coast (Trieste and Croatia), when cold air pools over the Balkan plateau with low pressure over the Adriatic. Its gusts can exceed 100 kt, and the sky is often cloudless or has a cap cloud over the mountains. Both bring severe low-level turbulence, strong crosswinds and wind shear, and ice ships with sea spray; Marseille, Montpellier, Split and Trieste are among the aerodromes affected. Mistral is the Rhône and France; Bora is the Adriatic and Croatia. `050.08.04.01.03`

The **Harmattan** is the dry, dusty north-east trade wind that blows from the Sahara over West Africa in the dry season, about November to March, when the ITCZ lies near the Gulf of Guinea. It carries fine dust, sometimes up to FL 100–150, making an extensive haze that can last for days. Visibility is often 1000–3000 m and at times below 1000 m over large areas, slant visibility on approach is very poor, and the METAR shows HZ or DU. There is little cloud: the hazard is visibility, not convection. `050.08.04.02.01`
