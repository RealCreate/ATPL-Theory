## Chapter 10 · Meteorological Information

The last chapter turns from the weather itself to the information about it: how it is observed at the surface, aloft, from satellites and by radar; how it is drawn on charts; the coded messages pilots read every day; and the organisations that produce them. It ends with a decoding self-test. In the exam, many 050 figures are SWC charts, upper wind and temperature charts, surface charts and coded METAR or TAF extracts, so practise reading them.

### 10.1 Wind and gusts

A **gust** is a brief increase in wind speed. In a METAR, a gust is reported (G after the mean speed) when the highest 3-second wind in the last 10 minutes exceeds the mean by 10 kt or more, as in 24018G32KT. With a smaller difference, there is no G group. Many operators add half the gust increment to the approach speed, within limits. `050.10.01.01.01`

The **METAR wind** is a 10-minute mean, with its direction in degrees true, representative of the aerodrome or its main runway. The **tower wind**, given by ATC or the ATIS for take-off and landing, is a 2-minute mean (with gusts over 10 minutes), with its direction in degrees magnetic, from sensors at the touchdown or departure zone of the runway in use. Where the magnetic variation is large, the two directions differ noticeably. `050.10.01.01.02`

### 10.2 Visibility

**Visibility** for aeronautical purposes is the greater of two distances: the greatest distance at which a black object of suitable size near the ground can be seen and recognised against a bright background; and the greatest distance at which lights of about 1000 candelas can be seen and identified against an unlit background. At night, visibility is therefore judged by lights, and can differ from the day value for the same air. `050.10.01.01.03`

It is measured either by an observer, using markers (objects or lights at known distances around the aerodrome), or by instruments (forward-scatter meters or transmissometers) that measure how much the air dims light, the *meteorological optical range* (MOR), which is converted to visibility. An automatic (AUTO) METAR's visibility comes from sensors that sample only a small volume of air. `050.10.01.01.04`

**Prevailing visibility** is the visibility reached or exceeded within at least half the horizon circle, or within at least half the aerodrome's surface; those areas need not be adjoining. It is the main visibility in the METAR. If the visibility in one direction is below 1500 m, or below half the prevailing value, the lowest visibility and its direction are also reported, as in 4000 1200SW. Prevailing visibility is not the minimum visibility. `050.10.01.01.05`

**Ground visibility** is the visibility at an aerodrome reported by an accredited observer or an automatic system. It is what VFR take-off and landing are based on, as opposed to *flight visibility*, seen from the cockpit in flight. `050.10.01.01.06`

Visibility is reported in metres: in steps of 50 m below 800 m, 100 m up to 5000 m, and 1000 m up to 9999, which means 10 km or more. Plain-language reports use kilometres, and the USA and Canada use statute miles (SM), as in 1/2SM or 10SM. **CAVOK** replaces visibility, weather and cloud when the visibility is 10 km or more, there is no cloud below 5000 ft (or the highest minimum sector altitude, if higher) and no CB or TCU, and there is no significant weather. `050.10.01.01.07`

### 10.3 Runway visual range

**Runway visual range** (RVR) is the range over which the pilot of an aircraft on the runway centre line can see the runway surface markings or the lights that outline the runway or mark its centre line. It is the controlling value for low-visibility approach and take-off minima. `050.10.01.01.08`

Instruments (transmissometers or forward-scatter meters) measure how much the air dims light, and the RVR is calculated by combining that with the intensity setting of the runway lights and the background brightness (day or night). It is reported when the visibility or the RVR is below 1500 m. At night, bright runway lights can make the RVR higher than the meteorological visibility. `050.10.01.01.09`

The sensors stand beside the runway, no more than about 120 m from the centre line, at about 2.5 m height (a pilot's eye height). They are placed at the **touchdown zone** (TDZ, about 300 m from the threshold), the **mid-point** (MID) and the **stop end** (END, about 300 m from the end). The operator's minima say which of these values are controlling, usually the TDZ, with MID and END for some operations. `050.10.01.01.10`

RVR is given in metres in Europe and in feet in the USA. In a METAR, R24/0550U means an RVR of 550 m on runway 24, tending upward; D means downward and N no change. A P before the value means more than the highest value the system can assess (P2000, more than 2000 m; older texts show P1500), and M means less than the lowest (M0050, below 50 m). R24/0550V0800 means the RVR is varying significantly between 550 and 800 m. `050.10.01.01.11`

Pilots receive RVR four ways: in the METAR or SPECI (a 10-minute mean with its tendency); in the local MET REPORT or SPECIAL (1-minute values); on the ATIS; and from ATC by radio, with current values for TDZ, MID and END. On approach, the latest RVR from ATC is the one used for the approach ban. `050.10.01.01.12`

So, compared: **ground visibility** is the general visibility at the aerodrome; **prevailing visibility** is the value reached in at least half the horizon or aerodrome; **RVR** is measured along a particular runway, from a pilot's eye position, at set points, and allows for the runway lights. Where no RVR is available, some approaches may use a converted meteorological visibility (CMV), according to the operator's rules. `050.10.01.01.13`

### 10.4 Weather and cloud

**Present weather** is observed by people (precipitation type and intensity, thunderstorms, obscurations) and by automatic sensors: present-weather sensors (forward scatter combined with precipitation detection), precipitation gauges, lightning detectors, ceilometers and visibility sensors. An AUTO METAR may miss some phenomena, such as TCU or small hail; an unknown element is shown as //. `050.10.01.01.14`

**Cloud** type and amount are judged by an observer or from satellite pictures. The cloud base is measured by a **ceilometer** (a laser or light pulse, timed on its return), at night by a cloud-base searchlight with an alidade, by ceiling balloons, or from pilot reports. Cloud tops come from pilot reports, satellite infrared temperatures, radiosondes and radar. METAR cloud bases are heights above aerodrome elevation; tops come only from forecasts or pilot reports. `050.10.01.01.15`

Only **CB and TCU** are named by type in METARs and TAFs (as in SCT025CB), and SIGMETs refer to CB and thunderstorms (as OBSC, EMBD, FRQ or SQL). All other cloud is described only by amount and base. NSC means no significant cloud; NCD, in an AUTO report, means no cloud detected. `050.10.01.01.16`

Cloud amount is measured in **oktas**, eighths of the sky covered: FEW is 1–2 oktas, SCT 3–4, BKN 5–7 and OVC 8. BKN therefore starts at 5 oktas, more than half the sky. `050.10.01.01.17`

The **cloud base** is the lowest level at which the obscuration changes perceptibly from clear air or haze to water droplets or ice crystals. It is reported as a height above aerodrome elevation. In fog or heavy snow, it is replaced by the vertical visibility. `050.10.01.01.18`

The **ceiling** is the height above the ground or water of the base of the lowest layer of cloud below 20 000 ft covering more than half the sky, that is BKN or OVC. A SCT layer never forms a ceiling, and vertical visibility is treated as one. `050.10.01.01.19`

Cloud bases are given in **feet**, in hundreds in METARs and TAFs (BKN008 is 800 ft), above aerodrome elevation (or above threshold elevation for precision runways where specified, and above sea level for offshore structures). Compare them with decision heights and minimum descent heights, which are above the threshold or aerodrome, not with decision altitudes and minimum descent altitudes, which are above sea level. `050.10.01.01.20`

**Vertical visibility** (VV) is the greatest distance an observer can see vertically into an obscuring medium such as fog, heavy snow or sand. It is reported when the sky is obscured and no cloud base can be determined: VV002 is 200 ft, and VV/// means the sky is obscured but the value is not available. `050.10.01.01.21`

It is measured by the ceilometer, as the depth its laser penetrates into the obscuration, or estimated by the observer, whenever the sky is obscured by fog or heavy precipitation. VV appears instead of a cloud group, never together with one. `050.10.01.01.22`

It is given in hundreds of feet in METARs and TAFs (VVhhh, so VV001 is 100 ft), and in metres in some local reports. `050.10.01.01.23`

### 10.5 Temperature and humidity

Air temperature is measured by thermometers (electrical resistance thermometers, thermistors, or mercury and alcohol thermometers) in a ventilated, shaded screen 1.25–2 m above the ground, with maximum and minimum thermometers alongside. Aircraft derive the static air temperature from their TAT probes. METAR temperatures are rounded to whole degrees, with M for minus (M02). `050.10.01.01.24`

Relative humidity is given in per cent and the dew point in °C (°F in the USA). They are measured by hygrometers (capacitive sensors) or psychrometers (wet- and dry-bulb thermometers). The METAR gives temperature and dew point, not relative humidity; estimate RH from the spread (Chapter 3). `050.10.01.01.25`

### 10.6 Radiosondes

A **radiosonde** is a small instrument package carried up by a hydrogen or helium balloon, rising at about 5 m/s. It measures pressure, temperature and humidity and radios them to the ground; the wind is found by tracking the balloon (by GPS or radar, a *rawinsonde*). The balloon bursts at about 25–35 km. Standard launches are made at 00 and 12 UTC worldwide, and their data feed upper-air charts, numerical models and stability diagrams. `050.10.01.02.01`

A sounding is plotted as temperature and dew point against pressure (height). From it you can read the ELR and so the stability, by comparing its slope with the DALR and SALR; inversions and isothermal layers; **cloud layers**, where temperature and dew point coincide or nearly so (spread 1–2 °C or less); dry layers, where the spread is large; the **freezing level**, where the temperature curve crosses 0 °C; and the tropopause, where the lapse rate changes to near-isothermal. For icing, look for cloud layers (T close to Td) between 0 and −20 °C. An inversion with moist air beneath it means stratus, fog or haze; with dry air beneath, a clear but hazy sky. `050.10.01.02.02`

### 10.7 Satellites

**Geostationary** satellites orbit about 36 000 km above the equator (Meteosat, GOES, Himawari). Each has a fixed view of one hemisphere and sends images every 5–15 minutes, but resolution is poor near the poles, beyond about 60–70°. Loops of their images show how systems move and develop. **Polar-orbiting** satellites fly at about 800–850 km (NOAA, MetOp). They give high resolution and global coverage, including the poles, but see each area only about twice a day. `050.10.01.03.01`

Satellite pictures are used to locate and track fronts, depressions, jet streams, Cb and convective systems, tropical cyclones, fog and low stratus, volcanic ash and dust, mountain waves (from lee-wave cloud) and icing-prone areas; to estimate cloud-top heights from infrared temperatures; and as input to numerical models and short-range forecasts, especially over oceans and other areas with few observations. Satellites cannot see CAT in clear air. `050.10.01.03.02`

There are three main types of image:

- **Visible** (VIS) images show reflected sunlight, so they work only by day. Brightness shows albedo: thick, water-rich cloud and snow are bright, and the texture and shadows of cloud tops are visible.
- **Infrared** (IR) images show emitted heat, so they work day and night. Brightness shows coldness: high, cold tops are white, while warm surfaces and low cloud are grey or dark.
- **Water vapour** (WV) images show emission from moisture in the middle and upper troposphere (wavelengths of about 6.2–7.3 µm). They show the upper-level flow, dry intrusions and jet streams, but cannot see the surface.

Fog and low stratus are therefore bright on VIS but dark or grey (warm) on IR, so use VIS by day or special fog products at night; a Cb is bright on both. `050.10.01.03.03`

**Stratiform** cloud looks smooth and uniform, with sharp, straight edges (fog and stratus often follow coasts and terrain), and is uniform grey on IR. **Cumuliform** cloud looks lumpy, cellular and mottled, with shadows on VIS. A Cb appears bright and rounded, often with an anvil plume and an overshooting top. Polar air over a warm sea shows **open cells** (rings of Cu around clear centres), while Sc under an inversion shows **closed cells**; open cellular Cu behind a cold front means cold, unstable air and showers. `050.10.01.03.04`

**Fronts** appear as long, continuous bands of cloud: a cold front as a narrow band with a sharp rear edge, a warm front as a broad shield of layered cloud ahead of the surface front, and an occlusion as a spiral or comma head around the low centre, with the triple point where they meet. The surface cold front usually lies along the rear, cold-air edge of its cloud band. `050.10.01.03.05`

**Atmospheric motion vectors** (AMVs) are wind estimates made by tracking cloud or water-vapour features between successive images and assigning them a height. Bands of high-speed vectors at upper levels show the jet axis. In the images themselves, a jet is also marked by sharp boundaries of moisture (on WV) and by cirrus edges and streaks parallel to the flow; it often runs along the poleward edge of a frontal cirrus shield. `050.10.01.03.06`

### 10.8 Weather radar

A **ground weather radar** transmits pulses of microwaves (in the S or C band) and measures the energy reflected by precipitation. The *reflectivity*, in dBZ, relates to the precipitation intensity (it rises with the sixth power of drop diameter), and the delay of the echo gives the range. **Doppler** radars also measure the speed of particles towards or away from the radar, revealing wind, wind shear and mesocyclones; **dual-polarisation** radars can tell hail, rain and snow apart. Networks of radars produce composite images. Radar shows precipitation, not cloud: cloud and fog that are not precipitating are invisible to it. `050.10.01.04.01`

On radar images, a colour scale shows reflectivity or rain rate: weak (blue or green), moderate (yellow), heavy (orange or red) and extreme (purple or magenta, which means hail). Convective cells appear as small, intense cores with strong gradients, stratiform rain as wide, uniform areas, squall lines and cold fronts as lines, and **bow echoes** warn of damaging winds. Animations show movement. Composite images may be several minutes old, so do not use them for close-in avoidance. `050.10.01.04.02`

**Airborne weather radar** is a nose-mounted X-band pulse radar (about 9.3 GHz, 3 cm wavelength) with a pencil beam about 3–5° wide, scanning ahead through ±60–90°. It shows precipitation intensity in colour levels: green, yellow, red and magenta. Its controls are range, **tilt** (the beam's elevation) and **gain**; its modes include WX (weather), WX+T (turbulence, by Doppler) and MAP (ground mapping), and some systems give predictive wind-shear alerts. Use the tilt to scan up and down; at high altitude, tilt down to see the more reflective lower parts of cells. `050.10.01.04.03`

The airborne radar has limits:

- It detects **liquid water** best. Dry hail, snow and ice crystals reflect weakly, and CAT and dry turbulence are invisible (except to a Doppler turbulence mode, in rain).
- **Attenuation**: heavy rain absorbs the beam, so cells behind it look weaker or vanish (a radar shadow). No return behind a strong echo does not mean no weather.
- **Beam spreading**: at long range the beam is wide and can pass over or under cells, and small cells are under-represented.
- **Wrong tilt**: the beam can overscan the tops of cells, giving weak echoes at high altitude, or pick up ground clutter.
- **Delay and range**: the display lags slightly and the range is limited.

`050.10.01.04.04`

On the display, red or magenta means heavy rain or hail: avoid it by at least 20 nm. Shapes that warn of severe turbulence and hail are **hooks**, **fingers** (protrusions), **scalloped edges**, **U-shapes** or crescents, shapes that change rapidly, and steep reflectivity gradients. Uniform green means stratiform rain. Deviate upwind, well clear of edges with steep gradients. `050.10.01.04.05`

### 10.9 Air-reports

A **routine air-report** (AIREP) gives position, time, flight level, wind and temperature (and sometimes more), made at designated reporting points or intervals; today it is mostly sent automatically by datalink (ADS-C, AMDAR). A **special air-report** (ARS) is made whenever certain hazardous phenomena are met or seen, by voice or datalink, and can trigger a SIGMET. `050.10.01.05.01`

Pilots **must** make special air-reports when they meet or observe the specified conditions, such as severe turbulence or severe icing, and routine reports where the air navigation services or operations require them. Report as soon as practicable to the ATS unit, with position, time, level and the phenomenon. `050.10.01.05.02`

A special air-report is made for: moderate or severe turbulence; moderate or severe icing; severe mountain wave; thunderstorms, with or without hail, that are obscured, embedded, widespread or in squall lines; a heavy dust storm or sandstorm; a volcanic ash cloud; and pre-eruption volcanic activity or an eruption. (For supersonic flight, moderate turbulence, hail and Cb in the transonic and supersonic phases are added.) The standard abbreviations include SEV TURB, MOD ICE, SEV MTW, TS, TSGR, HVY DS and VA CLD. `050.10.01.05.03`

### 10.10 Significant weather charts

**Significant weather** (SWC or SIGWX) charts are forecasts valid at a fixed time. The World Area Forecast Centres issue the high- and medium-level charts, and national MET offices the low-level ones. They show:

- **jet streams** of 80 kt or more: an arrow along the axis, wind barbs and flags, the flight level of the core, and sometimes the vertical extent of the 80 kt isotach (as in FL 270/390);
- **tropopause** heights, as flight levels in boxes, with H and L centres;
- **CB areas**, inside scalloped lines, described as ISOL (isolated), OCNL (occasional, well separated), FRQ (frequent, with little or no separation) or EMBD (embedded in layer cloud), with base and top. XXX means a base or top outside the chart's layer. A CB on the chart implies hail and moderate or severe icing and turbulence;
- **turbulence and CAT areas**, inside dashed lines, with intensity and vertical extent, plus icing areas and mountain waves;
- **fronts** with their speed and direction, tropical cyclones, volcanoes (with an eruption symbol), sandstorms and sites of radioactive release;
- on **low-level** charts, cloud layers (amount, type, base and top), the freezing level (0 °C: FL), visibility and weather.

![Reading a high-level SIGWX chart: jet stream, CAT area, CB area, tropopause heights, volcano and front (example only)](img/m-swc.jpg)

The high-level chart covers FL 250–630, the medium-level chart FL 100–450 (regional), and the low-level chart the surface to FL 100 (FL 150 in mountainous areas, up to FL 245 for some products). Typical exam figures ask where turbulence is absent at FL 340, what cloud there is at FL 160 over a city, or which is the best level on a route: read the areas, their vertical limits and the jet core's level. `050.10.02.01.01`

To describe the conditions along a route at a given level, draw the route on the chart and check, for your flight level: whether you are inside a CB area, and whether its tops are above your level; whether you are inside a CAT or turbulence area whose vertical range includes your level (MOD 300/400 affects FL 300 to FL 400 only); how close the jet core is and at what level, remembering the shear on its cold side; whether the tropopause is above or below you; and, on low-level charts, the icing areas, freezing level and fronts you will cross, with their speed. Choose a level that avoids CAT areas and stays above or clear of CB tops where possible. `050.10.02.01.02`

### 10.11 Surface charts

On a **surface chart** you should recognise ridges (U-shaped isobars extending from a high), cols (between two highs and two lows), troughs (V-shaped isobars extending from a low), and fronts: warm (red semicircles), cold (blue triangles), occluded (purple, alternating), stationary (red semicircles and blue triangles on opposite sides) and upper fronts (open symbols). You should also recognise the frontal side (ahead of the warm front), the warm sector and the rear side (behind the cold front) of a mid-latitude low, and highs and lows with their central pressures. An **analysis** shows the observed state at a given time; a **prognostic** (forecast) chart shows the expected state, often with arrows for movement. `050.10.02.02.01`

To find the **wind** from a surface chart, take the tangent to the isobars with low pressure on the left (northern hemisphere): this is the geostrophic or gradient wind at about 2000 ft. The surface wind is backed by about 30° over land, or 10° over the sea, towards low pressure. The speed comes from the isobar spacing, using the geostrophic wind scale for the chart's latitude (closer isobars, stronger wind), reduced to about 50 % over land or 70 % over the sea at the surface; it is lighter round lows and stronger round highs. Compare spacing only at similar latitudes. `050.10.02.02.02`

### 10.12 Upper-air charts

A **constant-pressure chart** shows conditions on a surface of constant pressure, such as 850, 700, 500, 300, 250 or 200 hPa: contours of the height of that surface (isohypses), isotherms, wind barbs and sometimes humidity. The height contours play the part of isobars, and the wind blows along them. The approximate flight levels are 850 hPa ≈ FL 050, 700 ≈ FL 100, 500 ≈ FL 180, 300 ≈ FL 300, 250 ≈ FL 340 and 200 ≈ FL 390. `050.10.02.03.01`

An **isohypse**, or contour line, joins points where a pressure surface lies at the same geopotential height; on a 500 hPa chart, 552 means 5520 geopotential metres. Low heights mean low pressure aloft and cold air in the column; high heights mean warm air and high pressure. The geostrophic wind on upper charts blows along the contours, with low heights on the left (northern hemisphere). `050.10.02.03.02`

An **isotherm** joins points of equal temperature on a level or pressure surface. Closely packed isotherms mark a frontal zone, a strong thermal wind, a jet stream above, and a CAT risk; the 0 °C isotherm marks the freezing level. `050.10.02.03.03`

An **isotach** joins points of equal wind speed. Isotachs show jet cores and wind-speed maxima, and tightly packed isotachs mean strong horizontal shear and CAT. `050.10.02.03.04`

The World Area Forecast System issues **forecast upper wind and temperature charts** for standard flight levels (such as FL 050, 100, 180, 240, 300, 340, 390 and 450), valid at fixed times (such as 00, 06, 12 and 18 UTC). They show wind arrows or barbs, with direction in degrees true and speed in knots (a half barb is 5 kt, a full barb 10 kt and a pennant 50 kt), and temperatures at grid points in °C. At high levels the temperatures are printed **without a minus sign**, because they are all negative; a positive value is marked + or PS. `050.10.02.03.05`

To find spot or average values along a route, read the grid values near the route at the right flight level, interpolating linearly between charts for intermediate levels and between points along the route. Average the temperature, and average the wind as a vector (or as its component along the track). Then the ISA deviation is the average temperature minus the ISA temperature for that **flight level**, never for the true altitude. Average directions and speeds separately only when the directions are similar; otherwise use components. `050.10.02.03.06`

> **Worked example · route temperature.** At FL 300 the temperatures along a route read −40, −44 and −48 °C, a mean of −44 °C. ISA at FL 300 is 15 − 60 = −45 °C, so the route is **ISA +1**.

### 10.13 Gridded forecasts

**Numerical weather prediction** represents the atmosphere on a three-dimensional grid: horizontal points of latitude and longitude, from a few to tens of kilometres apart, and vertical levels of height or pressure. The model solves the equations of motion, heat and moisture at every point, step by step in time. Phenomena smaller than the grid, such as a single Cb or a patch of CAT, cannot be resolved and must be estimated (parameterised). `050.10.02.04.01`

The two **World Area Forecast Centres**, in London and Washington, produce global gridded forecasts of upper wind, temperature and humidity at standard levels, which flight-planning systems use directly. Flight plans are computed from this WAFS data (in GRIB format), and the SWC charts also come from the WAFCs. `050.10.02.04.02`

The WAFCs also grid the flight level and temperature of the tropopause; the direction, speed and flight level of the maximum wind; the horizontal extent, base and top of CB cloud; icing potential; CAT and in-cloud turbulence potential; and the geopotential altitude of flight levels. These grids are the basis of the hazard layers in electronic flight bags and planning tools. `050.10.02.04.03`

Gridded CB and turbulence data can be displayed as hazard areas on route maps and vertical cross-sections, so crews can see where along the route, and at which levels, hazards are forecast, and choose route and level strategically. `050.10.02.04.04`

Information-processing systems merge these forecast grids with real-time data from aircraft (automated turbulence reports of eddy dissipation rate, AMDAR, pilot reports) and other observations, giving dispatchers and crews (on the EFB) an updated and more accurate picture of the hazards. Real-time reports correct the forecast where it has gone wrong. `050.10.02.04.05`

### 10.14 Aviation weather messages

The main messages are:

- **METAR**: the routine aerodrome observation, every 30 or 60 minutes. Its groups come in this order: type, ICAO identifier, day and time (Z), AUTO if automatic, wind (dddffGfmfmKT, VRB or dddVddd), visibility (with the minimum and its direction), RVR, present weather (intensity or proximity, descriptor, phenomenon), cloud (FEW, SCT, BKN or OVC with height, and CB or TCU, or VV, NSC or NCD), temperature and dew point, QNH (Q in hPa or A in inHg), supplementary information (RE for recent weather, WS for wind shear), runway state, and TREND. CAVOK replaces visibility, RVR, weather and cloud when conditions allow.
- **SPECI**: a special observation issued when specified changes occur between routine METARs.
- **TREND**: a two-hour forecast added to the METAR, using BECMG (becoming) and TEMPO (temporary fluctuations, each lasting less than an hour and together less than half the period), with FM, TL or AT times. NOSIG means no significant change.
- **TAF**: the aerodrome forecast, valid for 9–30 hours (as in 2412/2518). Its change groups are FM (from; a rapid change), BECMG (a gradual change during the period), TEMPO, and PROB30 or PROB40 (probability, alone or with TEMPO). TX and TN give the maximum and minimum temperatures, AMD marks an amended TAF and COR a corrected one.
- **SIGMET**: a warning of hazardous en-route phenomena for all aircraft, of three kinds: WS for weather, WV for volcanic ash and WC for tropical cyclones. It is valid for up to 4 hours, or 6 hours for volcanic ash and tropical cyclones.
- **AIRMET**: a warning of hazards for low-level flights below FL 100 (FL 150 in mountains) not already covered by the GAMET.
- **GAMET**: an area forecast for low-level flights in abbreviated plain language, in two sections: I, hazards, and II, general information.
- **ARS**: the special air-report.
- **Volcanic ash advisory**: a VAAC message or graphic giving the ash cloud's position and levels now and at +6, +12 and +18 hours.

`050.10.03.01.01`

> **Exam trap.** TEMPO in a TAF is not the prevailing condition, and a BECMG change is complete by the end of its period. PROB on its own is only ever 30 or 40 %. In a METAR, 9999 means 10 km or more, and cloud heights are in hundreds of feet above the aerodrome.

**Tropical cyclone advisories** are issued by the Tropical Cyclone Advisory Centres (such as Miami, Tokyo, New Delhi, La Réunion, Darwin, Honolulu and Nadi). They give the storm's name, its observed position and time, its movement (direction and speed), central pressure and maximum surface wind, forecast positions and winds at +6, +12, +18 and +24 hours, remarks, and the time of the next advisory. The graphical version shows the track and the CB area. The MWO uses the advisory to issue the tropical cyclone SIGMET (WC). `050.10.03.01.02`

The **MET REPORT** and **SPECIAL** are the local routine and special reports, in abbreviated plain language, for use at the aerodrome: on the ATIS, by the tower, and for departing and arriving aircraft. They use the 2-minute wind (magnetic for the ATIS) and runway-specific values (RVR by zone, wind by runway end), and are issued when conditions change. The ATIS is built from the MET REPORT, not the METAR. `050.10.03.01.03`

A **SIGMET** (for subsonic flight) is issued for: thunderstorms that are obscured, embedded, frequent or in a squall line (OBSC, EMBD, FRQ or SQL TS), with or without hail (TSGR); tropical cyclones; severe turbulence; severe icing (including freezing rain); severe mountain wave; heavy dust storms and sandstorms; volcanic ash; and radioactive cloud. An **AIRMET** (below FL 100, or FL 150 in mountains, for phenomena not already in a GAMET) is issued for: surface wind above 30 kt; widespread surface visibility below 5000 m; isolated or occasional thunderstorms, with or without hail; mountain obscuration; BKN or OVC cloud with a base below 1000 ft above ground; isolated, occasional or frequent CB or TCU; moderate icing; moderate turbulence; and moderate mountain wave. So isolated and occasional thunderstorms go in an AIRMET; frequent, embedded, obscured and squall-line thunderstorms in a SIGMET. `050.10.03.01.04`

The **runway state group** in a METAR has the form R, runway designator, /, then deposit (1 digit), extent (1 digit), depth (2 digits) and friction or braking action (2 digits):

> **Key numbers · runway state group** (decode with the code table)
>
> | Element | Codes |
> | --- | --- |
> | Deposit | 0 clear and dry · 1 damp · 2 wet · 3 rime or frost · 4 dry snow · 5 wet snow · 6 slush · 7 ice · 8 compacted snow · 9 frozen ruts |
> | Extent | 1 = 10 % or less · 2 = 11–25 % · 5 = 26–50 % · 9 = 51–100 % |
> | Depth | 00–90 = depth in mm · 92–98 = 10–40 cm · 99 = runway not operational |
> | Friction | Two digits = coefficient (45 = 0.45) · 95 good · 94 medium to good · 93 medium · 92 medium to poor · 91 poor · 99 unreliable |
>
> Special forms: CLRD (contamination cleared) and SNOCLO (aerodrome closed by snow).
>
> **Worked example.** R27/290155: runway 27; deposit 2, wet; extent 9, 51–100 %; depth 01, 1 mm; friction 55, a coefficient of 0.55.
>
> `050.10.03.01.05`

Since November 2021, Europe reports runway condition with the Global Reporting Format (the runway condition report and SNOWTAM), but the exam still tests the METAR code, with ICAO Doc 7754 as its reference.

### 10.15 Broadcasts

**VOLMET** is a continuous VHF broadcast of the current METAR or SPECI and TREND (and sometimes TAF and SIGMET) for a group of aerodromes; it is also available by datalink (D-VOLMET). The **ATIS** is an automatic, recorded broadcast for one aerodrome, giving its identifier letter, time, runway in use, approach type, surface wind, visibility or RVR, weather, cloud, temperature and dew point, QNH (and QFE), transition level, wind shear, and NOTAM or operational information. It is updated with each new MET REPORT and is also available by datalink (D-ATIS). ATIS winds are magnetic, from the local report; VOLMET relays METARs, so its winds are true. `050.10.03.02.01`

**HF VOLMET** broadcasts the METAR, TAF and SIGMET for major aerodromes on fixed schedules (at set minutes past each hour) and frequencies, for long-range flights beyond VHF range over oceans and remote areas. It is used to update destination and alternate weather on oceanic crossings, though datalink now often replaces it. `050.10.03.02.02`

### 10.16 Briefing and in-flight information

**Briefing** means an oral explanation or consultation with a forecaster, in person or by telephone, or self-briefing through approved systems. **Flight documentation** is the written, printed or electronic set of charts and messages (METAR, TAF, SIGMET, SWC, wind and temperature charts, advisories) for the planned flight. The commander must study the available meteorological information before flight. `050.10.03.03.01`

Before flight, the MET services provide METARs, SPECIs and TAFs for the departure, destination and alternate aerodromes; en-route SIGMETs, AIRMETs and GAMETs; SWC charts; upper wind and temperature charts; volcanic ash and tropical cyclone advisories; satellite and radar images; and aerodrome warnings. Crews apply them to choose the route and level (winds, CAT, CB, icing), the fuel, the alternates (the TAF must meet the planning minima, including TEMPO and PROB as the operator's rules require), and the take-off and landing performance (wind, temperature, contamination). `050.10.03.03.02`

In flight, crews can receive from the flight information service and ATC: SIGMETs and AIRMETs relevant to the route; special air-reports from other aircraft; aerodrome weather (METAR, SPECI, and TAF on request); ATIS, VOLMET and HF VOLMET; datalink weather (D-ATIS, D-VOLMET, ACARS); and radar weather information from ATC. Use these updates to reassess the destination, alternates and fuel, and decide whether to continue, divert or hold. `050.10.03.03.03`

### 10.17 Warnings

**Aerodrome warnings** give concise, plain-language information about conditions that could harm aircraft on the ground (including parked aircraft) and aerodrome facilities: tropical cyclones, thunderstorms, hail, snow (with expected accumulation), freezing rain and drizzle, rime or hoar frost, sandstorms and dust storms, rising sand or dust, strong surface winds and gusts, squalls, frost, volcanic ash and its deposits, toxic chemicals and tsunamis. **Wind-shear warnings** give observed or expected wind shear, a headwind or tailwind change of more than 15 kt or a significant downdraft, between runway level and about 1600 ft (500 m) along the approach or climb-out path. **Wind-shear alerts** are automated, up-to-date alerts from ground-based detection systems (LLWAS, Doppler radar, lidar), passed on by ATC, as in "WS ALERT, 30 KT LOSS, 2 MF" (a 30 kt loss 2 nm out on final). Aerodrome warnings concern aircraft on the ground; wind-shear warnings concern the approach and departure paths. `050.10.03.04.01`

### 10.18 Who provides what

The **World Area Forecast Centres**, WAFC London and WAFC Washington, run the World Area Forecast System. They prepare global gridded forecasts of upper winds, upper-air temperature and humidity; the direction, speed and flight level of the maximum wind; the flight level and temperature of the tropopause; areas of CB cloud, icing, and clear-air and in-cloud turbulence; and the geopotential altitude of flight levels; and they produce the high-level SIGWX forecasts. These are distributed through SADIS and WIFS. There are only two WAFCs. `050.10.04.01.01`

**Aerodrome meteorological offices** prepare TAFs, TRENDs, aerodrome warnings and wind-shear warnings, and provide briefing, consultation and flight documentation to crews and operators. The TAF comes from the MET office, not the WAFC. `050.10.04.01.02`

**Meteorological watch offices** (MWOs) keep a continuous watch over the weather in their flight information regions, issue SIGMETs and AIRMETs (and contribute to GAMETs), and pass them to ATS units and other MWOs. A SIGMET's heading identifies the FIR and the issuing MWO. `050.10.04.01.03`

**Aeronautical meteorological stations** at aerodromes make the routine and special observations and issue the METAR, SPECI, MET REPORT and SPECIAL. A classic matching question: observations come from the station, forecasts from the MET office, en-route warnings from the MWO. `050.10.04.01.04`

Nine **Volcanic Ash Advisory Centres** (VAACs), in London, Toulouse, Anchorage, Washington, Montreal, Buenos Aires, Tokyo, Darwin and Wellington, issue volcanic ash advisories, in text and graphic form, with the observed and forecast extent and levels of ash clouds. Europe is covered by VAAC London and VAAC Toulouse. `050.10.04.01.05`

The **Tropical Cyclone Advisory Centres** (TCACs), in Miami, Honolulu, Tokyo, New Delhi, La Réunion, Darwin and Nadi, issue tropical cyclone advisories with position, movement, intensity and forecasts. MWOs base the tropical cyclone SIGMET (WC) on them. `050.10.04.01.06`

Two international organisations stand above all this. **ICAO**, the UN specialised agency for civil aviation, sets the standards and recommended practices for aeronautical meteorological services (in Annex 3, *Meteorological Service for International Air Navigation*, and the PANS), the regional air navigation plans, and the frameworks for the WAFS, the international airways volcano watch and tropical cyclone warnings. The **WMO** (World Meteorological Organization), the UN specialised agency for meteorology, sets the technical regulations, the codes (the METAR and TAF formats, jointly with ICAO, in its *Manual on Codes*), the observing networks, data exchange and the training of meteorological staff, and works with ICAO through joint arrangements. `050.10.04.02.01`

### Check yourself

1. The METAR gives 24015KT and the tower 245/15. Why can the directions differ by more than rounding? *The METAR is a 10-minute mean in degrees true; the tower wind is a 2-minute mean in degrees magnetic.*
2. Where along the runway is the TDZ RVR measured, and at what height? *About 300 m from the threshold, within about 120 m of the centre line, at about 2.5 m height.*
3. FEW005 SCT010 BKN030 OVC080. What is the ceiling? *3000 ft, the lowest BKN or OVC layer.*
4. On a high-level SWC chart, a CB area reads ISOL EMBD CB XXX/400. What does XXX mean? *The base is below the chart's lower limit (FL 250).*
5. A TAF has TEMPO 0812/0818 TSRA. For how long, in total, may the thunderstorms last? *Each spell less than an hour, and all of them together less than half the 6-hour period.*
6. Is FRQ TS reported in a SIGMET or an AIRMET? *A SIGMET. Only isolated or occasional thunderstorms go in an AIRMET.*
7. Why does an area of strong returns sometimes hide another storm? *Heavy rain attenuates the radar beam, leaving a radar shadow behind the strong echo.*
8. On a forecast chart at FL 340, a temperature reads 52. What is it? *−52 °C: at high levels temperatures are printed without the minus sign.*
9. Which organisation issues SIGMETs, and which produces SWC charts? *SIGMETs come from the MWO; high-level SWC charts from the WAFCs.*
