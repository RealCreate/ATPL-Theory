## Chapter 6 · Air Masses and Fronts

Large bodies of air take on the character of the surface beneath them, then carry it with them. Where two such bodies meet, the warmer one is lifted over the colder one and the weather becomes active. This chapter covers the air masses, how they change on their travels, and the fronts and depressions that form between them: the core of mid-latitude weather.

### 6.1 What an air mass is

An **air mass** is a large body of air, hundreds to thousands of kilometres across, whose temperature and humidity are nearly the same everywhere at a given level, because it has taken them on from its source region. The uniformity is horizontal: the lapse rate within an air mass can vary with height. Air-mass weather is the background weather between fronts. `050.06.01.01.01`

A **source region** is a large, uniform surface (an ocean, a snow or ice field, a desert, a big continent) where light winds and slow subsidence let the air stay for days or weeks until it comes into balance with the surface. Source regions usually lie under semi-permanent anticyclones. Depressions and the mid-latitude westerly belt are not source regions: the air there is too mobile and too mixed. `050.06.01.01.02`

Air masses are named by where they come from. By latitude they are **arctic** (or antarctic) **A**, **polar P**, **tropical T** and **equatorial E**; by surface, **maritime m** or **continental c**. Examples are maritime polar (mP) from the North Atlantic, continental polar (cP) from Siberia or Canada in winter, maritime tropical (mT) from around the Azores, continental tropical (cT) from the Sahara, and maritime or continental arctic (mA, cA). "Polar" air comes from high mid-latitudes, roughly 50–70°, not from the pole itself, which is arctic air. `050.06.01.01.03`

![Air masses reaching Europe: source regions and typical tracks of mA, mP, rPm, mT, cT and cP](img/m-airmasses.jpg)

The letters also describe temperature and humidity at the source: A and P are cold, T and E warm; m is moist and c dry. The exam adds a third letter for the air's temperature compared with the surface it is crossing: **c** (sometimes written k) when the air is **colder** than the surface, making it unstable, and **w** when it is **warmer**, making it stable. So mPc (or mPk), polar maritime air colder than the surface, brings showers, and mTw, tropical maritime air warmer than the surface, brings fog and stratus. `050.06.01.01.04`

Each air mass has typical weather:

- **mP**: cool, moist and unstable over a warmer sea or land. Cu and Cb, showers of rain, snow or hail, good visibility outside the showers, gusty wind; over land it may clear at night.
- **cP**: in winter very cold, dry and stable at its source, with clear skies, frost and haze. It picks up moisture crossing a sea, giving snow showers on the coasts it then reaches. In summer it is warm and dry, with Cu.
- **mT**: warm, moist and stable over colder surfaces. St, Sc, drizzle, advection fog, poor visibility and hill fog. Over hot land in summer it can become unstable and produce thunderstorms.
- **cT**: hot, dry and dusty, unstable at low levels in summer: dust, haze, dry convection and high cloud bases.
- **mA and cA**: very cold; snow showers over open water, otherwise very good visibility.

Showers and good visibility point to mP; drizzle, fog and poor visibility to mT. `050.06.01.01.05`

Three air masses dominate Europe's weather: **maritime polar** from the North Atlantic, the most frequent; **maritime tropical** from around the Azores, which fills the warm sectors of depressions; and **continental polar** from Russia and Siberia, which brings winter cold spells on easterly winds. Less often, maritime arctic air or Saharan continental tropical air (with its dust) arrives. `050.06.01.01.06`

On a chart you can identify the air masses by where the isobars bring the air from, by their position relative to the fronts (the warm sector is mT, the air behind the cold front is mP), and by the temperatures and dew points at the stations. The exam notation is: first letter, humidity (c continental, m maritime); second letter, type (A arctic, P polar, T tropical, E equatorial); third letter, temperature relative to the surface (c cold, w warm). `050.06.01.01.07`

### 6.2 How air masses change

An air mass's final character depends on the temperature and moisture of the surfaces along its track, how long it spends over each (its track length and speed), the season, the terrain (lifting over mountains, Föhn), daily heating and cooling over land, and whether it is rising or subsiding. The same source can give very different weather depending on the route. `050.06.01.02.01`

A **sea track** adds moisture, plus heat in winter and cooling in summer. A **land track** dries the air and widens its daily temperature range. Continental polar air crossing the North Sea, for example, becomes moist and unstable in its lowest layers, which is why winter easterlies bring snow showers to the east coast of Britain. `050.06.01.02.02`

This is the rule behind almost every air-mass question. Air passing over a **warmer** surface is heated from below; its lapse rate steepens and it becomes unstable, with convection, Cu and Cb, showers and good visibility. Air passing over a **colder** surface is cooled from below; an inversion forms and it becomes stable, with stratus, fog, drizzle, poor visibility and smooth air. `050.06.01.02.03`

The season matters because it decides whether land or sea is warmer. In summer the land is warmer, so maritime air becomes unstable over land by day (afternoon Cu and Cb) and stable at night. In winter the land is colder, so maritime air is cooled over it and gives stratus and fog, especially at night. Mountains force the air to rise, bringing cloud and precipitation to the windward side and clearance and Föhn to the lee. Tropical maritime air over Europe therefore gives fog and stratus in winter, and afternoon thunderstorms over land in summer. `050.06.01.02.04`

An air mass becoming **less stable**, because it is crossing a warmer surface or colder air is arriving aloft, brings showers, Cb, turbulence, icing in convective cloud, hail, gusty winds and good visibility. One becoming **more stable**, because it is crossing a colder surface or subsiding, brings low stratus, fog, drizzle (possibly freezing), poor visibility, inversions with wind shear, and smooth air. For planning alternates the difference matters: a stable air mass can close many aerodromes at once with fog, while an unstable one affects each aerodrome only briefly, as a shower passes. `050.06.01.02.05`

### 6.3 Fronts

Where two air masses of different temperature, and so different density, meet, they do not mix easily. The colder, denser air forms a wedge beneath the warmer air, and the boundary is a sloping surface up which the warm air rises, making cloud and precipitation. Fronts lie in troughs of low pressure: the isobars kink at the front and the wind changes direction across it, veering as the front passes in the northern hemisphere. `050.06.02.01.01`

The **frontal zone** is the three-dimensional transition layer between the two air masses, from tens of kilometres to about 200 km wide, with a strong temperature gradient. The **front** is the line where the frontal zone (or frontal surface) meets the ground, or a chosen level: the surface front, or the 500 hPa front. The weather spreads across the whole frontal zone, extending far ahead of warm fronts and lying mostly near and behind cold fronts. `050.06.02.01.02`

There are two global frontal systems. The **polar front** separates polar air (mP, cP) from tropical air (mT) in mid-latitudes and is where most mid-latitude depressions are born. The **arctic front** separates arctic from polar air at higher latitudes. The ITCZ is not a front in this sense: it is a zone where the trade winds converge, not a boundary between air masses of different temperature. `050.06.02.01.03`

Over the North Atlantic the polar front lies at about 30–45° N in winter, running from Florida towards south-west Europe, and at about 45–60° N in summer, from Newfoundland towards the British Isles and Scandinavia. The arctic front lies at about 60–70° N, further south over the continents in winter. Both move from day to day. Because the polar front is so far south in winter, Spain and the Mediterranean are mainly affected by polar-front depressions in winter. `050.06.02.01.04`

### 6.4 The warm front

At a **warm front**, warm air advances and replaces cold air, gliding up over the retreating wedge of cold air. On charts it is a red line with semicircles pointing the way it moves. Friction holds back the cold air at the surface as it retreats, which keeps the frontal slope shallow. `050.06.02.02.01`

The warm front's slope is **shallow, about 1:100 to 1:200**, typically 1:150. Its cloud reaches up to about 800–1000 km ahead of the surface front, starting with cirrus at about 25 000–30 000 ft, and its band of precipitation is about 200–300 km wide. It moves more slowly than a cold front, at about two-thirds of the geostrophic wind component across it, typically 10–20 kt. `050.06.02.02.04`

> **Worked example · warm front slope.** With a slope of 1:150, how high is the frontal surface above a point 450 km ahead of the surface front? 450 km ÷ 150 = 3 km, about **10 000 ft**.

With the usual **stable** warm air, an observer ahead of the front sees a classic sequence: **Ci, then Cs (with a halo), then As, then Ns**, the cloud base lowering steadily. Continuous precipitation begins about 300 km ahead, some 12 hours before the front arrives, and ragged stratus (fractus) and frontal fog form in the cold air beneath. Visibility is poor in rain and fog. If the warm air is **unstable**, Cb and TCU are embedded in the Ns and As, with heavy showers and thunderstorms hidden in the layer cloud. The hazards are low ceilings over a wide area, moderate to severe icing in Ns, freezing rain ahead of the front in winter, embedded Cb (use the radar) and high ground hidden in cloud. As the front passes, the rain stops or turns to drizzle, temperature and dew point rise, the wind veers and the pressure stops falling. `050.06.02.02.02`

In cross-section the warm air rises over the cold wedge and cloud forms in the warm air above the frontal surface: Ns near the surface front, As further ahead, Cs and Ci at the leading edge. Precipitation falls from the Ns and As into the cold air, where stratus and frontal fog form. Freezing rain occurs in the cold wedge ahead of the surface front, where rain from the warm air falls into sub-zero air. `050.06.02.02.05`

![Warm front · vertical cross-section: Ci → Cs → As → Ns ahead of the surface front, rain into cold air, slope ≈ 1:150](img/warmfront.jpg)

The season changes the picture. In **winter** the freezing level is low, so snow, freezing rain or ice pellets fall ahead of the front, fog is widespread and ceilings very low; continental cold air at the surface makes freezing rain common, and the winter warm front is the classic freezing-rain case in the exam. In **summer** the freezing level is higher, precipitation is rain, the warm air is more often unstable so embedded thunderstorms are more likely, and there is less fog. `050.06.02.02.03`

### 6.5 The cold front

At a **cold front**, cold air advances and replaces warm air, pushing under it like a wedge and forcing it up. On charts it is a blue line with triangles pointing the way it moves. Cold fronts move faster than warm fronts and eventually catch them up, which is how occlusions form. `050.06.02.03.01`

The cold front's slope is **steep, about 1:50 to 1:100**, typically 1:80, and friction makes it steeper still at the surface, where the cold air forms a blunt nose. Its weather band is only about 50–100 km wide, with cloud and precipitation mainly at and just behind the surface front. It moves at about the full geostrophic wind component across it, often 20–30 kt. The steeper slope gives more vigorous lifting, so the weather is convective and concentrated in a narrow band. `050.06.02.03.04`

If the warm air ahead is **unstable**, the front is active (an *ana* front): the steep leading edge lifts the warm air quickly into a line of Cb and TCU with heavy showers, hail, thunderstorms and squalls, sometimes with a separate squall line ahead of the front. If the warm air is **stable**, the front is inactive (a *kata* front), with mainly Ns, As or St and Sc, moderate rain and little convection. Either way the weather band is narrow and passes quickly. The hazards are severe turbulence and icing in Cb, hail, lightning, wind shear and gust fronts at aerodromes, and sudden drops in visibility in heavy showers. As the front passes, the pressure rises sharply after its trough, temperature and dew point fall, the wind veers sharply (for example from south-west to north-west) and becomes gusty, and the visibility improves behind it. `050.06.02.03.02`

In cross-section, the cold air pushes under the warm air, and the steep leading edge lifts the unstable warm air quickly into Cb with anvils at the tropopause. Heavy showers fall at and just behind the surface front, and behind it come Cu and Cb in the cold air, with good visibility between them. The Cb line may be hidden inside layer cloud, so airborne radar is essential. Behind the front the wind is north-westerly (northern hemisphere) and gusty, with showers and good VFR conditions between them. `050.06.02.03.05`

![Cold front cross-section: steep slope, narrow Cb band, heavy showers at the front](img/coldfront.jpg)

Cold fronts are most active over land in **summer**, especially in the afternoon, with thunderstorms, hail and squalls; the most violent case is a summer-afternoon cold front over land with unstable mT air ahead of it. In **winter** there is less convection over cold land, but snow showers, icing and a low freezing level; over a warm sea, winter cold fronts are still active with Cb. `050.06.02.03.03`

### 6.6 The warm sector

The **warm sector** is the wedge of warm air, usually mT, between the warm front ahead and the cold front behind, on the equatorward side of the depression's centre. Its isobars are straight and parallel, and they show the direction in which the whole depression is moving. `050.06.02.04.01`

Warm-sector air is stable mT being cooled from below, so the weather is extensive St and Sc, drizzle, mist and fog (especially over cold sea or land) and hill fog. Visibility is moderate to poor, while temperature and wind (south-westerly in the northern hemisphere) stay steady. Low ceilings and poor visibility can persist for hours between the fronts, with high ground in cloud. `050.06.02.04.02`

In **winter** the warm sector brings low stratus, drizzle and advection fog over cold land, and the worst visibility of the whole system; aerodromes can stay below minima for long periods, so choose alternates outside it. In **summer**, daytime heating over land can lift and break the stratus into Sc or Cu and set off isolated thunderstorms, while over the sea there is sea fog. `050.06.02.04.03`

In cross-section, the warm sector between the two frontal surfaces holds warm, moist air with a low layer of St or Sc under an inversion and drizzle; higher up it may be clear or hold some Ac or As. Icing in that low cloud happens only if its temperature is below zero, which in the warm sector it often is not. `050.06.02.04.04`

### 6.7 Behind the cold front

Behind the cold front, polar maritime air moves over a warmer surface and becomes unstable: Cu and Cb, showers of rain, snow, hail or graupel, very good visibility outside the showers, gusty north-westerly winds (northern hemisphere) and low temperatures. A secondary trough often follows with another band of showers. Expect turbulence and icing in the Cu and Cb, and sudden snow showers that drop visibility at aerodromes. `050.06.02.05.01`

In **winter**, showers continue day and night over the sea, which is warmer than the air, while over land they die out at night and give way to clear skies, frost and possibly fog; at coastal aerodromes, snow showers drift in from the sea at night. In **summer**, showers and thunderstorms develop over land by day and the nights are clear. `050.06.02.05.02`

### 6.8 Occlusions

The faster cold front eventually overtakes the warm front and lifts the warm-sector air off the ground. The new front, where the two cold air masses now meet at the surface, is the **occluded front**, drawn in purple with alternating triangles and semicircles. The warm air lifted aloft forms a *trowal*, a trough of warm air aloft. An occlusion marks a mature or old depression, which usually fills afterwards. `050.06.02.06.01`

Which cold air stays on the ground depends on which is colder:

- In a **cold occlusion**, the air behind the cold front is colder than the air ahead of the warm front. The cold front continues to the ground and the warm front is lifted aloft, running ahead as an upper warm front. The weather is of warm-front type ahead, then cold-front type at the surface occlusion, with possible Cb and heavy showers, then clearing. This is typical of **summer** over Europe, with cool maritime air behind and warmer continental air ahead. `050.06.02.06.02`
- In a **warm occlusion**, the air ahead of the warm front is colder than the air behind the cold front. The cold front rides up over the cold air ahead, becoming an upper cold front, which runs ahead of the surface occlusion, so convective weather (Cb) can arrive aloft before the surface front. The weather is mostly of warm-front type, and freezing rain is possible at the surface. This is typical of **winter** over western Europe, when very cold continental air lies ahead. `050.06.02.06.03`

So over western and central Europe, **warm occlusions dominate in winter**, with extensive layer cloud, snow and freezing rain, and **cold occlusions dominate in summer**, with more convective activity. `050.06.02.06.04`

In both types the warm air is lifted into an inverted V above the two cold air masses, and the colder of the two stays at the surface. Ns and As surround the trowal, Cs and Ci lie ahead, and Cb are embedded wherever the lifted warm air is unstable. To identify the type, ask which surface air is colder; the surface occlusion takes the slope of whichever front stays on the ground. `050.06.02.06.05`

![Cold and warm occlusions: the colder air mass stays at the surface](img/occlusion.jpg)

Occlusion starts at the depression's centre, where the warm sector is narrowest, and spreads outward along the fronts. The **occlusion point** (or triple point), where the warm, cold and occluded fronts meet, moves away from the centre and along the warm front as the occlusion lengthens, leaving the low centre behind in cold air, where it begins to fill. New secondary lows often form at the triple point, and the weather is often worst there. `050.06.02.06.06`

### 6.9 The stationary front

A **stationary front** moves little or not at all (about 5 kt or less), because the wind blows almost parallel to it on both sides; its isobars run parallel to the front. It is drawn with red semicircles and blue triangles on opposite sides of the line. `050.06.02.07.01`

Its weather is like a weak warm front's: extensive layer cloud (St, Ns, As), persistent precipitation and low visibility on the cold side, lasting for days, with embedded Cb if the warm air is unstable. Waves often form along it and can grow into depressions, so watch for falling pressure along the front. `050.06.02.07.02`

### 6.10 The life of a frontal depression

A frontal depression is born on the polar front, where cold easterly air and warm westerly air slide past each other. Its life runs like this:

1. A stationary polar front, with shear between easterly cold air and westerly warm air.
2. A **wave** forms where an upper trough brings divergence aloft, and pressure falls at its crest.
3. The wave grows into a warm front and a cold front with an **open warm sector**. A wide rain belt lies ahead of the warm front and a narrow one along the cold front.
4. The cold front catches the warm front near the centre and **occlusion** begins. On satellite images the cloud takes a comma shape: the head is the occlusion, the tail the cold front.
5. Once fully occluded, the low **fills** and becomes cold-cored, with showers in the cold air. New waves may form on the trailing cold front, producing a family of depressions.

`050.06.02.08.05`

![Frontal depression in plan view, NH: warm and cold fronts, warm sector, winds veering, section A–B](img/plan.jpg)

Frontal depressions generally travel eastward (east to north-east in the northern hemisphere) with the upper westerlies, steered by the 500 hPa flow, at about 15–30 kt, slowing as they occlude. A life cycle lasts about 3–7 days: wave, developing (open warm sector, deepening), mature (occluding, at its deepest), dissipating (fully occluded, filling, cold-cored). The pressure is lowest at about the time of occlusion. `050.06.02.08.01`

A front moves with the component of the geostrophic wind at right angles to it, measured in the air behind it: in the cold air for a cold front, in the warm sector for a warm front. A **cold front** moves at about the full normal component, a little less at the surface because of friction; a **warm front** at about two-thirds of it. Fronts lying parallel to the isobars hardly move. In a chart exercise, measure the isobar spacing behind the front, convert it to a geostrophic speed, and take the component at right angles to the front. `050.06.02.08.02`

Cold fronts therefore move about **1.5 times as fast** as warm fronts, because the dense cold air pushes under the warm air, while at a warm front friction holds the retreating cold air back. The consequence is occlusion. `050.06.02.08.03`

A frontal depression moves:

- parallel to the isobars of its warm sector, with cold air on its left (northern hemisphere);
- at about the speed of the geostrophic wind in the warm sector, or 70–80 % of it;
- towards the area where pressure is falling fastest (the isallobaric low);
- steered by the upper flow at 500 hPa, slowing and turning left (northern hemisphere) as it occludes.

Occluded lows become slow-moving and can stay over the same area for days. `050.06.02.08.04`

### 6.11 A frontal wave passing a station

For a station on the equatorward side of a northern-hemisphere depression, the passage of a frontal wave follows a fixed pattern. Pressure falls ahead of the warm front, steadies in the warm sector and rises behind the cold front. Temperature rises at the warm front and drops at the cold front. The surface wind backs and strengthens ahead of the warm front and veers at each front. In the vertical, warm advection ahead of the warm front makes the wind veer with height, and cold advection behind the cold front makes it back with height. `050.06.02.09.01`

> **Key numbers · passage of a frontal wave (northern hemisphere)**
>
> | Element | Ahead of warm front | Warm-front passage | Warm sector | Cold-front passage | Behind cold front |
> | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
> | Pressure | Falls steadily | Fall stops | Steady or slight fall | Sharp rise | Rising |
> | Temperature | Cold | Rises | Warm, steady | Sharp drop | Cold |
> | Dew point | Rises in rain | Rises | High, steady | Drops | Low |
> | Wind | S–SE, backing, increasing | Veers to SW | Steady SW | Veers sharply to W–NW, gusty | NW, gusty |
> | Cloud | Ci → Cs → As → Ns, lowering | Ns, St | St, Sc | Cb, TCU | Cu, Cb, clearing |
> | Precipitation | Continuous RA or SN, increasing | Stops, or DZ | DZ | Heavy SHRA, GR, TS | Showers |
> | Visibility | Deteriorating, frontal fog | Poor | Poor (BR, FG) | Poor in showers | Very good outside showers |

In the southern hemisphere the wind backs at each front instead. Wind veering with height means warm advection, so a warm front is approaching; wind backing with height means cold advection. A station on the poleward side of the low sees no fronts at all: its wind backs steadily (for example from east through north to north-west) while the pressure falls and then rises.
