## Chapter 1 · The Atmosphere

Every later chapter of meteorology rests on three quantities: temperature, pressure and density. This chapter explains how they are arranged in the vertical, how the air gains and loses heat, and how the altimeter turns pressure into a height that is only correct on a standard day. Get these foundations right and wind, stability, cloud and altimetry questions become applications of the same few ideas.

### 1.1 Layers of the atmosphere

The atmosphere is divided into layers by how temperature changes with height, not by what the air is made of or by its pressure. In the **troposphere**, the lowest layer, temperature falls with height, on average by about 2 °C per 1000 ft, because the air is heated from below by the ground. The top of the troposphere is the **tropopause**: by the WMO definition, the lowest level at which the lapse rate falls to 2 °C per km or less and stays that small for at least 2 km above. Above it lies the **stratosphere**, which in the ISA is isothermal at −56.5 °C from 11 km to 20 km and then warms with height, because ozone absorbs ultraviolet sunlight. FL 650 is about 20 km, so the syllabus stops at the top of that isothermal layer. `050.01.01.01.01`

![Layers of the atmosphere: tropopause from about 16–18 km at the equator to 8 km at the poles, jet streams at the breaks, ISA temperature profile](img/m-layers.jpg)

Each layer has its own character. The troposphere holds almost all of the water vapour and is stirred by strong vertical mixing, so it is where weather happens: cloud, precipitation, icing and convection. The tropopause is a thin transition layer, broken into steps where jet streams lie. The stratosphere is very dry and very stable, since temperature constant or rising with height acts like an inversion; vertical motion is small and the wind usually weakens above the jet level. Cruise in the lower stratosphere is normally smooth and cloud-free. The exceptions are overshooting Cb tops, clear-air turbulence near the tropopause and breaking mountain waves. `050.01.01.01.02`

> **Exam trap.** The layers are defined by temperature behaviour, not by gas composition or pressure. And temperature does not rise through the whole stratosphere: in the ISA it stays at −56.5 °C up to 20 km and only rises above that.

### 1.2 The troposphere and the tropopause

The troposphere reaches about 8 km (FL 250–300) over the poles, 11 km (36 090 ft) in the ISA mid-latitudes and 16–18 km (around FL 550) over the equator. It contains roughly 75–80 % of the mass of the atmosphere, and about half of the total mass lies below 18 000 ft. Its mean lapse rate is 0.65 °C per 100 m. It is deeper over warm air, because a warm column of air expands, so it is thickest at the equator and in summer. `050.01.01.02.01`

The tropopause acts as a lid on convection: rising cloud reaches the very stable air above and spreads out sideways, which is why a mature Cb flattens into an anvil at tropopause level. An **overshooting top** that punches above the anvil marks an exceptionally strong updraft. The tropopause is not a continuous surface. It descends in steps at the boundaries of the main air masses, and the strongest winds and most clear-air turbulence concentrate near those breaks, with the maximum wind usually just below the tropopause. Significant weather (SWC) charts plot its height in boxes as flight levels (a box reading 340 means FL 340), with H and L marking local highs and lows of the tropopause surface. `050.01.01.02.02`

By volume, dry air is 78 % nitrogen, 21 % oxygen, 0.93 % argon and about 0.04 % carbon dioxide, plus traces. These proportions stay the same up to about 80 km. Water vapour is extra and very variable, from almost nothing to about 4 % by volume in warm tropical air. Small as it is, water vapour matters more than any other gas to weather: it is the source of all cloud and icing, and the latent heat it carries powers thunderstorms and tropical revolving storms. `050.01.01.02.03`

> **Exam trap.** The *percentage* of oxygen does not fall with height. What falls is its *partial pressure*, and that is what causes hypoxia.

Because the equatorial troposphere is so deep and keeps cooling all the way up, the equatorial tropopause is the **coldest**, at about −75 to −80 °C. The polar tropopause, much lower, sits at about −45 to −55 °C. The rule to remember is *high tropopause, cold tropopause*. The lowest outside air temperatures on long-haul flights are therefore met at high flight levels in the tropics, which matters for fuel temperature. Tropical Cb tops also reach FL 500 and above, so they cannot be overflown as they often can in mid-latitudes. `050.01.01.02.04`

The steps in the tropopause occur at the subtropical boundary near 30° and at the polar front between about 40° and 60°. Across these boundaries the horizontal temperature contrast is large, and through the *thermal wind* (explained in Chapter 2) that contrast produces the subtropical jet and the polar front jet. Each jet sits in its break, just below the higher, warm-side tropopause. Flying across a break you may see a sudden change in outside air temperature, strong vertical wind shear and probable CAT. `050.01.01.02.05`

The same expansion of warm air explains seasonal and pressure-related changes. The tropopause is higher in summer and over warm high-pressure systems, and lower in winter and over cold lows and cold upper troughs. Over a deep cold low it can come down to FL 250 or lower, so an aircraft holding a constant cruise level can find itself in the stratosphere: warmer, drier and possibly richer in ozone. `050.01.01.02.06`

> **Exam trap.** "The tropopause is higher over a low" is wrong. It is *lower* over cold lows and higher over warm highs.

### 1.3 The stratosphere and ozone

From the tropopause upward the ISA stratosphere is isothermal at −56.5 °C from 11 km (36 090 ft) to 20 km (65 617 ft), then warms by 1 °C per km up to 32 km. Real temperatures vary, and the polar winter stratosphere can warm suddenly. The air is very dry and very stable, the flow is mainly horizontal, and wind speed generally decreases above the jet core. Low density at these levels narrows the margin between high-speed buffet and low-speed buffet (the "coffin corner"). `050.01.01.03.01`

> **Exam trap.** Extrapolating the tropospheric lapse rate gives an ISA temperature at FL 450 of about −75 °C. That is wrong: above 36 090 ft the ISA temperature stays at −56.5 °C.

Ozone concentration increases above the tropopause and peaks at about 20–25 km. At jet cruise levels you meet the most ozone when the tropopause is low: at high latitudes, in winter and spring, and behind cold lows where stratospheric air is drawn down in a *tropopause fold*. Ozone irritates the eyes, nose and throat and can cause chest pain, coughing, headache and fatigue. Aircraft use catalytic ozone converters, and flight planning can avoid the highest levels at high latitudes in spring; descending is the immediate remedy. `050.01.01.03.02`

### 1.4 Temperature: definition and units

Air temperature measures the mean kinetic energy of the air molecules. The temperature in a METAR is measured in the shade, in a ventilated screen (the Stevenson screen or an equivalent) 1.25 to 2 m above short grass, so that sunshine and the hot or cold ground do not distort it. It is therefore not the runway surface temperature: after a clear night a runway can be icy while the METAR shows a positive temperature. Outside air temperature drives density altitude, true airspeed, icing risk and engine thrust. `050.01.02.01.01`

Aviation uses three scales. °F = 9/5 × °C + 32, °C = 5/9 × (°F − 32), and K = °C + 273.15. A *difference* of 1 °C equals 1 K or 1.8 °F. Two checks the exam likes: −40 °C equals −40 °F, and the ISA sea-level temperature of 15 °C is 288.15 K or 59 °F. METARs and TAFs report in °C, with M for minus (M05 is −5 °C); some US products use °F. `050.01.02.01.02`

> **Exam trap.** Temperature is measured at about 1.25–2 m. The 10 m figure belongs to the surface *wind*.

### 1.5 How temperature changes with height

In the ISA the temperature is +15 °C at mean sea level and falls by 0.65 °C per 100 m (1.98 °C per 1000 ft) to −56.5 °C at 11 km (36 090 ft). It then stays constant up to 20 km, about FL 650, and rises above that. The real atmosphere has the same shape on average but includes inversions and isothermal layers, and its tropopause height changes with latitude and season. `050.01.02.02.01`

Why does the troposphere get colder with height? The atmosphere lets most incoming sunlight through, so the **ground** is heated first and passes its heat to the air from below, by conduction, convection, turbulence, latent heat and long-wave radiation. Air higher up is further from this heat source. In addition, air that rises meets lower pressure, expands and cools *adiabatically*. These two effects together give the negative lapse rate, and they are why there is a freezing level, a cloud base and an icing layer at all. `050.01.02.02.02`

> **Exam trap.** The air does not get colder with height "because it is further from the sun" or "because the air absorbs sunlight directly". The source of heat is the ground.

To find the ISA temperature below the tropopause, use **ISA temperature = 15 − 2 × (altitude in thousands of feet)**; the exact factor is 1.98. Above 36 090 ft it is −56.5 °C. The **ISA deviation** is the actual temperature minus the ISA temperature at that pressure level. Always use the flight level (the pressure altitude), never the true altitude. `050.01.02.02.03`

> **Worked example.** At FL 180 the outside air temperature is −31 °C. ISA at FL 180 = 15 − 36 = −21 °C. Deviation = −31 − (−21) = **ISA −10**.
>
> At FL 390 the outside air temperature is −50 °C. ISA above the tropopause = −56.5 °C. Deviation = −50 − (−56.5) = **ISA +6.5**.
>
> On an upper wind and temperature chart, a route question may ask for the mean ISA deviation. Average the spot temperatures along the route first, then subtract the ISA value for that level.

### 1.6 How the air gains and loses heat

Heat flows from warmer to colder bodies, and in the atmosphere it moves in six ways: radiation, conduction, convection, advection, turbulence and latent heat. Uneven heating, between land and sea, day and night, or sunlit and shaded slopes, sets up local transfers that produce thermals, breezes and fog. In the exam, match each process to its weather: radiation gives radiation fog, advection gives advection fog, convection gives Cu and Cb, and turbulent mixing is what prevents fog. `050.01.02.03.01`

**Radiation** is energy carried by electromagnetic waves, which need no medium. Every body radiates according to its temperature: emitted energy rises with the fourth power of absolute temperature (Stefan–Boltzmann law), and the hotter the body, the shorter the wavelength at which it emits most (Wien's law). The sun, at about 6000 K, radiates **short-wave** energy. The Earth, at about 288 K, radiates **long-wave** infrared. Short-wave sunlight mostly passes through the air and heats the ground; it does not heat the air directly. `050.01.02.03.02`

Sunlight reaches us as visible light, ultraviolet and near-infrared. Of the energy arriving at the top of the atmosphere, about 30 % is reflected straight back to space by cloud, snow and ice (the **albedo**), about 20 % is absorbed in the atmosphere by ozone, water vapour and cloud, and about 50 % is absorbed by the surface. How much arrives at a given place depends on the sun's elevation, which changes with latitude, season and time of day. Fresh snow reflects 80–90 % and so stays cold even in sunshine, while daytime heating of bare land drives the afternoon maximum of thermals and Cu or Cb over land. `050.01.02.03.03`

The atmosphere filters sunlight on the way down. Ozone absorbs ultraviolet, which is what warms the stratosphere. Water vapour and CO₂ absorb parts of the near-infrared. Cloud, dust and aerosols reflect and scatter it; the scattering of blue light by air molecules (Rayleigh scattering) is why the sky is blue. In the visible band the air is nearly transparent, an "atmospheric window" for sunlight. Overcast skies reduce daytime heating, so convection is weaker and the daily temperature range smaller. UV exposure grows with altitude because less ozone and air lie above. `050.01.02.03.04`

The Earth's surface radiates long-wave infrared continuously, day and night. By day solar input more than replaces it; at night nothing does, so the surface cools. Night cooling is strongest with clear skies, dry air, light wind and long nights. The minimum temperature usually comes around sunrise, and radiation fog is often thickest just after sunrise. `050.01.02.03.05`

> **Exam trap.** The Earth does not "only radiate at night". It radiates all the time; at night its radiation balance turns negative.

Some of the long-wave radiation leaving the surface never escapes. Water vapour, CO₂, methane, ozone and, above all, cloud absorb it and send part of it back down as counter-radiation. This is the greenhouse effect. In clear, dry air some infrared escapes through a window at around 8–12 µm. That is why humid or cloudy nights stay mild, while dry desert nights cool sharply and the desert has a large daily range. If asked which constituent absorbs terrestrial radiation most effectively, the answer is water vapour, and cloud. `050.01.02.03.06`

Cloud therefore works both ways. By day its tops reflect much of the sunshine and the surface heats less. By night its base absorbs the ground's radiation and radiates it back down, and the surface cools less. An overcast day has a lower maximum, a higher minimum and a small daily range, and an overcast night rarely produces radiation fog; skies clearing in the evening sharply raise the fog risk. Cloud tops themselves cool by radiation at night, which destabilises the cloud layer and explains why thunderstorms over the ocean peak at night. `050.01.02.03.07`

**Conduction** is heat transfer by molecular contact, without the air itself moving. Air conducts heat very poorly, so conduction only affects the lowest centimetres to metres of air touching the ground. It starts the process; convection and turbulence spread the heat further. `050.01.02.03.08`

The ground warms or chills the thin layer of air in contact with it, and that layer's heat is then carried upward by turbulence (if there is wind) or by convection (if the air is unstable). In calm conditions the effect stays in a shallow layer, which is how strong surface inversions form at night. A calm, clear night gives shallow, intense cooling: shallow radiation fog and ground frost. A wind of 5–10 kt spreads the cooling through a deeper layer, so fog is thicker or lifts into low stratus; a stronger wind prevents fog altogether. `050.01.02.03.09`

**Convection** is vertical heat transport by bulk motion of the air. Air heated from below becomes warmer and less dense than its surroundings and rises as thermals, while cooler air sinks to replace it. Convection is efficient only when the air is unstable (Chapter 3). Its signatures are Cu, towering Cu (TCU) and Cb, showers, thermal turbulence below cloud base, and good visibility, because pollution is mixed upward and away. In meteorology *convection* always means vertical motion and *advection* horizontal motion. `050.01.02.03.10`

Convection occurs when the land is strongly heated by day, especially on summer afternoons; when cold air moves over a warmer surface, as with polar air over a warm sea in winter and at night; when conditionally unstable air is lifted at fronts, convergence lines or mountains (forced convection); and when cloud tops cool at night. Over land the convective maximum is in the afternoon, over the sea at night and early morning. Winter showers on west- and north-facing coasts come from polar air crossing the relatively warm sea. `050.01.02.03.11`

**Advection** is horizontal transport, by the wind, of the air's properties such as heat and moisture. Warm advection raises the temperature at a place and cold advection lowers it. Seen in the vertical, warm advection makes the wind veer with height in the northern hemisphere and cold advection makes it back. Warm, moist air advected over a cold ground or sea gives advection fog, which unlike radiation fog can form in wind and at any time of day. Cold air advected over a warm surface becomes unstable and produces showers. `050.01.02.03.12`

Advection happens whenever air masses move: with fronts, with air crossing between land and sea (sea breezes and onshore winds) and with large-scale flow from one source region to another, such as tropical maritime air reaching Europe in winter. At coastal airports an onshore wind shift can bring sea fog in within minutes, and in winter a warm sector of tropical maritime air over a cold continent produces widespread advection fog and low stratus. `050.01.02.03.13`

**Turbulence** is mechanical eddying caused by wind blowing over rough ground or by wind shear. The eddies mix the air vertically through the friction layer, roughly the lowest 1000–3000 ft, carrying heat, moisture and momentum. They lift surface cooling to higher levels and bring faster-moving air down to the surface. In moist air, turbulent mixing produces a *turbulence inversion* at the top of the mixed layer, with stratus or stratocumulus beneath it. A night wind above about 10–15 kt usually prevents radiation fog and gives low stratus instead. `050.01.02.03.14`

**Latent heat** is the energy hidden in a change of state. Evaporation takes about 2500 J per gram of water from the surroundings, and condensation gives the same amount back to the air. Water vapour therefore carries energy from the warm surface, mostly the oceans, to wherever it condenses aloft. Freezing releases about 334 J/g, and the direct change from vapour to ice (deposition) about 2834 J/g. The release of latent heat in condensing cloud is the fuel of Cb and tropical revolving storms, and it is why saturated air cools more slowly when it rises than dry air does (Chapter 3). The reverse matters too: rain evaporating into dry air cools that air, makes it dense and drives downbursts and microbursts under virga. `050.01.02.03.15`

### 1.7 Lapse rates

The **environmental lapse rate** (ELR) is the actual fall of temperature with height in the air around you, as measured by a sounding. Its mean value is 0.65 °C per 100 m, which is 1.98, or about 2 °C per 1000 ft. Actual values vary widely: steeper than 1 °C per 100 m just above a heated surface, zero in an isothermal layer and negative in an inversion. Keep the ELR separate from the two *adiabatic* rates, which describe a parcel of air that is moving vertically: the dry adiabatic lapse rate (DALR) of 1 °C per 100 m (3 °C per 1000 ft) and the saturated adiabatic lapse rate (SALR) of about 0.6 °C per 100 m (1.8 °C per 1000 ft) near the surface. A quick estimate of the freezing level above the ground is the surface temperature divided by 2, in thousands of feet: +12 °C at the surface gives a freezing level near 6000 ft above ground. `050.01.02.04.01`

> **Exam trap.** The ELR is a property of the environment. The DALR and SALR are properties of a moving parcel. An answer that quotes the ISA lapse rate as 3 °C per 1000 ft has confused it with the DALR.

### 1.8 Inversions

An **inversion** is a layer in which temperature increases with height; an **isothermal layer** is one in which it stays constant. They form in three ways: cooling from below (by radiation or advection), warming from above (by subsiding air), or one air mass lying over another (at a front). The types are the ground (radiation) inversion, the advection inversion, the subsidence inversion, the frontal inversion, the turbulence inversion at the top of the friction layer, the valley inversion, and the tropopause itself. Inversions trap haze, smoke and fog beneath them, and on climb or approach you can expect wind shear and turbulence at their top. `050.01.02.05.01`

![Four inversion types with their temperature profiles: radiation, subsidence, frontal and turbulence](img/m-inversions.jpg)

Both inversions and isothermal layers are **absolutely stable**. A parcel lifted into one cools at the DALR or SALR while the air around it stays as warm or gets warmer, so the parcel is always colder and denser than its surroundings and sinks back. Vertical motion is suppressed and convection stops at the inversion base. A stable layer therefore means stratiform cloud with flat tops, smooth air above, and haze and poor visibility below. `050.01.02.05.02`

> **Exam trap.** An isothermal layer is not "neutral". Its lapse rate (zero) is less than the SALR, so it is stable.

Each inversion has its own cause:

- A **nocturnal radiation inversion** forms when the ground cools on a clear, calm night and chills the air above it. It is shallow and breaks up with morning heating.
- An **advection inversion** forms when warm air flows over a cold surface and is cooled from below.
- A **subsidence inversion** forms when air sinking in a high-pressure system warms at the DALR and ends up warmer than the air beneath it. It typically lies at 3000–6000 ft and is persistent. It caps stratocumulus and haze in anticyclones, giving the dull, grey "anticyclonic gloom".
- A **frontal inversion** forms where warm air overlies the cold wedge of air at a front, so it slopes with the frontal surface. Rain falling from the warm air into sub-zero air beneath produces freezing rain.
- A **turbulence inversion** forms above the friction layer. Mixing gives the lowest layer a DALR profile, so its top ends up colder than the undisturbed air above.
- A **valley inversion** forms when cold air drains into a valley at night (a katabatic flow) and pools under warmer air. It traps fog and pollution in the valley and brings wind shear at its top.

`050.01.02.05.03`

### 1.9 Temperature near the surface

The surface temperature follows its radiation balance: positive by day, when absorbed sunlight exceeds emitted infrared, and negative at night. Land has a low heat capacity and passes little heat down into the ground, so it heats and cools quickly. Water has a high heat capacity, mixes, and loses heat by evaporation, so the sea surface temperature changes by less than about 1 °C in a day. Because the balance stays positive for a while after noon, the maximum air temperature comes 1–2 hours after local noon; the minimum comes around sunrise. This land–sea contrast drives sea and land breezes and, on the largest scale, the monsoons. `050.01.02.06.01`

The air in contact with the surface gains or loses heat by conduction, and turbulence spreads it through the friction layer. Over the sea the air temperature follows the sea temperature closely, so its daily variation is small. The daily range is largest over dry inland deserts and smallest over the oceans. Afternoon mixing also brings stronger wind down, which is why surface winds are often gusty by day and calm at night. `050.01.02.06.02`

Cloud reflects sunshine by day, lowering the maximum, and returns long-wave radiation at night, raising the minimum. Low, thick water cloud has the strongest effect; thin high cirrus the weakest. An overcast sky reduces the daily temperature range. For fog forecasting, evening clearing plus moist air plus light wind means a high risk. `050.01.02.06.03`

Wind creates turbulence that spreads surface heating or cooling through a deeper layer, so the air near the ground changes temperature less. Calm air allows extreme surface temperatures and strong inversions. A calm, clear night gives the lowest minima, ground frost and shallow fog; a moderate wind gives a milder night with low stratus instead of fog; and a strong daytime wind lowers the maximum by mixing. `050.01.02.06.04`

### 1.10 Pressure

Atmospheric pressure is the force per unit area exerted by the weight of the column of air above a point. Static pressure acts equally in all directions. The ISA mean sea level value is 1013.25 hPa, which equals 29.92 inHg or 760 mmHg. Pressure always decreases with height, because there is less air above; it never inverts the way temperature can. It is the quantity behind altimetry and the pitot-static instruments (altimeter, ASI and VSI). `050.01.03.01.01`

Europe uses hectopascals (1 hPa = 1 mbar = 100 Pa); the USA and Canada use inches of mercury. 1013.25 hPa = 29.92 inHg, 1 inHg is about 33.86 hPa and 1 hPa about 0.0295 inHg. Mixing them up is dangerous: setting 29.92 inHg as "992 hPa" puts the altimeter about 21 × 30 = 640 ft out. `050.01.03.01.02`

A **mercury barometer** balances the weight of the air pressing on an open reservoir against a column of mercury in an evacuated tube; at 1013 hPa the column stands about 760 mm high. Its readings need corrections for temperature, gravity and the barometer's height. An **aneroid barometer** uses a sealed, partly evacuated metal capsule that expands or contracts as pressure changes, moving a pointer through a linkage. A barograph is a recording aneroid that shows the pressure tendency. The aircraft altimeter is simply an aneroid barometer graduated in feet according to the ISA, which is the root of every temperature error in section 1.14. `050.01.03.01.03`

On surface charts, **isobars** join points of equal QFF, that is pressure reduced to mean sea level. They are usually drawn every 4 hPa (sometimes 5 or 2 hPa). Their spacing shows the pressure gradient: closely spaced isobars mean a strong gradient and a strong wind. In chart questions, the wind blows roughly along the isobars with low pressure on the left in the northern hemisphere, slightly backed and slower near the surface because of friction (Chapter 2), and stronger where isobars are closer. `050.01.03.01.04`

> **Exam trap.** Isobars on a surface chart are drawn from QFF, not QNH.

The shapes on a pressure chart have names:

- A **high**, or anticyclone, has closed isobars with the highest pressure in the centre. Air circulates clockwise around it in the northern hemisphere, diverges at the surface and subsides.
- A **low**, also called a depression or cyclone, has closed isobars with the lowest pressure in the centre. Air circulates anticlockwise in the northern hemisphere, converges at the surface and rises.
- A **trough** is an elongated extension of low pressure with V-shaped isobars. Air converges there, often with showers or a front, and the wind veers as it passes.
- A **ridge** is an elongated extension of high pressure with U-shaped isobars and generally fair weather.
- A **col** is the saddle between two highs and two lows. Its gradient is slack and its winds light and variable, so it brings fog in autumn and winter and thunderstorms in summer.

`050.01.03.01.05`

### 1.11 Pressure and height

Pressure falls with height according to the hydrostatic equation, dP = −ρ g dh: the fall over a layer equals the weight of the air in it. Since density decreases with height, each hectopascal corresponds to more height the higher you go, and pressure falls roughly exponentially. In cold, dense air pressure falls faster with height than in warm air, so the thickness between two pressure levels is proportional to the mean temperature of the layer. A given pressure level therefore lies lower in cold air, which is the root cause of the rule that cold air puts you lower than indicated. On upper-air charts, contours (isohypses) show the height of a pressure surface, and low heights mean cold air and low pressure aloft. `050.01.03.02.01`

Near mean sea level, 1 hPa corresponds to about 27–30 ft (8–9 m); the exam uses **30 ft per hPa**. This *barometric lapse rate* grows with height, to about 50 ft per hPa at 18 000 ft, 70 ft per hPa at FL 300 and over 100 ft per hPa at FL 400, and it is larger in warmer air. It gives the quick conversion QNH = QFE + elevation ÷ 30, with elevation in feet and the result in hectopascals. Do not use 30 ft per hPa at high altitude. `050.01.03.02.02`

In the ISA the pressure at 18 000 ft (FL 180) is about 500 hPa, half its sea-level value. Density falls more slowly than pressure, because the temperature is falling too (ρ = P / RT), so half the sea-level density is reached higher, at about 22 000 ft, and a quarter at about 40 000 ft. Lower density means a true airspeed higher than the indicated airspeed, less engine thrust or power, and a hypoxia risk. `050.01.03.02.03`

### 1.12 QFF and surface charts

**QFF** is the station pressure (QFE) reduced to mean sea level using the *actual* temperature (and humidity) at the station, assumed to fill an imaginary column of air between station level and sea level. It is the pressure used for synoptic charts. QNH is reduced using ISA conditions instead, so QFF and QNH are generally not equal. `050.01.03.03.01`

To reduce QFE to QFF, the meteorologist adds the weight of that imaginary column, worked out with the actual station temperature. A cold column is denser and heavier, so the correction is larger. For a station above sea level it follows that QFF is higher than QNH when the air is colder than ISA, lower when it is warmer, and equal in ISA conditions. For a station at sea level QFE, QNH and QFF are all the same. `050.01.03.03.02`

> **Worked example.** An aerodrome at 1000 ft reports QNH 1010 hPa with a temperature of ISA −15. Is QFF higher or lower than 1010? The real column is colder than ISA and so heavier, so the reduction adds more than the ISA reduction did: **QFF is greater than 1010 hPa.**

Reducing every station to sea level removes the effect of elevation, so the pressure at stations at different heights can be compared and isobars drawn between them. That is why surface analyses use QFF. Pilots still set QNH, never QFF. `050.01.03.03.03`

### 1.13 Surface systems and systems aloft

Whether a surface high or low persists with height depends on the temperature of the air column, because the thickness of a layer is proportional to its mean temperature. In a **cold low** (the cold core of a mature depression or an upper trough), isobaric surfaces sag more and more with height, so the low becomes deeper aloft. In a **warm high** (a subtropical or blocking high), the surfaces bulge more with height, so the high strengthens aloft. Cold lows and warm highs are the systems that extend to great heights. A **warm, thermal low** is low at the surface, but its warm column lifts the surfaces above it, so it weakens with height and becomes a high aloft; it is shallow. A **cold high**, such as the Siberian winter high, is high at the surface but low aloft, and is also shallow. Shallow systems like the summer thermal low over Iberia or the Siberian high do not appear on a 300 hPa chart. Upper-level systems steer the surface ones, so always read the upper-air charts together with the surface chart. `050.01.03.04.01`

### 1.14 Density

Pressure, temperature and density are linked by the equation of state for an ideal gas, **P = ρ R T**, where R is 287 J/kg·K for dry air and T is in kelvin. At constant pressure density is inversely proportional to absolute temperature; at constant temperature it is proportional to pressure. Moisture lowers density, because water vapour molecules are lighter than nitrogen and oxygen molecules. The lowest density, and so the highest density altitude, comes with a high, hot, humid aerodrome and low pressure: a longer take-off run, a poorer climb gradient and less engine output. Always use kelvin in ratio calculations. `050.01.04.01.01`

Density decreases with height, but more slowly than pressure because falling temperature partly offsets the falling pressure. In the ISA it is 1.225 kg/m³ at sea level, about half that at 22 000 ft and a quarter at 40 000 ft. Above the tropopause, where temperature is constant, density falls at the same rate as pressure. This is why true airspeed rises with altitude at constant indicated airspeed, why engine thrust falls, and why buffet margins narrow at high levels. Warm air at a given pressure altitude has a lower density and therefore a higher density altitude. `050.01.04.01.02`

### 1.15 The International Standard Atmosphere

The ICAO Standard Atmosphere (ISA) is a model atmosphere: dry, still air with fixed sea-level values and fixed lapse rates, roughly matching the annual mean at mid-latitudes. It is not the average at any particular place. It gives a common reference for calibrating pressure instruments (altimeter, ASI, VSI), for defining pressure altitude and flight levels, and for presenting aircraft performance, which is then corrected for the actual conditions, expressed as "ISA ±". Performance charts are entered with pressure altitude and either the ISA deviation or the OAT. `050.01.05.01.01`

### 1.16 Altimetry: the vocabulary

Altimetry questions are mostly about choosing the right reference. These terms are defined by ICAO:

- **Height** is the vertical distance of a level, point or object above a specified datum, such as the ground or the aerodrome (read on QFE).
- **Altitude** is the vertical distance above mean sea level (read on QNH).
- **Elevation** is the vertical distance of a point on the Earth's surface above mean sea level.
- **Pressure altitude** is the altitude in the ISA that matches the pressure at a point. It is what the altimeter reads with 1013.25 hPa set.
- A **flight level** is a surface of constant pressure related to 1013.25 hPa, separated from others by set intervals. The FL number is the pressure altitude divided by 100.
- A **pressure level** is any surface of constant pressure, such as 500 hPa.
- **True altitude** and **true height** are the real, geometric distances above sea level and above the ground.
- **QNH** is the setting that makes the altimeter read aerodrome elevation on the ground; it is QFE reduced to sea level using ISA. **QFE** is the pressure at aerodrome (or threshold) level, with which the altimeter reads zero on the ground. The **standard setting** is 1013.25 hPa.

Indicated altitude on QNH equals true altitude only in ISA conditions, and on the ground at the aerodrome. A flight level is a pressure surface, not a fixed height above sea level: its true height changes with pressure and temperature. `050.01.06.01.01`

The **transition altitude** (TA) is the altitude at or below which an aircraft's vertical position is given as altitude, on QNH. The **transition level** (TL) is the lowest flight level available above the transition altitude; it changes with QNH (and temperature), and a low QNH gives a higher TL. Between them lies the **transition layer**, where no level flight takes place. **Terrain clearance** is the true vertical distance between aircraft and terrain, and the **lowest usable flight level** is the lowest flight level that still gives the required terrain clearance in the actual pressure and temperature. Low pressure and cold air both raise the lowest usable flight level. `050.01.06.01.02`

### 1.17 Altimeter settings and procedures

Each setting goes with one reference: QFE gives height above the aerodrome or threshold, QNH gives altitude, and 1013.25 hPa gives pressure altitude and so the flight level. Some states also use a regional QNH, the lowest forecast QNH in a region, for safe altitudes en route. Decision altitudes and minimum descent altitudes are published on QNH; decision heights and minimum descent heights relate to QFE or the radio altimeter. `050.01.06.02.01`

Below the transition altitude, set the QNH of the nearest aerodrome or the regional QNH. At or above the transition level, set 1013.25 hPa. On a climb you change from QNH to standard as you **pass the transition altitude**; on descent you change from standard to QNH as you **pass the transition level**, once cleared to an altitude. In the transition layer, report your level as a flight level when climbing and as an altitude when descending. Before take-off, check the altimeter on QNH against the known aerodrome elevation. `050.01.06.02.02`

An error of 1 hPa in the setting gives about 30 ft of error in the reading. The direction is easy to remember: *subscale up, reading up*. If the QNH set is too high, the altimeter over-reads and the aircraft is lower than indicated, which is the controlled-flight-into-terrain risk.

### 1.18 Altimeter calculations

Near sea level the reading changes by about 30 ft for each hectopascal the setting changes. Raise the subscale by x hPa and the reading rises by about 30x ft. `050.01.06.03.01`

> **Worked example · changing the setting.** An aircraft flies at 3000 ft on QNH 1003 hPa. With 1013 set, it reads 3000 + (1013 − 1003) × 30 = **3300 ft, FL 033**. The aerodrome elevation is 600 ft, so QFE = 1003 − 600 ÷ 30 = **983 hPa**, and on QFE the altimeter reads 3000 − 600 = **2400 ft**.
>
> The most common error is the sign. With a low QNH, pressure altitude is *higher* than altitude. For any aerodrome above sea level, QFE is lower than QNH.

> **Worked example · through TA and TL.** The transition altitude is 5000 ft and QNH is 993 hPa. Climbing through 5000 ft on QNH, the pilot sets 1013: the reading jumps to 5000 + 20 × 30 = **5600 ft (FL 056)**, though the aircraft has not moved. Descending, with a transition level of FL 070, the pilot sets QNH 993 at FL 070 and the reading drops to 7000 − 600 = **6400 ft**.
>
> With QNH below 1013 the reading increases when 1013 is set; with QNH above 1013 it decreases. The transition level must keep a buffer above the transition altitude, so a low QNH means a higher TL.
>
> `050.01.06.03.02`

On the ground at the aerodrome, the altimeter reads zero on QFE, the elevation on QNH, and the pressure altitude on 1013.25 hPa, where pressure altitude = elevation + (1013 − QNH) × 30 ft. For example, at an elevation of 1500 ft with QNH 1023 hPa, 1013 set reads 1500 − 300 = 1200 ft. A high QNH gives a pressure altitude below the elevation, and so better performance. Before flight, the reading on QNH should be within the tolerance given in the aircraft manual, typically about ±60 ft. `050.01.06.03.03`

### 1.19 Temperature, pressure and true altitude

The altimeter assumes the ISA temperature. In air **colder** than ISA the column is denser, pressure falls faster with height, and the pressure levels are lower and closer together. You are lower than the altimeter shows, and two aircraft separated by 1000 ft in flight level are in fact less than 1000 ft apart. Warmer air does the opposite. The exam's rule is that the error is about **4 % of the height above the setting datum for every 10 °C of ISA deviation**. Measure it from the level of the station that supplied the setting, which on QNH or QFE is the aerodrome elevation, and where a question gives terrain heights, read carefully which reference it intends. In very cold weather, cold-temperature corrections are added to minimum altitudes such as the decision altitude, the minimum descent altitude and step-down fixes. `050.01.06.03.04`

![Altimeter errors: cold air and flying from high to low put you lower than indicated](img/alti.jpg)

Pressure does the same thing. If you fly at a constant indicated altitude without updating the QNH, you are following a constant pressure surface, and towards a low that surface descends, by about 30 ft per hPa. Flying towards lower pressure, your true altitude falls; towards higher pressure, it rises. Both effects are captured in one phrase: **from high to low, look out below**. `050.01.06.03.05`

> **Worked example · pressure.** An aircraft cruises at a constant flight level from A (QNH 1030 hPa) to B (QNH 990 hPa). The 40 hPa difference is about 40 × 30 = 1200 ft, so the aircraft is **about 1200 ft lower** in true altitude over B. In the northern hemisphere, a drift to the right (wind from the left) means you are heading towards lower pressure, and so lower than indicated.

The 4 % rule is the same as **4 ft per 1000 ft per °C**. To find true altitude, add ISA deviation × 4 × (height above the datum ÷ 1000) to the indicated value; with a negative deviation this subtracts. Use the deviation of the mean temperature of the column if the question gives it. `050.01.06.03.06`

> **Worked example · temperature.** An aircraft indicates 9000 ft on QNH at an aerodrome with an elevation of 1000 ft, in ISA −15. Height above the aerodrome is about 8000 ft. The correction is −15 × 4 × 8 = −480 ft, so the true altitude is **about 8520 ft**.

To find terrain clearance, first allow for pressure, then for temperature, then subtract the terrain. At a flight level, the true height in ISA temperature is FL × 100 − (1013 − QNH) × 30. Then apply 4 % per 10 °C, then subtract the elevation of the terrain. Low QNH and cold air both reduce clearance, and they add together. `050.01.06.03.07`

> **Worked example · terrain clearance.** An aircraft flies FL 080 over terrain 5000 ft high. QNH is 998 hPa and the temperature is ISA −10.
>
> 1. Pressure: 8000 − (1013 − 998) × 30 = 8000 − 450 = **7550 ft**.
> 2. Temperature: 4 % of 7550 is about 300 ft, so about **7250 ft**.
> 3. Terrain: 7250 − 5000 = **about 2250 ft clearance**.

The 4 % rule is not exact, but it can be used in either direction: to find true altitude from indicated altitude or indicated altitude from true. The small error this introduces is acceptable in the exam. Apply it to the height above the setting datum, then add the elevation of the datum. `050.01.06.03.08`

### 1.20 Wind over mountains and the altimeter

Wind accelerating over a ridge or through a gap (the Venturi effect) has a lower static pressure, by Bernoulli's law, p + ½ρv² = constant. The altimeter senses that lower pressure and **over-reads**, so the aircraft is lower than indicated, and the error is greatest right next to the terrain causing it. Combined with cold air and the downdrafts of mountain waves (Chapter 2), the error can reach several hundred feet, or more than 1000 ft. In strong winds across mountains, add a margin to minimum safe altitudes and cross ridges well above them. `050.01.06.04.01`

> **Exam trap.** In accelerated flow the altimeter *over-reads*. It does not under-read.

### Check yourself

1. Where is the tropopause coldest, and why? *Over the equator, at about −75 to −80 °C. The equatorial troposphere is deepest, so the air keeps cooling to a much greater height.*
2. At FL 280 the outside air temperature is −35 °C. What is the ISA deviation? *ISA at FL 280 is 15 − 56 = −41 °C. −35 − (−41) = ISA +6.*
3. An aerodrome at 1500 ft reports QNH 1001 hPa with the air much colder than ISA. Is QFF above or below 1001? *Above. The cold, heavy imaginary column adds more pressure than the ISA reduction did.*
4. You fly FL 060 with QNH 983 hPa and ISA −20 over a ridge of 4000 ft. Roughly what is your clearance? *Pressure: 6000 − 30 × 30 = 5100 ft. Temperature: 8 % of 5100 ≈ 400 ft, so about 4700 ft. Clearance about 700 ft.*
5. Which two kinds of pressure system extend to great heights? *Cold lows and warm highs.*
6. Is an isothermal layer stable, neutral or unstable? *Absolutely stable: its lapse rate (zero) is below both the SALR and the DALR.*
7. A runway shows +2 °C in the METAR after a clear, calm night. Can it be icy? *Yes. The METAR temperature is measured at 1.25–2 m; the surface itself can be well below zero after strong radiative cooling.*
