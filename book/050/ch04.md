## Chapter 4 · Clouds and Fog

Chapter 3 showed how air saturates and how stability decides whether lifted air keeps rising. This chapter applies that to what you see out of the window: how cloud forms and disappears, how to name it, what it does to an aircraft flying through it, and how fog, mist and haze form and clear at the surface.

### 4.1 How cloud forms

Cloud forms when air is cooled to its dew point, or moistened until it saturates, and the vapour condenses on nuclei. There are four ways to cool the air:

- **Adiabatic cooling** of rising air, by convection, by lifting over mountains, at fronts, where air converges and in turbulent eddies. This is by far the main cause of cloud.
- **Conduction**, by contact with a cold surface, which gives fog and stratus.
- **Advection** of moist air over a colder surface, which gives advection fog and stratus.
- **Radiation**, from the ground at night or from the top of a moist layer, which gives radiation fog, and stratus at the top of moist layers.

Cooling at the surface generally produces fog and low stratus rather than deep cloud. Asked how most cloud forms, answer adiabatic cooling of rising air. `050.04.01.01.01`

There are three ways of lifting air:

- **Unorganised lifting in thin layers and turbulent mixing.** Mechanical turbulence in the friction layer lifts moist air; the top of the mixed layer cools to saturation and forms stratus or stratocumulus beneath a turbulence inversion.
- **Forced lifting** at fronts and over mountains. At a warm front this gives Ns, As and Cs; at an active cold front, Cb. Over mountains it gives stratus or Ns in stable air, Cu or Cb in unstable air, and cap cloud.
- **Free convection.** Surface heating makes parcels buoyant, building Cu, TCU or Cb, depending on how deep the unstable layer is and how much moisture it holds.

The same lifting produces flat or heaped cloud depending on the stability of the air being lifted. `050.04.01.01.02`

So the cloud type tells you the stability. **Stable** air gives layered cloud with flat tops: St, Sc under an inversion, As, Ns, Cs, Ci, and lenticulars. Flying in it means continuous precipitation, poor visibility, smooth air and widespread rime or mixed icing. **Unstable** air gives cloud with vertical development: Cu, TCU, Cb, Ac castellanus and floccus, and Cc. Flying in it means showers, good visibility between them, turbulence, and clear ice in short exposures. `050.04.01.01.03`

Cloud disappears when its air is warmed above the dew point or dried. That happens through **subsidence** (adiabatic warming in highs and behind troughs), **mixing with dry air** at the cloud's edges, **surface heating** (morning stratus and fog), **advection over a warmer surface**, **loss of moisture** as precipitation, or simply because the lifting stops. Morning stratus and fog often lift into a low ceiling before breaking up, and on the lee side of mountains descending air clears the cloud into a Föhn gap. `050.04.01.01.04`

### 4.2 Naming clouds

The WMO classification has **ten genera**, grouped by height (the *étage*) and by form. Each genus divides into **species**, which describe shape and structure (castellanus, lenticularis, congestus); **varieties**, which describe arrangement and transparency; and **supplementary features and accessory clouds**, such as virga, mamma, the anvil (incus) and the cap cloud (pileus). In METARs and TAFs only CB and TCU are named; other cloud is reported only by amount and base. `050.04.01.02.01`

By form there are three families:

- **Cirriform** cloud is fibrous and hair-like, made of ice crystals: Ci, Cc and Cs.
- **Cumuliform** cloud is heaped and detached, with vertical development and sharp outlines, made by convection: Cu and Cb, and the lumpy elements of Sc, Ac and Cc.
- **Stratiform** cloud is a uniform sheet or layer, made by stable lifting: St, As, Ns and Cs.

Cs counts as both cirriform and stratiform, since it is a sheet of ice. `050.04.01.02.02`

The ten genera, by level:

> **Key numbers · the ten cloud genera**
>
> | Level | Genus | What it looks like |
> | --- | --- | --- |
> | High | Cirrus (Ci) | White fibrous filaments or hooks |
> | High | Cirrocumulus (Cc) | Small white ripples or grains, no shading |
> | High | Cirrostratus (Cs) | Thin whitish veil; produces a **halo** |
> | Middle | Altocumulus (Ac) | White or grey patches or rolls with shading; "mackerel sky" |
> | Middle | Altostratus (As) | Grey or bluish sheet; sun as if through ground glass; no halo |
> | Low | Stratocumulus (Sc) | Grey or whitish patches or rolls with dark parts |
> | Low | Stratus (St) | Uniform grey layer with a low base; drizzle |
> | Several levels | Nimbostratus (Ns) | Dark grey, thick layer with a low base; continuous rain or snow; sun hidden |
> | Several levels | Cumulus (Cu) | Detached heaps with sharp outlines and flat bases |
> | Several levels | Cumulonimbus (Cb) | Very large and towering; ice anvil; showers and thunder |
>
> `050.04.01.02.03`

A **halo** means ice crystals, so Cs; a **corona** (coloured rings close round the sun or moon) means water droplets, so Ac or As. The WMO classes Ns as a middle cloud, but its base is usually low and it spans several levels.

Six species and features are worth knowing by sight:

- **Castellanus** (cas): turrets rising from a common base, in Ac, Sc or Cc. It shows instability at mid-levels: Ac castellanus in the morning means thunderstorms are likely later.
- **Lenticularis** (lent): smooth lens or almond shapes, in Ac, Sc or Cc. It marks mountain waves.
- **Congestus** (con): Cu with strong vertical growth and cauliflower tops. This is TCU.
- **Calvus** (cal): a Cb whose top has lost its sharp outline but shows no fibrous ice yet.
- **Capillatus** (cap): a Cb with a fibrous, striated ice top, often an anvil. The storm is mature or dissipating.
- **Virga**: trails of precipitation that evaporate before reaching the ground. Under a high-based Cb in dry air, virga warns of a dry microburst.

`050.04.01.02.04`

### 4.4 Water, mixed and ice clouds

Temperature decides what a cloud is made of. **Water clouds** are above 0 °C, or slightly below it with only supercooled droplets: St, Sc and Cu at low levels. **Mixed clouds** hold supercooled droplets and ice crystals together, roughly between 0 and −20 °C (down to −40 °C in strong updrafts): Ns, As, Ac, TCU and the middle of Cb. **Ice clouds** form below about −20 to −40 °C: Ci, Cc, Cs and Cb anvils. Most airframe icing happens in water and mixed cloud; ice cloud rarely ices an airframe, though ice crystals can cause engine icing (Chapter 9). `050.04.01.02.06`

### 4.5 Inversions and cloud

An inversion is a very stable layer: a rising parcel entering it becomes colder than the warm air of the inversion and stops. Convection, cloud tops, haze and pollution are all capped at its base. Cu or Sc with flat tops all at the same height reveal an inversion, and above it the air is smoother and clearer. `050.04.01.03.01`

Moisture trapped under an inversion, whether a subsidence, turbulence or frontal inversion, accumulates. Turbulent mixing cools the top of the moist layer to saturation, forming an extensive sheet of St or Sc with a flat top just below the inversion, and radiative cooling of the cloud top at night reinforces it. Under a winter high this gives the persistent grey Sc of *anticyclonic gloom*: poor visibility underneath and clear sky on top. `050.04.01.03.02`

A **ground inversion** formed by night radiation lets the coldest, moistest air near the ground saturate first, and stops it mixing with the drier air above. Fog forms and stays inside the inversion layer, usually a few hundred feet deep. In shallow fog you may see the sky overhead while horizontal and slant visibility are very poor, which is why RVR, not vertical visibility, governs the approach. `050.04.01.03.03`

The **tropopause** is the inversion at the top of the troposphere. The stable stratosphere above stops convective updrafts, and the top of a Cb spreads sideways into an anvil there. Very strong updrafts overshoot a few thousand feet into the stratosphere; an overshooting top seen on satellite images marks an intense storm with hail and severe turbulence. Cb tops reach about FL 350–450 in mid-latitudes and FL 500–600 in the tropics. `050.04.01.03.04`

### 4.7 Fog, mist and haze

The three are defined by visibility and by what is in the air:

- **Fog** (FG): visibility below 1000 m, caused by water droplets or ice crystals.
- **Mist** (BR): visibility from 1000 m to 5000 m, caused by water droplets, with high humidity (typically 95 % or more).
- **Haze** (HZ): visibility reduced by dry particles too small to see individually (dust, smoke, salt), with lower humidity; reported when visibility is 5000 m or less.

Mist and haze are told apart by humidity, wet against dry, not by visibility. METARs also use the descriptors MIFG (shallow fog), BCFG (fog patches), PRFG (partial fog) and FZFG (freezing fog). `050.04.02.01.01`

Fog and mist form when the air near the surface is cooled to its dew point, by radiation or advection, or moistened, by evaporation from warm water or falling rain, until it saturates and condenses on nuclei. Each type of fog is named after the process that saturated it: radiation, advection, steam (sea smoke), frontal or orographic. Haze forms when dry particles accumulate in stable air, especially under an inversion, with no rain to wash them out. `050.04.02.01.02`

Fog and mist are favoured by:

- high relative humidity, so a small spread;
- effective cooling, from a clear sky and a long night;
- a light wind of about 2–8 kt, enough to spread the cooling through a layer;
- plenty of condensation nuclei;
- moist ground;
- stable air under an inversion.

With no wind at all you get dew, frost or only shallow fog. A wind above about 10–15 kt gives low stratus instead of radiation fog. `050.04.02.01.03`

Haze is favoured by stable air under a low subsidence inversion, a long-lasting anticyclone, sources of dust, smoke or pollution, light winds and no rain. It is worst late in a long summer anticyclone, and slant visibility into the sun is especially poor. `050.04.02.01.04`

**Freezing fog** (FZFG) is fog of supercooled droplets below 0 °C; it is reported whenever fog occurs below zero. It deposits rime on surfaces and on aircraft, even while taxiing, so aircraft must be de-iced or anti-iced and holdover times are short. **Ice fog** is made of ice crystals and occurs at very low temperatures, typically below about −30 °C, often near arctic towns and airports, where combustion adds the moisture. `050.04.02.01.05`

### 4.8 Radiation fog

On a clear night the ground loses heat by long-wave radiation, the air touching it cools by conduction, and light turbulence spreads the cooling upward until the air reaches its dew point. Radiation fog needs a clear sky, a light wind of 2–8 kt, moist air with a small spread, a long night, and land: the sea does not cool enough overnight, so **radiation fog never forms over the sea**. The classic setting is an autumn or winter high, ridge or col, inland, over low-lying ground, often after rain. `050.04.02.02.01`

It is usually shallow, from a few metres to about 300–500 ft (deeper in long winter nights), often patchy, and thickest in valleys and hollows where cold air drains. It forms after midnight and is thickest around and just after sunrise. RVR can collapse suddenly while a neighbouring aerodrome stays clear, and from above shallow fog can look thin while the slant visibility on final is very poor. `050.04.02.02.02`

Radiation fog clears when the sun warms the ground beneath it, burning off from the edges and the bottom and often lifting into low stratus first. It also clears when the wind increases and mixes in drier air, when cloud arrives and stops the radiative cooling, or when drier air is advected in. It usually clears by mid-morning, but in winter, with weak sun and thick fog, it can last all day under a high. A freshening wind may at first only lift the fog into a low stratus ceiling. `050.04.02.02.03`

### 4.9 Advection fog

**Advection fog** forms when warm, moist air moves over a surface colder than its dew point. The air is cooled from below by conduction, and the wind's turbulence spreads the cooling. Unlike radiation fog it does not need a clear sky, it forms at any time of day or night, and it can persist in winds up to about 15–20 kt; a stronger wind lifts it into stratus. `050.04.02.03.01`

It forms in three settings:

- **Over the sea**, when warm air crosses a cold current, as off Newfoundland, off California and over the North Sea in spring and early summer. In western Europe sea fog is a spring and early-summer hazard, while the sea is still cold.
- **Over land**, when mild, moist tropical maritime air flows over cold or snow-covered ground in winter, typically in the warm sector of a depression.
- **On coasts**, when sea fog is carried inland by an onshore wind or the sea breeze.

`050.04.02.03.02`

Advection fog can cover very large areas and is deeper than radiation fog, from hundreds to more than 1000 ft. It is persistent, changes little between day and night over the sea, and moves with the wind. A nearby coastal alternate may be fogged in by the same air mass, so choose alternates outside it. `050.04.02.03.03`

It clears when the air mass or wind direction changes, as with a frontal passage or an offshore wind; when the wind increases and lifts it into low stratus; or, over land, when strong spring or summer heating warms the surface. Over the sea heating has almost no effect, so do not count on morning burn-off for sea fog. `050.04.02.03.04`

### 4.10 Sea smoke

**Sea smoke**, also called arctic sea smoke or steam fog, forms when very cold air flows over much warmer water. Evaporation from the water is intense, the lowest layer of cold air saturates, and the vapour condenses at once into fog rising like steam. The mechanism is **moistening**, the opposite of advection fog, where warm air is cooled over cold water. `050.04.02.04.01`

It needs a large difference between water and air temperature (about 10 °C or more), cold, stable air aloft (an inversion) to keep it shallow, and a light to moderate wind. It is typical over ice-free water at high latitudes in winter, over lakes in autumn, in fjords and near ice edges. Below 0 °C it can contain supercooled droplets that ice ships, low-flying aircraft and helicopters. `050.04.02.04.02`

It dissipates when the temperature contrast decreases (the air warms, or the water cools or freezes), when a stronger wind mixes it out, or when the air mass changes. It is usually only tens to a few hundred feet deep, so offshore helicopter operations are the most affected. `050.04.02.04.03`

### 4.11 Frontal fog

Rain falling from the warm air above a frontal surface drops into the cold air below. The drops are warmer than that cold air, so they evaporate and moisten it until it saturates. This is **frontal fog**, made mostly by moistening, with some cooling, not by radiation. It is most common ahead of warm fronts and occlusions. `050.04.02.05.01`

Frontal fog forms a wide band parallel to the front, often 100–200 km ahead of the surface warm front, with low stratus and continuous rain or drizzle. It can occur in moderate wind and moves with the front, so many aerodromes may be below minima at once: plan distant alternates and extra fuel. `050.04.02.05.02`

It clears when the front passes and warm-sector air replaces the foggy cold air, when the precipitation stops, or when the wind increases. Even after the warm front passes, warm-sector stratus and drizzle may keep ceilings low. `050.04.02.05.03`

### 4.12 Orographic (hill) fog

Moist, stable air forced up a slope cools adiabatically to its dew point, and cloud forms on the hillside and covers the high ground. From inside, this **orographic fog** is simply low stratus or cap cloud. The mechanism is adiabatic cooling, not radiation or advection. `050.04.02.06.01`

It lasts as long as the upslope wind blows, it can occur in moderate or strong winds, and it often comes with drizzle. Ridges and passes are covered while the valleys may be clear, so a VFR flight over high ground risks flying into cloud-covered terrain. `050.04.02.06.02`

It clears only when the wind changes direction so that the flow is no longer upslope, when drier air arrives, or when the air warms. There is no daily burn-off to rely on. `050.04.02.06.03`

> **Key numbers · fog types compared**
>
> | Fog | How the air saturates | Wind | Where and when | How it clears |
> | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
> | Radiation | Ground cools by night radiation | Light, 2–8 kt | Inland, clear nights, autumn and winter highs; never over the sea | Sun heating, stronger wind, cloud cover |
> | Advection | Warm moist air over a colder surface | Up to ≈ 15–20 kt | Sea, coasts, warm sectors over cold land; day or night | Change of air mass or wind; not by daytime heating over the sea |
> | Sea smoke | Cold air over much warmer water (moistening) | Light to moderate | High latitudes in winter, lakes in autumn | Smaller contrast, stronger wind |
> | Frontal | Rain evaporating into cold air under a front | Any | Ahead of warm fronts and occlusions | Front passes, rain stops |
> | Orographic | Upslope lifting, adiabatic cooling | Moderate to strong | Windward slopes and ridges | Wind change, drier air |

### Check yourself

1. Which process forms most cloud? *Adiabatic cooling of rising air.*
2. A halo is seen round the sun. Which cloud? *Cirrostratus: a halo needs ice crystals.*
3. Ac castellanus at 0800. What does it suggest? *Instability at mid-levels: thunderstorms are likely later in the day.*
4. Why can radiation fog not form over the sea? *The sea's large heat capacity keeps its surface temperature almost constant overnight, so it does not cool the air enough.*
5. Advection fog over the sea in spring. Will it burn off by midday? *Probably not: daytime heating barely changes the sea temperature. It needs a change of air mass or wind.*
6. What separates mist from haze? *Humidity: mist is water droplets in moist air; haze is dry particles in drier air.*
7. Very cold air flows over an ice-free fjord. What forms, and by what mechanism? *Sea smoke, by moistening: evaporation from the warm water saturates the cold air.*
8. In which cloud is airframe icing least likely: Ns, Sc or Cs? *Cs, which is made of ice crystals.*
