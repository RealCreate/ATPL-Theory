## Chapter 9 · Flight Hazards

Everything in the earlier chapters comes together here, as the weather that can damage or bring down an aircraft: icing, turbulence, wind shear, thunderstorms, tornadoes, inversions, the stratosphere, mountains and poor visibility. For each hazard the chapter explains the cause, how to recognise it and how to avoid it, and gives the ICAO terms used to report it.

### 9.1 When ice forms on an aircraft

Ice builds up on an aircraft when two things are true: its **surface is at or below 0 °C**, and there is **supercooled liquid water** (in cloud, fog, drizzle or rain) or **water vapour that can deposit directly as ice** (hoar frost). In practice airframe icing happens between 0 and −40 °C, and is most frequent and most severe between 0 and −15 °C. The airframe is not always at air temperature: after a long cold cruise it can be colder (cold soak), and at high speed it is warmer (kinetic heating). With the outside air slightly above 0 °C, ice can still form on a cold-soaked surface or on a probe cooled by expansion, which is why manuals call for anti-ice whenever there is visible moisture and a temperature at or below about +10 °C (typically for jets, using TAT) or +5 °C. `050.09.01.01.01`

A **venturi carburettor** cools the air passing through it in two ways: the pressure drop in the venturi cools it adiabatically, and evaporating fuel takes latent heat. Together they can cool the intake air by 20–30 °C. If the air is humid, its vapour condenses and freezes in the venturi and on the throttle valve. Carburettor icing is possible at outside air temperatures from about −10 °C to +30 °C, and most likely with high humidity (a small spread) and temperatures between about 0 and +20 °C. It is worst at low power, with the throttle partly closed, as in a descent, and it can happen in clear air. Use carburettor heat as the aircraft's manual says: expect the RPM to drop at first, then rise as the ice melts. `050.09.01.01.02`

> **Exam trap.** Carburettor icing does not need sub-zero air. It is most common well above 0 °C.

**Airframe** ice forms in cloud or precipitation that contains supercooled water: Cu, Cb, Ns, Sc, St and As below 0 °C; freezing rain or drizzle beneath a warm-front inversion; and freezing fog. Icing becomes more severe with more liquid water, larger drops and temperatures nearer 0 to −10 °C. The worst situations are Cb, freezing rain under warm fronts, supercooled large drops in St and Sc, and cloud forced up over mountains. `050.09.01.01.03`

As Chapter 3 explained, droplets cooled below 0 °C stay liquid because freezing nuclei are scarce. In cloud, rising air cools the droplets below zero; in freezing rain, rain from a warm layer falls into sub-zero air and supercools; and freezing drizzle often forms within supercooled St or Sc by coalescence. **Supercooled large drops** (SLD) can lie outside the conditions an aircraft was certified for: CS-25 Appendix C covers ordinary icing, and the separate Appendix O covers SLD. `050.09.01.01.04`

The colder the air, the less supercooled water it holds: it can carry less vapour, and more freezing nuclei become active. The amount is greatest from 0 to −10 °C, moderate down to −20 °C, small below −20 °C (except in strong Cb updrafts) and practically nil below −40 °C. To escape icing, climb to colder air below −20 °C or descend into air above freezing. `050.09.01.01.05`

Cloud type changes the droplets. **Cumuliform** cloud, with strong updrafts, holds large droplets and a high liquid water content (up to several grams per cubic metre), so ice builds fast, but only over short distances: intense, brief, clear icing. **Stratiform** cloud, with weak updrafts, holds small droplets and less water (about 0.1–0.5 g/m³), but over large distances: lighter, prolonged rime. `050.09.01.01.06`

On the **ground**, ice can form whenever the surface is at or below 0 °C, and even with the outside air at +3 to +5 °C if the wings are cold-soaked. It comes from frost (after a clear night, when the surface falls below the frost point), freezing fog, snow, sleet, freezing drizzle or rain, rain or high humidity on cold-soaked surfaces, and snow or slush thrown up while taxiing. The *clean aircraft* rule applies: no take-off with frost, ice or snow on critical surfaces, which means de-icing, anti-icing and respecting holdover times. `050.09.01.01.07`

In **flight**, ice forms inside cloud from supercooled droplets, and in precipitation from freezing rain or drizzle (even below the cloud) and wet snow. It can also form **outside cloud and precipitation**: hoar frost forms when an aircraft cold-soaked at altitude descends into warm, moist air, or flies from a cold layer into a warm, humid one. Clear air is no guarantee of no ice. `050.09.01.01.08`

After a long flight at cold altitude, the fuel in the wing tanks can stay at −20 °C or colder. On the ground, the wing skin over the tanks stays below zero, so rain, drizzle or even humid air can freeze on it. This **cold-soak** ice is clear and hard to see, and can form on the upper wing with outside temperatures up to about +10 to +15 °C; check it by touch where procedures require. On a clear night, radiation can also cool surfaces several degrees below the air temperature, producing frost. `050.09.01.01.09`

The **intensity** of icing depends on:

- the **liquid water content**: more water, faster accretion;
- the **drop size**: larger drops strike more of the surface and further back;
- the **temperature**: 0 to −10 °C is worst, colder air holds less water;
- **ice crystals**: a cloud that has turned to ice holds less supercooled water, though engines can still ingest the crystals;
- the **aircraft's speed**: higher speed sweeps up more water, but kinetic heating raises the surface temperature, by about 1 °C at 100 kt TAS and about 10 °C at 300 kt;
- the **shape** of the part: thin objects such as antennas, probes, the tailplane and thin leading edges collect water efficiently and ice faster than a thick wing.

Because the tailplane ices before the wing, extending flap can provoke a tailplane stall. `050.09.01.01.10`

Mountains make icing worse. Air forced up the windward side rises strongly and persistently, condensing more water and holding large drops aloft. The freezing level may be lower over mountains, where cold air pools, and the minimum safe altitude limits any escape downward. The most severe icing is on the windward slopes and just above ridge level. `050.09.01.01.11`

Even **stratiform** cloud formed over mountains can hold a high concentration of droplets, because the continuous forced ascent keeps supplying vapour to condense. Its liquid water content can approach that of cumuliform cloud, so orographic stratiform cloud can give moderate to severe icing. `050.09.01.01.12`

> **Exam trap.** Stratiform cloud does not always mean light icing. Over mountains it can be moderate to severe.

### 9.2 Types of ice

**Clear ice**, or glaze, is a smooth, transparent or translucent, dense, heavy layer, formed when large supercooled drops freeze slowly and spread over the surface before freezing. It is the most dangerous type: heavy, strongly adhering, hard to remove and prone to forming horns. `050.09.01.02.01`

It forms with large drops and high liquid water content, between 0 and about −10 °C (to about −15 °C in Cb), so it is typical of Cu, Cb, Ns and freezing rain. Freezing rain always gives clear ice. `050.09.01.02.02`

When a large drop strikes the surface, only a small part of it freezes at first, about 1/80 of the drop for each degree of supercooling, because the latent heat released warms the rest towards 0 °C. The unfrozen water flows back along the surface and freezes later, with no air trapped in it. That gives dense, clear ice, and **runback** ice behind the protected areas, beyond the boots or heated zone, where it disrupts the airflow over the wing. `050.09.01.02.03`

![Which drops hit an aerofoil, and how rime and clear ice build up](img/m-icing.jpg)

Clear ice looks glossy and transparent, smooth or lumpy with horns. It is heavy, with a density close to 0.9 g/cm³, and very hard and firmly stuck. It is hard to see on the wings and is often detected only by ice detectors or on the wipers and probes. `050.09.01.02.04`

**Rime ice** is white, opaque, rough and granular. It forms when small supercooled droplets freeze instantly on impact, trapping air between the frozen particles, and it builds forward into the airflow on leading edges and probes. `050.09.01.02.05`

Rime forms with small droplets, low liquid water content and temperatures of about −10 to −20 °C or colder, so it is typical of St, Sc, As and freezing fog. In stratiform cloud it can build up over long periods and block pitot and static ports and air intakes. `050.09.01.02.06`

Rime is white, opaque and rough or feathery; light, because it is porous; and brittle, so de-icing systems remove it fairly easily. It is less dangerous than clear ice for weight, but its rough shape still reduces lift and adds drag. `050.09.01.02.07`

**Mixed ice** is a mixture of clear and rime ice, in layers or patches, often with snow or ice crystals embedded. It forms when drop sizes vary, or when ice crystals and supercooled drops are present together. It is rough and solid, and carries a severe aerodynamic penalty. `050.09.01.02.08`

It forms at about −10 to −15 °C, with varied droplet sizes, in mixed-phase cloud (Cu, Ns, frontal cloud) or where snow mixes with supercooled drops. It is common in frontal Ns and on the flanks of Cb. `050.09.01.02.09`

Mixed ice looks whitish or cloudy, with rough, irregular shapes. It is fairly heavy, hard and firmly stuck, between clear ice and rime, and can build large, irregular horns quickly. `050.09.01.02.10`

**Snow** at low temperatures is dry and usually does not stick. **Wet snow** near 0 °C, or snow mixed with supercooled drops, does stick and freeze, building heavy deposits on leading edges, windscreens and engine intakes. On the ground, snow accumulates and can melt and refreeze on cold surfaces. Wet snow can block intakes and pitot heads, and de-icing fluid holdover times are short in snow. `050.09.01.02.11`

**Hoar frost** is a white, crystalline, feathery deposit of ice formed by deposition: water vapour turning directly to ice on a surface colder than the frost point. There is no liquid stage, so it forms in clear air. `050.09.01.02.12`

On the ground it forms on a clear, calm night when the surface cools by radiation below 0 °C and below the frost point. In flight it forms when a cold-soaked aircraft descends quickly from a cold altitude into warmer, moist air, or climbs from a cold layer into a warmer, moist one through an inversion. The windscreen can frost over suddenly on descent, so use windscreen heat early. `050.09.01.02.13`

Hoar frost is white, feathery and crystalline, thin and light, and only loosely attached, so it is easy to remove; but it must be removed before take-off. Even a thin layer on the upper wing, rough as sandpaper, can cut maximum lift by up to about 30 % and raise the stall speed. `050.09.01.02.14`

### 9.3 Icing hazards and how to avoid them

ICAO defines three intensities of icing:

- **Light**: the rate of build-up may cause a problem if flight continues in it for a long time; occasional use of de-icing or anti-icing removes or prevents it.
- **Moderate**: even short encounters become potentially hazardous; de-icing or anti-icing, or a diversion, is necessary.
- **Severe**: the de-icing and anti-icing equipment cannot reduce or control the hazard; immediate diversion or exit is necessary.

A SIGMET is issued for severe icing (SEV ICE, and SEV ICE (FZRA) for freezing rain); moderate icing appears in AIRMETs and GAMETs. `050.09.01.03.01`

Ice reduces lift and raises the stall speed (the wing stalls at a lower angle of attack), increases drag and weight, and causes control problems (changed hinge moments on ailerons and elevator, tailplane stall). It ices propellers and engines (power loss, damage from shed ice), blocks pitot, static and angle-of-attack sensors so that instruments become unreliable, obscures the windscreen, damages antennas, and ices the landing gear and brakes. The stall warning may not protect an iced wing, so add the approach speed increments the manual gives, and beware of the autopilot masking control anomalies in severe icing. `050.09.01.03.02`

Clear ice is the most dangerous type: heavy, firmly attached, running back beyond the protected areas and forming horns. Mixed ice has a severe roughness and shape penalty. Rime blocks probes and intakes but is lighter. Hoar frost is dangerous mainly at take-off, through lost lift. Supercooled large drops and freezing rain build ice behind the protected areas and beyond the certification envelope, so leave clear-icing and freezing-rain conditions at once. `050.09.01.03.03`

The dangerous zones are predictable:

- At a **warm front**, freezing rain and drizzle fall in the cold air under the frontal inversion ahead of the surface front, and moderate to severe icing lies in the Ns and As near and above the freezing level.
- At a **cold front or occlusion**, there is severe clear icing in the Cb and TCU along the front.
- In **cumuliform cloud**, the worst icing is in the middle and upper part of the updraft, between 0 and −20 °C, where the water content is highest.
- In **stratiform cloud**, it is near the cloud top, where the drops are largest, between 0 and −15 °C. Descending a short distance below the tops often reduces it.
- In **precipitation**, freezing rain and drizzle ice the aircraft below the cloud, and wet snow near 0 °C.

The front cross-sections in Chapter 6 show where these zones lie. `050.09.01.03.04`

To **avoid** icing, start in planning. Study the freezing levels, SIGMETs, AIRMETs and GAMETs, the icing areas on the SWC chart, and pilot reports. Choose a route and level where the air is above 0 °C, below −20 °C, or clear of cloud; consider whether the aircraft is certified for known icing; and avoid areas of freezing rain. In flight, recognise ice early (visual cues, the ice detector, airspeed decay), switch anti-ice on, and change level: climb above the tops or into a warm layer, or into colder air. Change route if necessary, and leave freezing rain at once, by a 180° turn or by climbing into the warm layer if you know where it is. Have an escape plan before you enter icing conditions; aircraft not certified for icing must stay out of known icing. `050.09.01.03.05`

### 9.4 Ice-crystal icing

At high altitude in and near large convective systems, the air can hold very high concentrations of small ice crystals with little supercooled water. When the crystals enter a turbofan core, some melt on the warm compressor surfaces; the wet surface then catches more crystals, and ice builds up inside the engine. The result can be power loss, rollback, surge, flame-out or damage. Air-data probes such as the pitot and TAT probes can be affected too. The airframe usually shows little or no visible ice, and airframe ice detectors may not trigger. `050.09.01.04.01`

Strong, deep convective updrafts carry large amounts of water to high levels, where it freezes into many small crystals that spread out into anvils and stratiform regions downwind. The **ice water content** (IWC) is the mass of ice crystals per unit volume of air, in g/m³. Engine events are linked with high IWC, above about 1 g/m³ and up to 3 g/m³ or more. Because small crystals reflect radar energy weakly, high IWC does not show as heavy returns on weather radar. `050.09.01.04.02`

High ice-crystal concentrations are most likely in large tropical convective systems (mesoscale convective systems, the ITCZ, monsoons and tropical cyclones), especially over warm oceans; above and downwind of Cb cores; and in anvils. They are typically met above about FL 200, at temperatures of about −10 to −50 °C, often near or above the freezing level in deep, moist tropical air. Most events happen in tropical and subtropical regions, at cruise altitude. `050.09.01.04.03`

The hazards are engine power loss, rollback or flame-out (often of several engines), engine damage, and air-data errors: a frozen pitot gives unreliable airspeed and disconnects the autopilot and autothrust, and the TAT probe can stick near 0 °C. Other cues are "rain" on the windscreen at sub-zero temperatures, St Elmo's fire, an ozone-like smell, and light to moderate turbulence. `050.09.01.04.04`

To avoid ice crystals, do not fly over or downwind of convective cores, or through anvils. Tilt the weather radar down to look below your level, where the cores are, and increase the gain. Deviate upwind of Cb. Treat areas of weak returns next to strong cells with suspicion, since weak returns in dense, high-altitude cloud near Cb may mean high IWC. Follow the manufacturer's procedures, such as engine anti-ice and continuous ignition. `050.09.01.04.05`

### 9.5 Turbulence

ICAO defines the intensity of turbulence by its effect on the aircraft and its occupants:

- **Light**: slight, erratic changes in altitude or attitude. Occupants may feel a slight strain against their seat belts; loose objects stay at rest.
- **Moderate**: changes in altitude or attitude, but the aircraft stays in positive control. Indicated airspeed varies. Occupants feel definite strain against their belts, unsecured objects are dislodged, and walking is difficult.
- **Severe**: abrupt, large changes in altitude or attitude and large airspeed variations. The aircraft may be momentarily out of control, and occupants are thrown violently against their belts.
- **Extreme** (sometimes added): the aircraft is violently tossed about and practically impossible to control, and may be damaged.

Severe turbulence triggers a SIGMET; moderate turbulence appears in AIRMETs, GAMETs and on the SWC chart. `050.09.02.01.01`

Turbulence rapidly changes the load factor (the gust load grows with gust velocity, true airspeed and the slope of the lift curve), causes airspeed and altitude excursions, and can exceed structural limits, or stall the aircraft when a strong gust meets it at low speed. It can injure crew and passengers, cause loss of control, raise workload and make instruments hard to read. Fly the turbulence penetration speed (VRA or VB), which balances the margin to the stall against the margin to structural limits; hold attitude, not altitude; and disconnect the autothrust if the procedures recommend it. `050.09.02.01.02`

To **avoid** turbulence, plan with the SWC chart's turbulence, CAT and CB areas, the jet stream positions, SIGMETs, AIRMETs, pilot reports and mountain-wave forecasts. Choose a route and level away from jets, Cb, mountains in strong winds and low inversions with shear. In flight, change level (often by 2000–4000 ft) or track, use the airborne radar for Cb, ask ATC for ride reports, and secure the cabin early. Avoid Cb by at least 10 nm (20 nm for severe storms or at high level), and if overflying, clear the tops by at least 5000 ft if possible. `050.09.02.01.03`

Three words describe related things. **Turbulence** is random, irregular eddying in three dimensions; it mainly loads the structure and bumps the aircraft. **Gustiness** is the short-period fluctuation of the surface wind about its mean, which is turbulence near the ground as an anemometer sees it. **Wind shear** is a change of wind speed or direction over a distance, vertically or horizontally, including updrafts and downdrafts; it changes the aircraft's airspeed and energy, and can produce turbulence. Wind shear threatens performance, by draining energy near the ground, more than it threatens the structure. `050.09.02.01.04`

Turbulence happens on small scales, from hundreds of metres to tens of kilometres, and over short times, below the resolution of forecast models, so turbulence forecasts are not very reliable and are best read as probabilities. **Pilot reports** (special air-reports and automated reports of eddy dissipation rate) are the main observations, and they let others prepare or avoid it. Always report moderate or severe turbulence with position, level, time and aircraft type. `050.09.02.01.05`

### 9.6 Clear-air turbulence in flight

CAT (Chapter 2) starts suddenly and without any visual warning. It is usually light to moderate, but occasionally severe, and its vertical accelerations can injure anyone not strapped in. At high altitude its airspeed fluctuations can approach the buffet limits, because the margins between low-speed and Mach buffet are narrow there, and it causes altitude excursions. Keep seat belts fastened whenever seated, and slow to turbulence speed while protecting the buffet margins; descending widens them. `050.09.02.02.01`

To avoid CAT, plan with the SWC chart's CAT areas and jet streams, upper wind charts (strong vertical or horizontal shear, tightly packed isotachs), tropopause breaks, sharply curved upper troughs, mountain-wave areas and pilot reports. In flight, change level by 2000–4000 ft, move away from the jet core (often towards its warm, equatorward side), avoid areas where the temperature changes rapidly, and use reports from ATC and other company aircraft. If climbing or descending makes the temperature change rapidly, you are crossing a frontal or jet zone. `050.09.02.02.02`

### 9.7 Wind shear

**Wind shear** is a change of wind speed or direction, or both, over a short distance, including updrafts and downdrafts. **Vertical** shear is change with height, in knots per 100 or 1000 ft; **horizontal** shear is change along a horizontal distance, in knots per nautical mile. For an aircraft, what matters is how fast the headwind or tailwind component, and the vertical wind, change along its flight path. Updrafts and downdrafts, as in a microburst, count as wind shear. `050.09.03.01.01`

**Low-level wind shear** is wind shear along the final approach, along the runway, or along the take-off and initial climb path, typically below about 1600 ft (500 m) above the ground, where the aircraft is low and slow. It is reported in the supplementary part of the METAR (WS R24, or WS ALL RWY), by the ATIS and ATC, and by low-level wind-shear alert systems and Doppler radar. `050.09.03.01.02`

Wind shear forms:

- in **thunderstorms**, as downbursts, microbursts and gust fronts reaching 10–20 nm from the cell;
- along **squall lines** and **active cold fronts**, with sharp wind shifts and gusts;
- across **frontal surfaces**, most markedly at fast cold fronts, and below the inversion at warm fronts;
- at **low inversions**, where calm air at the surface lies under a strong wind above, as with a night radiation inversion and the low-level jet;
- at **sea- and land-breeze fronts**;
- in the **friction layer**, with strong winds over rough ground;
- over **relief**: mountain waves, rotors and lee eddies, and around buildings and hangars near the runway.

The most dangerous is a microburst during approach or take-off. `050.09.03.02.01`

Because of its inertia, an aircraft at first keeps its groundspeed, so a sudden change in the headwind changes its indicated airspeed at once. An **increasing headwind** or decreasing tailwind raises the IAS and the lift, and the aircraft rises above its path: a *performance-increasing* shear. A **decreasing headwind** or increasing tailwind lowers the IAS and the lift, and the aircraft sinks below its path: a *performance-decreasing* shear, the dangerous one near the ground. A **downdraft** takes height away directly and changes the angle of attack. A microburst combines all three, in sequence. The classic accident follows that sequence: the crew reduces thrust in the first gain of headwind, and then cannot recover from the tailwind and downdraft that follow. `050.09.03.03.01`

![Microburst on approach: headwind gain, downdraft, then tailwind and sink](img/microburst.jpg)

To **avoid** wind shear, plan with the TAF and METAR (watch for TS, CB, +SHRA, strong gusts and WS reports), SIGMETs, aerodrome wind-shear warnings, pilot reports and alert systems. Delay the departure or approach until a Cb or heavy shower has passed, usually 15–20 minutes; choose another runway or aerodrome; add the gust and wind increments to the approach speed; and use the longest runway. In flight, watch the IAS, groundspeed and vertical speed against what you expect, and heed the predictive and reactive wind-shear warnings. Warning signs on approach are a sudden IAS gain of more than 15 kt, large thrust changes needed to hold the path, groundspeed and IAS diverging, and visible heavy rain shafts, virga or rings of dust. At the first sign, fly the **wind-shear escape manoeuvre**: TOGA thrust, pitch up towards the pitch limit indicator or stick-shaker, no configuration changes until clear, and do not chase airspeed. `050.09.03.03.02`

### 9.8 Thunderstorms

Several cloud types warn that thunderstorms are developing: **TCU** (Cu congestus, growing fast, with hard cauliflower tops); **Ac castellanus** and **Ac floccus**, showing instability at mid-levels, often in the morning before afternoon storms; **Cb calvus**, a developing storm; and **Cb capillatus** with an anvil, a mature one. Ac castellanus in the morning is a forecasting signal for thunderstorms in the afternoon or evening. `050.09.04.01.01`

Every thunderstorm needs **moisture**, deep **instability** and a **trigger** to lift the air. There are five types:

- **Air-mass** (single-cell) storms form when moist, unstable air is heated from below. They are isolated, form on summer afternoons over land (at night over the sea), live 30–60 minutes per cell, and grow in weak wind shear, which is why they die quickly: the downdraft falls back through the updraft and cuts it off.
- **Frontal** storms form along cold fronts in lines, or are embedded in warm fronts and occlusions, hidden in Ns and As. They can occur at any time of day and be widespread. Embedded storms are the most insidious, so use the radar.
- **Squall lines** are organised lines of multicell storms ahead of a cold front or along a convergence line. They bring severe gusts and hail and last for hours.
- **Supercells** are single, very large, long-lived storms with a rotating updraft (a *mesocyclone*). They need strong vertical wind shear and high instability, and bring large hail, tornadoes and downbursts.
- **Orographic** storms form when unstable air is forced up over mountains. They stay nearly stationary over the ridges and peak in the afternoon.

`050.09.04.01.02`

### 9.9 The life of a thunderstorm

A single cell lives about 1–2 hours in all, in three stages:

1. **Cumulus stage** (about 10–15 minutes): updrafts only, up to about 3000 ft/min; the cloud grows, but no precipitation reaches the ground yet.
2. **Mature stage** (about 15–30 minutes): it begins when **precipitation reaches the ground**. Updrafts and downdrafts run side by side, with lightning, hail and a gust front, and every hazard is at its worst. The anvil forms.
3. **Dissipating stage** (about 30 minutes or more): downdrafts dominate, the precipitation decreases, and the anvil detaches and may persist.

Multicell storms keep renewing themselves for hours, and supercells last hours too. Lightning risk is highest within about 5 °C either side of the freezing level. `050.09.04.02.01`

![Thunderstorm cell life cycle: cumulus, mature, dissipating](img/tslife.jpg)

> **Exam trap.** The mature stage begins when rain reaches the surface. That is the exam's definition.

A **supercell** develops in four stages:

1. **Initial**: a strong updraft grows in an environment with strong vertical wind shear that veers with height, and the updraft tilts the horizontal spin of the sheared air into the vertical.
2. **Supercell**: the updraft rotates as a mesocyclone 3–10 km wide. Its updraft and downdraft are separated, so it lives a long time. On radar it shows a hook echo and a bounded weak-echo region (a vault). It produces large hail and an overshooting top, with a wall cloud below its base.
3. **Tornado**: the low-level mesocyclone tightens, the rear-flank downdraft wraps around it, and a tornado may descend from the wall cloud.
4. **Dissipating**: the downdraft cuts off the inflow, the mesocyclone weakens, and the storm decays or begins a new cycle.

Avoid supercells by at least 20 nm; hook echoes and large hail on the radar mean extreme danger. `050.09.04.02.02`

A fully developed thunderstorm carries every hazard: severe or extreme turbulence, with updrafts and downdrafts of up to 6000 ft/min; severe clear icing between 0 and −20 °C (higher in strong updrafts); hail, including from the anvil and in clear air nearby; lightning, with strikes and temporary blindness; heavy rain, with engine water ingestion and poor visibility; microbursts and gust fronts, giving low-level wind shear; squalls; tornadoes; altimeter errors, from rapid local pressure changes of several hectopascals; static and radio interference; and ice-crystal icing aloft. Never take off or land with a thunderstorm over the aerodrome or approaching within about 5–10 nm. `050.09.04.02.03`

The most dangerous zones of a **single cell** are its core, at the boundary between updraft and downdraft; beneath the cloud (downbursts and heavy rain); below and downwind of the anvil (falling hail); ahead of the storm along the gust front (wind shear up to 10–20 nm away); and above its top (turbulence and overshooting). In a **multicell** storm, new cells grow on one flank, usually the right or forward flank in the northern hemisphere (often the south-west or south side); mature cells lie in the middle and dissipating ones on the opposite flank, and the strongest turbulence lies between cells. Do not fly between closely spaced cells or under the anvil, and pass on the upwind side. `050.09.04.02.04`

### 9.10 Lightning

In fair weather the Earth's surface is negatively charged and the upper atmosphere (the ionosphere) positively, with a field of about 100 V/m at the surface. Inside a Cb, collisions between graupel and ice crystals in the presence of supercooled water separate charge: positive charge collects in the upper part, in the ice crystals of the anvil; the main negative charge sits in the middle, around the −10 to −20 °C level; and a small positive region lies near the base. The ground below becomes positively charged by induction. Most lightning strikes on aircraft happen near the freezing level (within about 5 °C of it), often in or near precipitation. `050.09.04.03.01`

There are five types of lightning: **cloud-to-ground** (ground strokes); **intra-cloud**, within one cloud, the most frequent; **cloud-to-cloud**; **cloud-to-air**; and **upward** lightning, which starts from a tall structure or from an aircraft acting as a conductor and travels upward. Aircraft often trigger lightning themselves by bridging regions of different charge. `050.09.04.03.02`

**St Elmo's fire** is a visible bluish or violet glow, a corona discharge, from the sharp points of an aircraft (windscreen frames, wiper blades, wingtips, static dischargers, the nose) in a strong electric field. It occurs in or near Cb, in snow or ice crystals, and in volcanic ash. It is harmless itself, but it shows that the field is strong enough for a lightning strike, causes radio static (precipitation static), and is a cue of ice-crystal or volcanic-ash conditions. `050.09.04.03.03`

When the electric field exceeds the breakdown strength of air, a **stepped leader** advances from the cloud in short steps, ionising a channel. An upward **streamer** from the ground, or from an object, meets it, and a powerful **return stroke** flows, typically 30 000 A and up to 200 000 A, heating the channel to about 30 000 K. Dart leaders and further strokes may follow. The rapid expansion of the heated channel produces thunder. The time in seconds between flash and thunder, divided by 3, gives the storm's distance in kilometres. `050.09.04.03.04`

A lightning strike usually leaves entry and exit points (burn marks or small holes) on the aircraft's extremities: the nose, wingtips and tail. It can damage the radome, antennas, composite structure and lights; cause electrical and avionics transients and failures; magnetise the structure so that the magnetic compass is in error; and blind the crew briefly. Engine flame-out or fuel ignition is rare, because aircraft are designed against it. At night in a thunderstorm area, turn the cockpit storm lights fully on to reduce flash blindness. After a strike, check the systems and the compass, and have the aircraft inspected. `050.09.04.03.05`

### 9.11 Downbursts

A **downburst** is a strong, local downdraft from a convective cloud (a Cb, or even a TCU or cloud producing virga) that spreads out on or near the ground in a burst of damaging, diverging winds. An aircraft flying through it meets a headwind on entry and a tailwind on exit. `050.09.04.04.01`

Downbursts come in two sizes:

> **Key numbers · macroburst and microburst**
>
> | | Macroburst | Microburst |
> | --- | --- | --- |
> | Outflow extent | More than 4 km (2.2 nm) | 4 km (2.2 nm) or less |
> | Duration of damaging winds | 5–30 min | Peak 2–5 min (whole event up to ≈ 15 min) |
> | Winds | Up to ≈ 120 kt | Outflow up to ≈ 150 kt; downdraft up to ≈ 6000 ft/min |
>
> Across a microburst the change from headwind to tailwind can exceed 50–80 kt within a few kilometres. The 4 km (2.2 nm) limit and the 2–5 minute peak are the classic exam numbers. `050.09.04.04.02`

Downbursts come from Cb and heavy showers, and especially from: high-based Cb or TCU above a deep, dry layer, giving **dry microbursts** with virga, driven by evaporative cooling; heavy precipitation cores, giving **wet microbursts**, driven by the drag of the precipitation together with melting and evaporation; mature thunderstorms and squall lines; and even TCU with little rain reaching the surface. Virga beneath a high-based convective cloud on a hot, dry day warns of a dry microburst even without a thunderstorm. `050.09.04.04.03`

A downburst develops when falling precipitation drags air down with it, and the rain evaporating (and hail and snow melting and sublimating) into the drier air below the cloud cools that air, making it denser than its surroundings. The heavy air accelerates downward, strikes the ground and spreads out radially, often with a curling vortex ring at its leading edge. The visual cues are a rain shaft curling outward near the ground, a ring of dust, and virga. `050.09.04.04.04`

A microburst is at its strongest for 2–5 minutes and lives about 10–15 minutes in all; a macroburst lasts 5–30 minutes. Waiting about 15–20 minutes after a heavy shower or thunderstorm has passed usually removes the hazard. `050.09.04.04.05`

For an aircraft on approach or take-off, a downburst brings, in turn: (1) an increasing headwind, so the IAS rises and the aircraft climbs above the path; (2) a strong downdraft, so it sinks; and (3) an increasing tailwind, so the IAS and lift fall and the aircraft sinks below the path. The total energy lost can exceed the aircraft's climb capability. On the ground, downbursts bring damaging winds and structural damage. Escape early, with maximum thrust and pitch up to the stick-shaker or pitch limit indicator. `050.09.04.04.06`

### 9.12 Avoiding thunderstorms

Each type of thunderstorm can be anticipated. Before flight, use the TAF and METAR (TS, CB, TCU, +SHRA), SIGMETs (for obscured, embedded or frequent thunderstorms, or squall lines: OBSC, EMBD, FRQ or SQL TS), the CB areas and tops on the SWC chart, satellite images (overshooting tops, cold infrared tops), radar composites, lightning maps and stability forecasts. In flight, watch Cu growing, anvils and mammatus; use radar information from ATC; and use the **airborne weather radar**, adjusting tilt, gain and range and watching for attenuation shadows behind strong cells. A **lightning detector** (stormscope) detects discharges in all directions, including in areas without heavy rain, and complements the radar, though its bearings and ranges are approximate. Remember that weather radar detects precipitation, mainly liquid water: it does not see turbulence, dry hail or ice crystals. Chapter 10 describes both instruments. `050.09.04.05.01`

The techniques for avoiding thunderstorm hazards are:

- Avoid every Cb by at least 10 nm, and by 20 nm for severe storms, at high altitude or with strong echoes. Deviate on the upwind side where possible.
- Do not fly under the anvil or overhang, and do not fly between cells less than about 20–30 nm apart.
- If overflying, clear the top by at least 5000 ft, which is often impossible with tops at FL 400 or above.
- Do not take off or land with a storm within about 5–10 nm of the aerodrome or approach path.
- If penetration is unavoidable, fly at turbulence penetration speed and hold attitude, not altitude; set the autothrust and autopilot as the procedures say; switch anti-ice on and cockpit lights fully bright; do not turn back once inside; and take the shortest path through the weakest part (lowest reflectivity), at a level that avoids the 0 to −20 °C band of worst icing and lightning where possible.

`050.09.04.05.02`

> **Exam trap.** Never "pass on the downwind side" or "fly below the anvil to avoid the hail". Both are wrong.

### 9.13 Tornadoes

A **tornado** is a violently rotating column of air, in contact with the ground, hanging from a cumuliform cloud (usually a supercell Cb). It is often visible as a funnel cloud of condensation, a cloud of debris, or both. Over water it is a **waterspout**. A funnel cloud that does not reach the ground is not yet a tornado. The METAR code is FC, for funnel cloud (tornado or waterspout). `050.09.05.01.01`

Strong low-level vertical wind shear gives the air a horizontal spin, which the supercell's updraft tilts into the vertical to form the mesocyclone. Stretching the rotating column makes it spin faster (conservation of angular momentum), and its interaction with the rear-flank downdraft concentrates the rotation into a narrow vortex that reaches the ground. The pressure in its core can be tens of hectopascals lower than around it. Tornadoes need a supercell or a strong squall line, not ordinary air-mass storms. `050.09.05.01.02`

A tornado appears as a funnel or rope-shaped cloud hanging from the cloud base, often from a wall cloud, with debris at the ground. It is about 50–500 m across (sometimes more than 1 km). Tornadoes are most common in spring and early summer (April to June in the USA), in the late afternoon and early evening, from mature supercells. They move at about 10–40 kt, in the USA usually from south-west to north-east. Their winds range from about 60 kt to more than 250 kt (EF0 to EF5 on the Enhanced Fujita scale), and their paths are typically a few kilometres long, sometimes more than 100 km. Avoid the parent storm by at least 20 nm. `050.09.05.01.03`

Tornadoes are far more frequent and violent in the central plains of the USA ("Tornado Alley", over 1000 a year), where cold, dry air from the Rockies, warm, moist air from the Gulf of Mexico and strong shear meet. Europe has a few hundred a year, mostly weak (EF0–EF2), though strong ones occasionally occur, and waterspouts are common over the Mediterranean in late summer and autumn. In the exam: tornadoes in Europe are less frequent and usually weaker than in the USA. `050.09.05.01.04`

A **dust devil** is a small whirlwind, a few metres to tens of metres wide and up to a few hundred metres to about 1000 m tall, formed by intense surface heating in clear air. It is not attached to any cloud, lasts minutes, usually has winds below about 50 kt, and can turn either way. A tornado is attached to a Cb and is far larger, stronger and longer-lived, and mostly turns cyclonically. Dust devils can still upset light aircraft and helicopters near the ground on hot afternoons; the METAR code is PO (dust or sand whirls). `050.09.05.01.05`

### 9.14 Inversions and performance

A **strong inversion on its own** affects take-off: climbing out of the cold air into the warmer air above, the density falls, so thrust or power and lift fall and the climb gradient decreases, which is critical for heavy aircraft and obstacle clearance. Haze, fog or smoke beneath it reduce visibility. On approach, entering the cold layer improves performance slightly. An inversion **combined with marked wind shear**, such as a calm, cold surface layer under a strong wind above (the night low-level jet), adds sudden IAS changes when crossing it, gains or losses depending on the wind direction relative to the flight path, possible sink near the ground, and turbulence at the boundary. The most dangerous cases are climbing out of a calm layer into a tailwind above, which loses IAS at low height, and descending from a strong headwind into calm air, which loses IAS on short final. When the forecast shows strong winds at 1000–2000 ft over a calm surface, expect the shear and brief it. `050.09.06.01.01`

### 9.15 Flying in the stratosphere

The stratosphere has advantages for jets. It lies above almost all cloud and weather, except overshooting Cb tops; the air is generally smooth, except for CAT near the tropopause and jets and breaking mountain waves; the low density means less drag and better specific range; the constant low temperature (ISA −56.5 °C) helps engine efficiency; there is usually no icing; and visibility is good. Optimum cruise levels for jets lie near or just above the tropopause. `050.09.07.01.01`

Its phenomena influence flight in four ways:

- **Wind**: the maximum, at the jet core, is just below the tropopause, and wind decreases with height above it.
- **Temperature**: roughly constant or slightly rising with height. Temperatures above ISA reduce engine thrust and the maximum altitude, and raise the speed of sound, changing the relationship between Mach number and TAS.
- **Density**: low, so the margin between low-speed and high-speed buffet is narrow (the coffin corner) and the manoeuvre margin reduced.
- **Turbulence**: CAT near tropopause breaks and above jet cores, breaking mountain waves, and overshooting tops.

Ozone (which irritates the cabin) and cosmic radiation at high latitudes and altitudes also matter. In turbulence at high level near the buffet limits, descend to widen the margins. `050.09.07.01.02`

### 9.16 Mountains

Mountains slow and deform fronts. The low-level cold air is blocked on the windward side, so the front may become almost stationary and its passage delayed, while the extra lifting increases cloud and precipitation on the windward slopes (heavy rain or snow). In the lee the front may weaken, through the Föhn effect, and then re-form further downstream, and lee lows such as the Genoa low may develop. The windward side of the Alps or Pyrenees therefore gets prolonged frontal weather, and the lee side improvement, Föhn and turbulence. `050.09.08.01.01`

Mountains bring their own vertical motions, shear and turbulence: upslope and downslope winds (anabatic and katabatic), valley and mountain winds, forced ascent and strong descent across ridges, mountain waves with strong up- and downdrafts, rotors, mechanical turbulence and lee eddies, channelling and Venturi acceleration in valleys and passes, marked shear near ridges and at valley inversions, and convective turbulence over sunny slopes in summer. Cross ridges at a safe height and at 45°, approach a ridge from the upwind side, where the updrafts are, and avoid the lee at low level in strong wind. `050.09.08.02.01`

On a sketch of a mountain chain, the turbulent zones lie as follows. On the windward side there is moderate mechanical turbulence close to the slope. At the crest the flow accelerates, with a cap cloud and strong shear. In the lee a strong downdraft runs down the slope, and rotors lie beneath the wave crests at or below ridge height, with severe or extreme turbulence. Above them is smooth wave flow with lenticulars, and the waves may break at high levels, giving CAT. Sketch questions place the rotor under the first wave crest in the lee; the mountain-wave diagram in Chapter 2 shows it. `050.09.08.02.02`

Relief increases icing. Lifting over mountains produces cloud with high liquid water content and large drops on the windward side and over the ridge; the freezing level may be lower in valleys where cold air pools; and the minimum safe altitude may force you to fly in the icing layer with no escape downward. Plan routes where the minimum safe altitude is below the freezing level, or above the cloud tops. `050.09.08.02.03`

### 9.17 Valley inversions

At night, air cooled on the slopes drains down into the valley floor and collects as a pool of cold air beneath warmer air on the slopes above (the *thermal belt*). This makes a strong **valley inversion**, which traps fog, frost and pollution. Valley aerodromes get radiation fog and low stratus in the morning, while the middle of the slopes may be warmer and clearer. `050.09.08.03.01`

![Valley at night: katabatic drainage fills the floor with a cold pool and fog under an inversion; warmer thermal belt on the slopes](img/m-valley.jpg)

A valley inversion also forms when **warm air flows over the valley** (warm advection or Föhn) but cannot push out the dense pool of cold air in it. The warm wind blows over the top, making a sharp inversion with strong wind shear at its top and calm, cold air below. In winter such a pool can last for days, and descending from the warm, windy layer into the calm cold pool on approach brings strong shear. `050.09.08.03.02`

For an aircraft, a valley inversion means poor visibility (fog, haze and smoke) beneath it; wind shear and turbulence at its boundary; sudden IAS changes when climbing or descending through it; reduced climb performance on entering the warmer air above; and possible icing in fog or stratus below zero. Brief the shear and the performance change for departures from valley aerodromes in winter. `050.09.08.03.03`

### 9.18 Visibility

Precipitation reduces visibility by different amounts. **Drizzle** has many small drops, often with mist or fog, so visibility is often below 3000 m and sometimes below 1000 m. **Rain** has little effect when light (above 5 km), reduces visibility to 3–10 km when moderate, and when heavy, especially in showers, can bring it below 1000 m and cover the windscreen in water. **Snow** is the most effective: light snow gives 1–5 km, moderate about 500–1000 m, and heavy snow less than 500 m, down to about 100 m. For the same intensity, snow reduces visibility most, then drizzle, then rain. `050.09.09.01.01`

Obscurations reduce it too:

- **Fog** gives visibility below 1000 m, and **mist** (wet) 1000–5000 m.
- **Haze**, of dry particles, typically gives 1–5 km, worse looking into the sun.
- **Smoke** (FU), from fires and industry, reduces horizontal and slant visibility, and is often yellowish or brown.
- **Volcanic ash** (VA) is made of abrasive glass particles. Besides reducing visibility, it can severely damage engines, abrade the windscreen, block the pitot and cause flame-out. It is often invisible at night or in cloud. Avoid it completely, following VAAC advisories and VA SIGMETs (Chapter 10); its signs include St Elmo's fire, a sulphur smell, dust in the cockpit and rising exhaust gas temperatures.

`050.09.09.01.02`

**Dust** (DU) or **sand** (SA) suspended in the air, raised earlier by wind somewhere else, makes a dry haze. It can reduce visibility to a few kilometres or less over large areas and for long periods, as with the Harmattan, or Saharan dust reaching Europe. It persists after the wind has dropped; BLDU and BLSA, by contrast, are dust or sand being raised by the local wind at the time of the observation. `050.09.09.01.03`

Meteorological visibility is measured **horizontally at the surface**. From above a shallow fog or haze layer, a pilot may see the ground straight down, through only a short path in the layer, while the **slant visibility** along the approach path, a long path through the layer, is much poorer. Inside the layer, flight visibility may differ from the reported ground visibility; above a haze layer, horizontal visibility can be excellent while the ground is hidden at shallow angles. On an approach into shallow fog the runway lights can disappear as you enter the layer, so rely on the RVR and the approach minima, not on what you saw from above. `050.09.09.01.04`

Wind can raise snow, dust or sand. **Drifting snow** (DRSN) is raised by the wind to less than 2 m; it does not significantly reduce visibility at eye level, but it hides surface features and runway markings. **Blowing snow** (BLSN) is raised to 2 m or more, and can reduce horizontal visibility below 1000 m even when no snow is falling. It can cause a loss of surface contrast (whiteout) and is reported even under a clear sky. `050.09.09.02.01`

In the same way, **drifting dust or sand** (DRDU, DRSA) is raised below 2 m, and **blowing dust or sand** (BLDU, BLSA) is raised to 2 m or more by the local wind, reducing visibility at eye level. On take-off it brings engine ingestion and erosion, and it hides the runway edges. `050.09.09.02.02`

In a **dust storm** (DS) or **sandstorm** (SS), strong, turbulent winds raise large amounts of dust or sand to considerable heights, often several thousand feet and up to FL 100 or more. Visibility is generally below 1000 m, and below 200 m when heavy (+DS, +SS). It often arrives with a wall-like leading edge (a haboob, from Cb outflow). Avoid it: aerodromes close, and the dust causes severe abrasion and engine damage. It can arrive suddenly with a gust front. `050.09.09.02.03`

**Ice or frost on the windscreen**, from supercooled drops, freezing rain, or hoar frost on descent into warm, moist air, can reduce forward visibility to zero, and rain on an unheated screen reduces it too. Switch on windscreen heat before entering icing conditions; hoar frost on descent can form suddenly. `050.09.09.02.04`

Looking towards a **low sun**, light scattered by haze, dust or a dirty or scratched windscreen dramatically reduces contrast (glare), so visibility into the sun is much worse than down-sun. Landing into a low sun in haze, the runway and other traffic are hard to see; consider the timing and runway direction, and use visors or sunglasses. `050.09.09.02.05`

**Sunlight reflected** from the tops of haze, fog or cloud layers makes a bright, uniform glare that reduces the contrast of anything below or within the layer and blurs the horizon. Flying just above such a layer, the ground, traffic and terrain are hard to see, and the false horizon risks spatial disorientation. `050.09.09.02.06`
