## Chapter 1 · The Earth, Position and Direction

Navigation starts with three questions: what shape is the Earth, how do we say where a point is on it, and how do we say which way we are pointing. This chapter answers all three. It builds the grid of latitude and longitude, the units that come from it, and the three norths (true, magnetic and compass) that every heading and track is measured from. The chapters that follow on speed, charts and great circles all use these ideas.

### 1.1 The shape of the Earth

The Earth is not a perfect sphere. If the oceans were free to settle under gravity alone and could flow through channels under the continents, mean sea level would form a surface called the **geoid**. It is an equipotential surface of gravity, shaped only by **gravity and the centrifugal force** of the Earth's rotation. Because mass is unevenly spread inside the Earth, the geoid is irregular, with bumps and hollows of up to about ±100 m. Elevations and altitudes above mean sea level are measured from the geoid. `061.01.01.01.01`

An irregular surface is no use for drawing a grid, so mapmakers replace it with a smooth mathematical shape, an **ellipsoid**. The Earth is close to an *oblate* ellipsoid: flattened at the poles and bulging at the equator, with a polar diameter about 43 km shorter than the equatorial one (a flattening of about 1/298). Many ellipsoids have been used for national mapping, each fitted to its own region. For aviation ICAO requires all published coordinates to be on **WGS-84** (World Geodetic System 1984), the global ellipsoid also used by GPS. Mixing coordinates from different datums can shift a position by hundreds of metres, which matters for RNAV approaches and database coding. GNSS heights are measured above the ellipsoid; the difference from height above MSL is the *geoid undulation*. `061.01.01.01.02`

> **Exam trap.** The geoid is the irregular one; the ellipsoid is the smooth model. The geoid is shaped by gravity and centrifugal force only, not by tides or wind.

The size of the Earth comes straight from the definition of the nautical mile. A circle has 360° × 60' = **21 600 minutes of arc**, and one minute of latitude is about one nautical mile, so the circumference is about **21 600 NM**, or about **40 000 km**. Pole to pole is half of that, 10 800 NM, and the equator to a pole is 90 × 60 = 5400 NM. `061.01.01.01.03`

> **Key numbers · the Earth**
>
> | Quantity | Value |
> | --- | --- |
> | Circumference | ≈ 21 600 NM ≈ 40 000 km |
> | Equator to pole | 5400 NM |
> | 1° of latitude | 60 NM, anywhere |
> | Flattening | ≈ 1/298 (polar diameter ≈ 43 km shorter) |
> | Reference ellipsoid | WGS-84 (ICAO Annex 15) |

### 1.2 Rotation, seasons and daylight

The Earth spins on its axis from **west to east**, anticlockwise seen from above the North Pole. Relative to the Sun one turn takes 24 hours, so the Sun appears to move westward at **15° of longitude per hour**. The Earth orbits the Sun once in about 365¼ days, in a plane called the **ecliptic**. Its spin axis is tilted **23½° from the perpendicular** to the ecliptic, which is the same as **66½° to the plane** of the ecliptic, and the axis keeps pointing the same way in space all year. The orbit is slightly elliptical, and the Earth is in fact closest to the Sun in early January, during the northern winter. `061.01.01.02.01`

Because of the tilt, the latitude where the Sun is overhead at noon, its *declination*, moves through the year between **23½° N** at the June solstice (about 21 June) and **23½° S** at the December solstice (about 21 December). It crosses the equator at the equinoxes, about 20 March and 23 September. When the Sun is high, each square metre of ground receives more energy (more *insolation*) and the day is longer. At the equinoxes day and night are about 12 hours everywhere. Beyond the polar circles at 66½° there is at least one day of continuous daylight in summer and of continuous night in winter. At the equator the day is about 12 hours all year, and the yearly variation grows with latitude. `061.01.01.02.02`

> **Exam trap.** The seasons come from the tilt of the axis, not from the distance to the Sun. Both 23½° and 66½° appear in questions: one is measured from the perpendicular, the other from the plane.

### 1.3 Latitude and longitude

A position is given by **geodetic latitude and longitude on the WGS-84 ellipsoid**. Geodetic latitude is the angle between the *normal to the ellipsoid* at the point and the plane of the equator. It is not the angle at the Earth's centre, which is the geocentric latitude and differs slightly because the Earth is flattened. Charts and GNSS use geodetic latitude. Coordinates are written latitude first, for example N40°25.5' W003°41.2'. `061.01.02.01.01`

![Latitude and longitude graticule on the Earth](img/gn-latlong.jpg)

**Latitude** is the angular distance north or south of the equator, from 0° to 90° N or S. **Parallels of latitude** are circles parallel to the equator joining points of equal latitude. The equator is the only parallel that is a great circle; all the others are *small circles*, and they get shorter towards the poles: the length of a parallel is the length of the equator × cos latitude. At 60° a parallel is half as long as the equator. `061.01.02.01.02`

The **difference of latitude** (d.lat) is the arc of a meridian between two parallels. If both points are in the same hemisphere, subtract their latitudes; if they are in opposite hemispheres, add them. Name the result N or S by the direction of travel. Because one minute of latitude is one nautical mile, d.lat in minutes is the distance in NM along a meridian. `061.01.02.01.03`

> **Worked example · d.lat.** From 52°30' N to 47°10' N: same hemisphere, subtract: d.lat = 5°20' S = 320', so 320 NM along the meridian. From 10° N to 15° S: opposite hemispheres, add: d.lat = 25° S.

**Longitude** is the angle east or west of the prime meridian through Greenwich, from 0° to 180° E or W. **Meridians** are semi-great circles running from pole to pole. A meridian together with its anti-meridian, 180° away, forms a complete great circle. All meridians meet at the poles and are parallel to each other only where they cross the equator. 180° E and 180° W are the same meridian, which is the basis of the date line (Chapter 6). `061.01.02.01.04`

The **difference of longitude** (d.long) is the smaller angle at the pole between two meridians. In the same hemisphere, subtract; in opposite hemispheres, add, and if the sum exceeds 180°, take it from 360° and reverse the direction, because the shorter way is then across the 180° meridian. `061.01.02.01.05`

> **Worked example · d.long across the date line.** From 170° E to 160° W: 170 + 160 = 330°, more than 180°. So d.long = 360 − 330 = **30° E**, travelling east across the 180° meridian.

> **Exam trap.** d.long is an angle, not a distance. It becomes a distance only when multiplied by cos latitude (departure, Chapter 2).

### 1.4 True north and how to measure from it

**True north** (TN) is the direction along a meridian towards the geographic North Pole. True directions are measured clockwise from TN, from 000° to 360°. Charts are drawn relative to true north, so a track measured on a chart with a protractor is a true track. Some directions you meet in flight are true and others magnetic: winds in METARs, TAFs and wind charts are true, while the wind given by ATIS or the tower for landing, runway directions, VOR radials and most AIP en-route tracks are magnetic. `061.01.03.01.01`

To measure a true direction on a chart, align the protractor with a **meridian** (never with the edge of the chart) and measure clockwise from it at the point you are interested in. On a Mercator chart all meridians are parallel, so a straight line crosses them all at the same angle. On a Lambert or polar chart the meridians converge, so a straight line crosses each one at a different angle. Measuring at the **mid-meridian** gives approximately the rhumb-line track; measuring at the departure meridian gives the initial great-circle track (Chapter 4). `061.01.03.01.02`

### 1.5 Magnetic north and variation

**Magnetic north** (MN) is the direction a freely suspended magnet points: along the horizontal part of the Earth's magnetic field towards the north magnetic pole. That pole is not at the geographic pole. It lies in the Arctic Ocean north of Canada and is drifting towards Siberia. Close to the magnetic poles the field points steeply downwards (large *dip*), the horizontal part is weak and the compass becomes unreliable. `061.01.03.01.03`

**Variation** is the angle between true north and magnetic north at a place. It is named **East** if magnetic north lies east of true north, and **West** if it lies west. To convert: True = Magnetic + East variation, and True = Magnetic − West variation. The memory aid is "variation east, magnetic least; variation west, magnetic best". Lines joining places of equal variation are **isogonals**; the line of zero variation is the **agonic line**. `061.01.03.01.04`

![World map of magnetic variation from the World Magnetic Model: isogonals, the agonic lines and the north magnetic pole](img/gn-isogonic.jpg)

> **Worked example · variation.** True track 090°, variation 5° W. Magnetic is "best" with west variation, so MT = 090 + 5 = **095°**.

Variation changes with **position**, shown by the isogonals on a chart, and with **time**. As the magnetic poles drift, variation everywhere changes slowly; this *secular change* is a few minutes of arc per year in Europe. Charts give the variation for a stated year and the annual change, so add the annual change × the number of years since then. There are also small daily changes, and sudden disturbances during magnetic storms caused by solar activity, which also upset HF radio. Because of secular change, runway designators and VOR alignments are updated from time to time. `061.01.03.01.05`

> **Exam trap.** Isogonal = equal variation; agonic = zero variation; isoclinal = equal dip; aclinic = zero dip. A VOR radial uses the variation at the station.

### 1.6 Compass north and deviation

**Compass north** (CN) is where the aircraft's own compass actually points. It differs from magnetic north because the aircraft has magnetic fields of its own, from ferrous parts and electrical currents. Unlike magnetic north, compass north depends on the aircraft and on its heading. `061.01.03.01.06`

**Deviation** is the angle between magnetic north and compass north, named **East (+)** if compass north lies east of magnetic north and **West (−)** if west. Magnetic = Compass + East deviation; Magnetic = Compass − West deviation. Deviation changes with the aircraft's heading and with electrical load, so it is measured in a *compass swing* and listed on a deviation card for each heading. Swing the compass again after major modifications or avionics changes, after a lightning strike, and after moving to a base at a very different magnetic latitude. `061.01.03.01.07`

The two corrections chain together from the compass to true: **C → D → M → V → T** (Compass, Deviation, Magnetic, Variation, True), adding East and subtracting West at each step. Going the other way, from true to compass, reverse the signs.

![True, magnetic and compass north: variation 8° W, deviation 3° E](img/gn-north.jpg)

> **Worked example · C–D–M–V–T.** Compass heading 270°, deviation 2° E, variation 4° W. Magnetic heading = 270 + 2 = **272°**. True heading = 272 − 4 = **268°**.

> **Exam trap.** Deviation depends on the aircraft's heading; variation depends on the aircraft's position. Never apply variation to a drift angle: drift is an angle between two directions, not a direction.

### 1.7 Heading, track and drift

The **heading** is where the nose points; the **track** is the path over the ground. The wind makes them differ. **Drift** is the angle between the heading and the track made good: the aircraft is blown downwind, and drift is named right (starboard) or left (port). The **wind correction angle** (WCA) is the angle between the track you want and the heading you must fly to make it good; it is applied *into* the wind. In a steady wind the WCA has the same size as the drift it cancels. With the wind from the left, the drift is to the right, so the heading must be to the left of the track. `061.01.03.02.02`

So track made good = heading + right drift, or heading − left drift. Use true heading with true track, magnetic with magnetic. `061.01.03.02.03`

> **Worked example · track from heading.** Heading 075° T, drift 7° left. Track = 075 − 7 = **068° T**.

How big is the drift? It depends on the **crosswind component** (XWC), the part of the wind blowing across the track: XWC = wind speed × sin(wind angle), where the wind angle is between the wind direction and the track or runway. For mental dead reckoning (MDR), use the **clock code**: treat the angle as minutes of an hour, so 10° ≈ 1/6 of the wind, 20° ≈ 1/3, 30° ≈ 1/2, 40° ≈ 2/3, 50° ≈ 5/6, and 60° or more ≈ the full wind. Use the same reference for both directions: true wind with true track, magnetic wind with a runway. `061.01.03.02.01`

> **Worked example · crosswind.** Track 090°, W/V 130/30. Angle 40° → XWC ≈ 2/3 × 30 = **20 kt** from the right (exactly 30 × sin 40° = 19.3 kt). The clock code slightly overestimates around 40–50°.

The **track error** (TKE) is the angle between the required track and the track actually made good, named by which side of the planned track the aircraft has gone. It appears whenever the WCA you flew does not match the real drift: a wrong forecast wind, or a heading flown carelessly. `061.01.03.02.04`

> **Worked example · track error.** Required track 100°. You fly heading 094° (WCA 6° left), but the actual drift is only 2° right. Track made good = 094 + 2 = 096°. TKE = **4° left**.

### 1.8 Off-track corrections: the 1 in 60 rule

The **1 in 60 rule** says that 1° of angle corresponds to 1 NM of offset for every 60 NM of distance. It is accurate enough for angles up to about 20° and lets you correct a track error in your head. At a fix that shows you off track:

- **Track error** (TE) = off-track distance × 60 / distance *flown*.
- **Closing angle** (CA) = off-track distance × 60 / distance *to go*.
- To fly **direct to the next waypoint**, alter heading by **TE + CA** towards the track.
- To **regain the track** in the same distance as already flown, alter by **2 × TE**, then turn back by TE once on track.

`061.01.03.02.05`

![One-in-sixty rule: track error and closing angle](img/gn-1in60.jpg)

> **Worked example · 1 in 60.** After 40 NM you are 4 NM right of track, with 60 NM to go. TE = 4 × 60 / 40 = 6°; CA = 4 × 60 / 60 = 4°. Turn **10° left** to reach the waypoint.

A fix also tells you the **actual drift**. If you know the heading you flew, the track made good from the last fix gives the drift: drift = track made good − heading, and the track made good is the required track corrected by TE. If you were already applying a WCA, the actual drift is not simply the TE. The new heading must allow for the actual drift as well as the closing angle. `061.01.03.02.06`

> **Worked example · average drift.** Heading 090° with no WCA. After 30 NM the fix is 3 NM left of the 090° track: TE = 3 × 60 / 30 = 6° left, so track made good = 084° and the average drift is **6° left**.

> **Exam trap.** Distance flown goes with the track error; distance to go goes with the closing angle. Swapping them is the classic mistake.

### Check yourself

1. Which forces shape the geoid, and what is WGS-84? *Gravity and the centrifugal force of rotation only. WGS-84 is the reference ellipsoid ICAO requires for aeronautical coordinates.*
2. How long is 1° of latitude, and how far is it from the equator to a pole? *60 NM; 5400 NM.*
3. Find d.long from 175° W to 165° E. *Opposite hemispheres: 175 + 165 = 340°, more than 180°, so d.long = 20° W.*
4. Compass heading 140°, deviation 3° W, variation 6° E. What is the true heading? *Magnetic 137°, true 143°.*
5. Track 330°, W/V 300/40. What crosswind component, and which way is the drift? *Angle 30°, so about 20 kt from the left; drift is to the right.*
6. After 45 NM you are 3 NM left of track with 75 NM to go. What heading change takes you direct to the waypoint? *TE 4°, CA 2.4°, so about 6° right.*
7. Why is the Sun's declination never more than 23½°? *Because the spin axis is tilted 23½° from the perpendicular to the ecliptic.*
