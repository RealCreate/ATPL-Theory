## Chapter 5 · Charts

A chart flattens a curved Earth, and no flat chart can show everything correctly at once. Each projection keeps some properties and gives up others. This chapter sets out what ICAO asks of an aeronautical chart, how chart convergency and scale work, the three projections in the exam (Mercator, Lambert conformal conic and polar stereographic), and how to measure tracks and plot radio bearings on them.

### 5.1 What ICAO requires

ICAO Annex 4 requires aeronautical charts to be **conformal**: angles and shapes are correct over small areas, and the scale at any point is the same in every direction, so bearings and tracks can be measured correctly. Where practical, a **straight line should approximate a great circle**. This is why the Lambert conformal conic projection is the standard for en-route charts in middle latitudes, and the polar stereographic at high latitudes. No flat chart can be both conformal and equal-area. `061.04.01.01.01`

### 5.2 Projections and how to recognise them

Charts are made by projecting the graticule onto a surface that can be laid flat. `061.04.02.01.01`

- **Azimuthal (plane).** The surface is a plane touching the Earth, usually at a pole, as in the polar stereographic. Meridians are straight lines radiating from the pole; parallels are concentric circles.
- **Cylindrical.** The surface is a cylinder, usually touching the equator, as in the Mercator. Meridians are parallel straight lines; parallels are straight lines too.
- **Conical.** The surface is a cone touching the Earth along one parallel, or cutting it along two, as in the Lambert. Meridians are straight lines converging towards the apex; parallels are arcs of circles.

So the graticule gives the projection away: parallel meridians mean cylindrical, converging straight meridians with arcs for parallels mean conical, and radial meridians with circular parallels mean azimuthal.

### 5.3 Chart convergency and the constant of the cone

On a conical chart the meridians are straight lines meeting at the apex of the cone, and the angle between two of them is the **chart convergency**:

**Chart convergency = d.long × n**, where **n = sin (parallel of origin)**

n is the **constant of the cone**. The *parallel of origin* is where the cone touches the Earth, or, for a cone cutting the Earth along two standard parallels, approximately midway between them. The same formula covers the other two projections as limits: the polar stereographic is a cone flattened into a plane, with n = sin 90° = 1, and the Mercator is a cone opened into a cylinder, with n = 0. Because n is constant over the whole chart, chart convergency depends **only on d.long**, not on latitude. `061.04.01.02.01`

> **Worked example · constant of the cone.** Parallel of origin 48°: n = sin 48° = 0.743. Between meridians 20° apart, chart convergency = 20 × 0.743 = **14.9°**.

A straight line on a chart represents a great circle exactly only where **chart convergency = Earth convergency**. On a Lambert chart this happens at the parallel of origin, where sin(parallel of origin) = sin(latitude); elsewhere the straight line is a very close approximation, and the true great circle is a slight curve concave towards the parallel of origin. On a polar stereographic chart, chart convergency = d.long while Earth convergency = d.long × sin latitude, so they match exactly only at the pole and closely at high latitudes. On a Mercator chart the chart convergency is zero, so straight lines are rhumb lines. `061.04.01.02.02`

> **Key numbers · convergency on charts**
>
> | Chart | n | Chart convergency | Straight line is |
> | --- | --- | --- | --- |
> | Mercator | 0 | 0 | a rhumb line |
> | Lambert | sin parallel of origin | d.long × n | ≈ great circle (exact at parallel of origin) |
> | Polar stereographic | 1 | d.long | ≈ great circle (exact at the pole) |
> | The Earth itself | — | d.long × sin mean lat | — |

### 5.4 Scale

**Scale** is chart length divided by Earth distance. It can be given as a **representative fraction** (1:500 000), as a **graduated scale line**, or as a **statement** ("1 cm = 5 km"). On Mercator and Lambert charts the latitude graduations along a meridian also work as a scale, since 1' of latitude = 1 NM. A *larger* scale, such as 1:250 000, shows more detail over a smaller area than a smaller scale such as 1:1 000 000. `061.04.01.03.01`

Earth distance = chart distance × the scale denominator. Keep the units straight: 1 km = 100 000 cm. Common scales are 1:500 000 for VFR charts and 1:1 000 000 for the ICAO World Aeronautical Chart. `061.04.01.03.02`

> **Worked example · scale.** At 1:500 000, 12 cm on the chart = 12 × 500 000 = 6 000 000 cm = 60 km = **32.4 NM**. In reverse, 100 NM at 1:1 000 000 = 185.2 km = **18.5 cm**.

### 5.5 Direct Mercator

The direct Mercator is a cylinder touching the equator, made conformal mathematically. Its properties follow from that. `061.04.02.03.01`

- Meridians are parallel, equally spaced straight lines; parallels are straight lines, spaced further apart towards the poles.
- Chart convergency is 0, which is correct only at the equator.
- **Rhumb lines are straight**; great circles are curves **concave to the equator**, bulging towards the pole.
- The scale is correct at the equator (or at a stated latitude) and expands with **sec latitude**; the poles cannot be shown.

To plot a radio bearing (a great circle) on a Mercator chart, apply the conversion angle first (Chapter 4).

Because the scale grows as sec latitude, the representative fraction changes across the chart: **denominator at latitude 2 = denominator at latitude 1 × cos lat 2 / cos lat 1**. A higher latitude means a larger scale and a smaller denominator. `061.04.02.03.02`

> **Worked example · Mercator scale.** 1:1 000 000 at the equator. At 60° N: 1 000 000 × cos 60° / cos 0° = **1:500 000**.

So a given length on the chart represents **less Earth distance at higher latitude**: Earth distance ∝ cos latitude. Measure distances on a Mercator chart with the latitude scale at the mid-latitude of the line. `061.04.02.03.03`

> **Worked example · same length, different distance.** 10 cm represents 100 NM at 30° N. At 60° N it represents 100 × cos 60° / cos 30° = **57.7 NM**.

### 5.6 Lambert conformal conic

The Lambert is a cone that cuts the Earth along **two standard parallels**, made conformal. `061.04.02.04.01`

- Meridians are straight lines converging towards the apex (near the pole); parallels are arcs of concentric circles.
- Chart convergency = d.long × sin(parallel of origin), constant across the chart and correct at the parallel of origin, which lies roughly midway between the standard parallels.
- A **straight line ≈ a great circle**, good enough for navigation.
- The scale is correct on the standard parallels, slightly contracted between them (least at the parallel of origin), and expanded outside them.
- Rhumb lines are curves concave to the pole.

It is the standard ICAO chart in middle latitudes, used for Jeppesen en-route charts and the ICAO 1:500 000 chart.

![Lambert conformal conic chart of Europe: converging meridians, standard parallels, a straight-line track and how its true track changes (real coastlines)](img/gn-lambert.jpg)

The scale behaves in a set pattern. It is exactly as stated **on the two standard parallels**; slightly **smaller between them**, smallest at the parallel of origin; and it **increases outside them**, with distance from the parallel of origin. If the standard parallels are no more than about two-thirds of the chart's latitude range apart, the scale anywhere between them differs from the stated scale by **less than 1 %**, so for practical navigation it is treated as constant. `061.04.02.04.03`

The true track of a straight line on a Lambert chart changes by the **chart convergency, d.long × n**, which depends only on the d.long and n, not on the latitudes of the points. `061.04.02.04.02`

> **Worked example · Lambert track change.** n = 0.75. A straight line from 010° W, initial track 060° T, to 020° E: d.long 30°, change = 30 × 0.75 = 22.5°, so the final track is **082.5° T** (northern hemisphere, eastbound: increasing).

Turned around, the same relationship finds the unknown. If the track change of a straight line and the d.long are known, **n = chart convergency / d.long** and the parallel of origin = arcsin n. Use the chart convergency here, not the Earth convergency. `061.04.02.04.05`

> **Worked example · parallel of origin.** A straight-line track changes by 18° over 24° of d.long. n = 18 / 24 = 0.75, so the parallel of origin ≈ **48.6°**.

On a printed Lambert chart, measure the angle between two meridians (the chart convergency) and divide by their d.long to get n; or read the standard parallels from the legend and take the parallel of origin as about midway between them. Meridians 10° apart that meet at 7.5° give n = 0.75, a parallel of origin of about 48.6°. `061.04.02.04.06`

And with n and the two tracks known, **d.long = (final track − initial track) / n**. In the northern hemisphere, an increasing track means the second point is east of the first. `061.04.02.04.07`

> **Worked example · d.long from track change.** n = 0.6; the straight-line track is 075° T at A and 093° T at B. Change 18°, so d.long = 18 / 0.6 = **30°**, and B is east of A.

For two positions, the rhumb-line track is the average of the initial and final great-circle tracks: initial GC = RL ∓ CA and final GC = RL ± CA, with CA = ½ × d.long × sin mean latitude. In the northern hemisphere, eastbound, initial GC < RL < final GC; westbound the tracks decrease. `061.04.02.04.04`

> **Worked example · tracks between two positions.** Initial GC track 050° T, final GC track 070° T. RL track = **060° T**, convergency 20°, CA **10°**.

### 5.7 Polar stereographic

The polar stereographic is projected from the opposite pole onto a plane touching the Earth at a pole. `061.04.02.02.01`

- It is conformal; meridians are straight lines radiating from the pole and parallels are concentric circles.
- Chart convergency = d.long (n = 1), correct at the pole and close at high latitudes.
- A **straight line ≈ a great circle**, very nearly exact near the pole.
- The scale is correct at the pole and **expands away from it**: at 70° it is about 3 % larger. It is used above about 70° latitude.
- Rhumb lines are curves concave to the pole.

True direction changes very quickly near the pole, so polar charts usually carry a grid overlay for *grid navigation*.

![Polar stereographic chart of the Arctic: a straight-line track whose true track changes by exactly the d.long (real coastlines)](img/gn-polar.jpg)

Because chart convergency = d.long, the true track of a straight line changes by **exactly the d.long** between its ends. On a Lambert it changes by d.long × n, and on a Mercator not at all. `061.04.02.02.02`

> **Worked example · polar track change.** A straight line from 70° N 020° W, initial track 080° T, to a point at 030° E: d.long 50°, eastbound in the northern hemisphere, so the final track is 080 + 50 = **130° T**.

### 5.8 Symbols, measuring and plotting

Learn to recognise the standard **ICAO Annex 4 symbols**: VOR (a hexagon, usually inside a compass rose), DME (a square), VOR/DME (a hexagon inside a square), VORTAC, NDB (a circle of dots), aerodromes (civil, military or joint, with or without hard runways), obstacles (single, group, lit), airspace boundaries (controlled airspace, and prohibited P, restricted R and danger D areas), reporting points (a **filled triangle is compulsory**, an open one on request), spot heights and maximum elevation figures. A P area is prohibited, an R area restricted under conditions, and a D area one where hazardous activity may take place. `061.04.03.01.01`

To **measure a track**, align the protractor with a meridian and measure at the mid-meridian on a Lambert chart. To **measure a distance**, use dividers against the latitude scale near the mid-latitude, or the chart's scale line. Convert true to magnetic with the variation at mid-track. IFR en-route charts print magnetic tracks and distances along the airways; VFR charts usually require you to measure true tracks yourself. `061.04.03.02.01`

To **fix with VOR and DME**: a VOR radial is a magnetic bearing *from* the station, so convert it to true with the variation **at the station** and draw it from the station. A DME range is an arc around the station; it is slant range, which matters only close to the station at height. A radial and a DME range from the same station give a rho-theta fix; two radials, or two DME ranges, also fix the position. `061.04.03.02.02`

An **NDB bearing** is measured at the aircraft, so it uses the variation **at the aircraft**. The ADF gives a relative bearing (RB): QDM, the magnetic bearing to the station, = MH + RB, and the true bearing to the station = TH + RB. The bearing to plot from the station is that ± 180°, but it was measured at the aircraft's meridian. On a Lambert chart, apply the chart convergency between the aircraft's and the station's meridians before plotting it from the station. Convergency can be ignored only if both meridians are the same; on a Mercator chart use the conversion angle instead. `061.04.03.02.03`

> **Worked example · NDB bearing.** TH 040°, RB 300°. True bearing to the station = 040 + 300 = 340° T at the aircraft, so the bearing from the station is 160° T measured at the **aircraft's** meridian. Apply the convergency to transfer it to the station's meridian before drawing it.

> **Exam trap.** VOR: variation at the station. NDB: variation at the aircraft. MH + RB = QDM; TH + RB = true bearing to the station.

### Check yourself

1. Why does ICAO want charts to be conformal? *So that angles, and therefore bearings and tracks, are correct.*
2. What is the constant of the cone for a Lambert chart with standard parallels 37° N and 65° N? *Parallel of origin about 51°, n ≈ 0.78.*
3. On a polar stereographic chart a straight line runs from 75° N 060° W to 80° N 040° E. By how much does its true track change? *By the d.long, 100°.*
4. A Mercator chart has a scale of 1:2 000 000 at 30° N. What is the scale at 50° N? *2 000 000 × cos 50° / cos 30° ≈ 1:1 484 000.*
5. Where is the scale of a Lambert chart smallest? *At the parallel of origin, between the standard parallels.*
6. Which variation do you use for a VOR radial, and for an NDB bearing? *Station variation for the VOR; aircraft variation for the NDB.*
7. On a Lambert chart a straight line's track changes from 070° to 088° over 25° of d.long. What is n, and the parallel of origin? *n = 0.72, parallel of origin ≈ 46°.*
8. On which chart is a straight line a rhumb line? *The Mercator.*
