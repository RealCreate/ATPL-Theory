## Chapter 3 · Visual Navigation

Visual navigation is dead reckoning checked against the ground. The pilot flies an accurate heading and keeps time, and map reading confirms or corrects the result. This chapter covers what makes a good landmark, how the view changes at low level and at night, how terrain is shown on a chart, and what to do when a checkpoint fails to appear or you are no longer sure where you are.

### 3.1 What makes a good landmark

A ground feature is useful for navigation when it is **unique**, so that it cannot be confused with a similar one nearby; **large or prominent**; **in contrast** with its surroundings; and **shown accurately** on the chart. *Line features* such as coastlines, large rivers, motorways and railways make good collecting features, because you cannot miss crossing them. *Point features* such as towns with a distinctive shape, lakes, dams, masts, bridges and junctions give pinpoints. A combination is best: a railway crossing a river beside a town is far more certain than the town alone. Small roads and villages change faster than charts are revised. `061.02.01.01.01`

### 3.2 Low level, poor visibility and night

At **low level** the field of view is small, features pass quickly and are seen at a shallow angle, and terrain can hide landmarks until the last moment. Visibility is cut by haze, mist, fog, precipitation, low cloud and smoke, and by **sun glare**, which is worst when flying towards a low sun and makes haze look much thicker. The workload is higher too, with obstacles, airspace and terrain to watch. In poor visibility features appear late, so plan with large, linear features and keep to DR timing. `061.02.01.02.01`

At **night**, unlit ground and water are invisible. Navigation relies on lit features: towns, major roads with traffic, aerodrome beacons and lighthouses, and coastlines under moonlight. Town lights can look closer than they are and distances are hard to judge. Cloud and high ground are hard to see, so the minimum safe altitude matters even more; large dark areas such as lakes, forests or the sea can be mistaken for cloud, and the other way round. Plan the route along well-lit features and radio aids. `061.02.01.02.02`

### 3.3 Map reading and checkpoints

**Map reading** is finding the aircraft's position by matching features on the ground with features on the chart. Work **from map to ground**: use the DR position and the chart to predict what should appear and when, then confirm it outside. Working the other way, seeing something and searching the map for it, easily leads to a false identification. `061.02.02.01.01`

A **visual checkpoint** is an easily identified ground feature on or near the route, chosen during planning to check position and timing, and marked on the chart with its planned time. For VFR flight, checkpoints every 10–15 minutes are typical. `061.02.02.01.02` A good checkpoint is unmistakable, visible from a distance and from the planned altitude, and does not depend on the season or the light: a river junction, a motorway interchange, a large lake, a town of characteristic shape next to a railway, a prominent peak or a headland. Avoid single small features such as a farm or a minor road, and pick a line feature just beyond each checkpoint as a backstop in case you miss it. `061.02.02.01.03`

At each checkpoint, comparing the DR position with the actual position shows how the real wind differs from the forecast. Use the difference to revise heading (the 1 in 60 corrections of Chapter 1) and ground speed for the next leg, and to update the ETAs. A consistent drift to one side means the wind is wrong: correct the heading, not just the position. `061.02.02.01.04`

### 3.4 Position lines and fixes

A **position line** is a line on which the aircraft is known to be at a given time: a line through a feature in a measured direction, a coastline, a railway being crossed. Two or more position lines that cross give a **fix**. The fix is most accurate when the lines cross at about 90°; three lines usually form a small triangle, a *cocked hat*, whose size shows the error. If the lines were not observed at the same time, move the earlier one along the track by the distance flown since, to make a *running fix*. `061.02.02.01.05`

A **single position line** cannot fix the position, but it can still check progress. A line **across** the track, such as a railway at right angles to the route, checks the ground speed and ETA. A line **along** the track checks track-keeping, that is drift. `061.02.02.01.06`

### 3.5 Preparing the chart

Before flight, draw the tracks; mark distance or time ticks along them (every 10 NM or every 5 minutes, say); and add headings, checkpoints, the minimum safe altitude, frequencies and airspace. In flight, hold the chart **track-up**, so that features left and right of the line on the chart are left and right outside, and fold it so the current leg is visible. `061.02.02.01.07`

### 3.6 Visual navigation techniques

Several techniques work together. `061.02.02.01.08`

- Use the DR position to know **where to look and when**.
- Identify a charted feature by **several characteristics** at once: its shape, its size and its relation to other features.
- Allow for **season and weather**: snow hides roads and small lakes, dry seasons shrink rivers, haze hides low features, lakes freeze and look like fields, and flooded fields look like lakes.
- **Distance from successive bearings.** When the relative bearing of a landmark doubles, say from 45° to 90° off the nose, the distance flown between the two sightings equals the distance from the landmark at the second sighting ("doubling the angle on the bow").
- **Distance from the sighting angle.** Horizontal distance ≈ height / tan(depression angle). At 45° below the horizon the distance equals the height; by the 1 in 60 rule, a feature 6° below the horizon from 3000 ft is about 5 NM away.

If there is **no checkpoint at a turning point**, or it cannot be identified, turn on the ETA onto the next heading and confirm with the next feature, ideally a line feature as a backstop. Do not orbit searching for the turning point: that destroys the DR. `061.02.02.01.09`

Map reading has **limits**. It is difficult over featureless terrain (desert, forest, sea, snowfields), over areas full of similar features (many lakes, uniform farmland), and where charts are inaccurate or out of date. Mountains hide features and add turbulence. In such areas rely on DR, timing and radio aids, and plan more fuel and more frequent radio fixes. `061.02.02.01.10`

The standard VFR method combines everything: fly the planned heading accurately, note the time at each checkpoint, compare with the plan, correct the heading with the 1 in 60 rule, and revise ETAs and fuel. The order is **clock, map, ground**: look at the clock, find on the map where you should be, then find it on the ground. Accurate heading and timing make visual navigation work; map reading confirms it. `061.02.02.01.14`

### 3.7 Terrain on the chart

**Contour lines** join points of equal elevation above MSL. Close spacing means a steep slope, wide spacing a gentle one, and closed rings are hills (or hollows, if marked). The contour interval is given in the legend. Contours show the shape of the ground; *spot heights* mark the highest points. `061.02.02.01.11`

**Layer tinting** colours the areas between contours with a graded scale, typically green for low ground through yellow to brown for high ground, so the relief is visible at a glance. The legend gives the height band of each colour. Layer tints show terrain only; they do not show obstacles such as masts or buildings standing above it. `061.02.02.01.12`

From the contours you can picture the ground. In a **valley** the contours form Vs pointing **upstream**, towards higher ground; on a **ridge or spur** the Vs point **downhill**. A **saddle** or pass lies between two closed high areas; contours that merge mark a **cliff**; a wide flat area inside a high contour is a **plateau**. `061.02.02.01.13`

> **Exam trap.** In a valley the V of the contours points uphill; on a spur it points downhill.

### 3.8 Diversion, uncertain of position, lost

A **diversion** starts at once: choose the new destination, estimate the track and distance by eye or with a ruler, turn onto an approximate heading straight away (over a known point if possible), then refine the heading, time and fuel, and tell ATC.

When **uncertain of position**, keep flying the heading, check the DR, look for a line feature and use radio aids. Think of a *circle of uncertainty* around the DR position, with a radius of about 10 % of the distance flown since the last fix.

When **lost**, tell someone early: ATC, or 121.5 MHz with a PAN call. Climb if it is safe to do so, for better radio and visual range; conserve fuel; ask for radar or VDF help; and land before the fuel or the daylight runs out. Never descend below the safe altitude to look for your position. `061.02.02.02.01`

### Check yourself

1. Why work from map to ground rather than ground to map? *Predicting what should appear from the DR position avoids identifying the wrong feature.*
2. A railway lies at right angles across your track. What does crossing it tell you? *Your progress along track (GS and ETA), but not your position across track.*
3. A church is 30° off the nose at 1012 and 60° off the nose at 1018, at GS 120 kt. How far away is it at 1018? *The angle doubled, so the distance equals the distance flown: 6 min at 2 NM/min, 12 NM.*
4. From 2000 ft, a mast is 10° below the horizon. Roughly how far away is it? *By the 1 in 60 rule, distance ≈ height × 60 / angle = 2000 × 60 / 10 = 12 000 ft, about 2 NM.*
5. Contours form a series of Vs pointing towards lower ground. What is the feature? *A spur or ridge.*
6. The planned turning point cannot be found. What do you do? *Turn onto the next heading at the ETA, then confirm with the next feature or a backstop line feature.*
7. What radius of uncertainty would you assume 50 NM after the last fix? *About 5 NM.*
