## Chapter 2 · Distance, Speed and Dead Reckoning

With a grid and a north in place, navigation becomes arithmetic: how far, how fast, and therefore where and when. This chapter covers the units of distance, distances along the grid, airspeeds and Mach number, wind components and ground speed, the triangle of velocities, dead reckoning, and the rules of thumb for climbs and descents. Almost every calculation question in the exam is built from these pieces.

### 2.1 The nautical mile

The international **nautical mile** is exactly **1852 m**. It is the average length of one minute of latitude on the WGS-84 ellipsoid. Because the ellipsoid is flatter near the poles, a minute of latitude there is slightly longer (about 1861 m) than at the equator (about 1843 m), but for navigation 1' of latitude = 1 NM, and 1° of latitude = 60 NM anywhere. `061.01.04.01.01`

At the equator, which is a great circle, **1' of longitude is also about 1 NM**. Away from the equator the meridians close in, and one minute of longitude covers only cos latitude NM: about 0.5 NM at 60°, and nothing at the poles. Never treat minutes of d.long as nautical miles except on the equator. `061.01.04.01.02`

### 2.2 Units of distance

You need to move between five units without hesitation. `061.01.04.02.01`

> **Key numbers · distance units**
>
> | Unit | Equals |
> | --- | --- |
> | 1 NM | 1852 m = 6076 ft ≈ 1.15 SM |
> | 1 SM (statute mile) | 1609 m = 5280 ft |
> | 1 km | 0.54 NM |
> | 1 m | 3.28 ft |
> | 1 ft | 0.3048 m = 12 in |
> | 1 in | 2.54 cm |

> **Worked example · conversions.** 250 km = 250 / 1.852 = **135 NM**. 3000 m = 3000 × 3.28 = **9843 ft**.

> **Exam trap.** km → NM: divide by 1.852 (or multiply by 0.54). NM → SM: multiply by 1.15. METAR visibility and RVR are in metres, cloud bases in feet.

### 2.3 Distances on the graticule

Three kinds of graticule distance appear in the exam. `061.01.04.03.01`

- **Along a meridian**, distance in NM = d.lat in minutes.
- **Across a pole**, between points on opposite (antipodal) meridians, where d.long = 180°, the great-circle route goes over the pole. Distance = (90° − lat A) + (90° − lat B), in degrees × 60.
- **Along a parallel**, the rhumb line east–west, the distance is the **departure**: departure (NM) = d.long (minutes) × cos latitude.

Working backwards gives a new position: d.lat (') = NM north or south; d.long (') = NM east or west ÷ cos latitude. If the route changes latitude, use the mean latitude.

![Departure: the same d.long covers less distance at higher latitude, departure = d.long × cos lat](img/gn-departure.jpg)

> **Worked example · departure.** Fly 600 NM east along 60° N. d.long = 600 / cos 60° = 600 / 0.5 = 1200' = **20°**.

> **Worked example · across the pole.** From 70° N 010° E to 75° N 170° W. d.long = 180°, so the great circle crosses the pole: (90 − 70) + (90 − 75) = 35° = **2100 NM**. Along the parallel the same journey would be far longer, which is why polar routes exist.

### 2.4 Air distance and ground distance

Distance measured through the moving air mass is **air distance**, in nautical air miles (NAM). With a headwind the aircraft must fly more air miles to cover a given ground distance; with a tailwind, fewer. Flying higher raises the TAS for the same IAS and usually improves the specific range, but the ratio of air to ground distance depends only on TAS and ground speed. Range and fuel tables in aircraft manuals are often in NAM, so they must be converted with the wind. `061.01.04.04.01`

Time is the same in both frames: t = NM / GS = NAM / TAS. Therefore **NAM = NM × TAS / GS**, and NM = NAM × GS / TAS. `061.01.04.04.02`

> **Worked example · NAM.** 500 NM over the ground, TAS 450 kt, 50 kt headwind so GS 400 kt. NAM = 500 × 450 / 400 = **562.5 NAM**. A headwind must always give the larger number.

### 2.5 True airspeed and Mach number

The airspeed indicator measures dynamic pressure, so it reads correctly only at sea-level density. Corrected for instrument and position errors it gives the **calibrated airspeed** (CAS). The **true airspeed** (TAS), the actual speed through the air, is CAS corrected for density, that is for pressure altitude and temperature. On a mechanical computer (CRP or E6B), set the outside air temperature against the pressure altitude in the airspeed window, then read TAS on the outer scale opposite CAS on the inner scale. The rule of thumb is **TAS ≈ CAS + 2 % per 1000 ft** in ISA conditions. Above about FL 250 or M 0.5, compressibility makes the rule and the simple computer setting less accurate, and a compressibility correction or the Mach number is used. `061.01.05.01.01`

> **Worked example · TAS rule of thumb.** CAS 150 kt at FL 100: 10 × 2 % = 20 %, so TAS ≈ 150 × 1.20 = **180 kt**. If the air is warmer than ISA, the TAS is a little higher.

The **Mach number** is TAS divided by the local speed of sound (LSS). The speed of sound depends **only on temperature**: **LSS = 38.95 × √T** kt, with T in kelvin. In ISA at sea level (288 K) LSS ≈ 661 kt; at the tropopause (−56.5 °C, 216.5 K) LSS ≈ 573 kt. So TAS = M × LSS, and the same TAS gives a higher Mach number in colder air. `061.01.05.02.01`

> **Worked example · TAS from Mach.** M 0.80 at −50 °C: T = 223 K, LSS = 38.95 × √223 ≈ 582 kt, TAS = 0.80 × 582 ≈ **466 kt**.

![Airspeeds: IAS to CAS to EAS to TAS, and how TAS and Mach change in a climb at constant CAS then constant Mach](img/gn-airspeed.jpg)

How the three speeds move in a climb or descent follows from these two relationships. `061.01.05.03.01`

- **Climbing at constant CAS**: density falls, so TAS rises. Temperature falls too, so the speed of sound falls and the Mach number rises even faster than TAS.
- **Climbing at constant Mach** below the tropopause: the temperature, and so the LSS, falls, so TAS falls; CAS falls too.
- **Constant Mach above the tropopause**, in isothermal air: LSS is constant, so TAS is constant, while CAS keeps falling.

Jets therefore climb at a constant CAS and change to a constant Mach number at the **crossover altitude**, so that they do not run into the Mach limit (MMO). In the descent the order is reversed: constant Mach first, then constant CAS, and as CAS rises in the descent at constant Mach, the pilot watches VMO below the crossover.

### 2.6 Wind components and ground speed

The part of the wind along the track is the **headwind** or **tailwind component**: HWC or TWC = wind speed × cos(wind angle). Quick values: cos 30° ≈ 0.9, cos 45° ≈ 0.7, cos 60° = 0.5. A wind less than 90° from the nose is a headwind, more than 90° a tailwind; at 60° the headwind is only half the wind, at 90° nothing. `061.01.05.04.01`

> **Worked example · components.** Track 360°, W/V 030/40. Angle 30°: HWC = 40 × 0.87 ≈ **35 kt**, XWC = 40 × 0.5 = **20 kt**.

For small drift angles, **GS ≈ TAS − HWC** or TAS + TWC, and in reverse TAS ≈ GS + HWC. Ground speed is used for time and fuel; TAS for air distance. `061.01.05.04.02`

There is a catch with strong crosswinds. When the aircraft crabs into wind, only **TAS × cos WCA** acts along the track; this is the *effective TAS*. With a pure crosswind, GS = TAS × cos WCA, which is less than TAS. At a WCA of 20°, cos 20° = 0.94, a 6 % loss. A 90° crosswind therefore still reduces the ground speed, which matters most for slow aircraft in strong winds. `061.01.05.04.03`

### 2.7 The triangle of velocities

The **triangle of velocities** (TOV) adds two vectors to give a third. The **air vector** is true heading and TAS, drawn with one arrowhead. The **wind vector** is the wind velocity, drawn *downwind* with three arrowheads (a wind from 270° is drawn pointing east). The **ground vector**, track and GS with two arrowheads, closes the triangle from the start of the air vector to the end of the wind vector. Air vector + wind vector = ground vector, and the angle between the air and ground vectors is the drift. Any two complete vectors define the third. `061.01.06.01.01`

![Triangle of velocities: heading and TAS, wind, track and GS, and drift](img/gn-tov.jpg)

There are three standard problems. `061.01.06.02.01`

1. **Heading and GS** (pre-flight planning), from track, TAS and W/V. On the computer: mark the wind down from the centre, set the *track* at the index, slide TAS under the centre, read the WCA and GS. By MDR: WCA = XWC / SF and GS ≈ TAS − HWC.
2. **The W/V** (in flight), from heading, TAS, track and GS. On the computer: set the heading, TAS at the centre, mark the track and GS point, then rotate to read the wind.
3. **Track and GS** (dead reckoning), from heading, TAS and W/V. On the computer: set the *heading*, mark the wind, read drift and GS; track = heading ± drift.

Problem 1 starts from the track and problem 3 from the heading. Mixing them up gives a drift error of up to twice the WCA. Check the answer: the heading must point into wind, and a headwind must lower the ground speed.

On the computer, the GS solution works the same way: wind direction under the index, wind speed marked up from the centre, track set, TAS under the centre, read drift and GS under the wind mark. By MDR, find the XWC with the clock code, WCA = XWC / SF with SF = TAS / 60, and GS = TAS × cos WCA − HWC. `061.01.05.04.04`

> **Worked example · MDR ground speed.** TAS 180 kt (SF 3), track 090°, W/V 060/30. Angle 30°: XWC ≈ 15 kt, so WCA = 15 / 3 = **5° left**; HWC ≈ 26 kt, so GS ≈ 180 − 26 ≈ **154 kt**.

### 2.8 Ground speed, distance and time

Distance = GS × time, so time = distance / GS and GS = distance / time. Working in minutes: time (min) = distance × 60 / GS. Take care converting decimals of an hour: 1.4 h is 1 h 24 min, not 1 h 40 min. `061.01.05.04.05`

> **Worked example · time.** 175 NM at GS 210 kt: 175 × 60 / 210 = **50 min**.

To arrive at a waypoint at a set time, the required GS = distance to go / time available; then find the TAS needed from the wind component, and check it is within the speed range and the fuel plan. This is the basis of time-controlled arrivals. `061.01.05.04.06`

> **Worked example · revised GS.** 120 NM to go, and the waypoint must be reached in 25 min: GS = 120 × 60 / 25 = **288 kt**.

Two fixes give the **average GS**: the distance between them divided by the time between them. Use the measured distance between the fixes, not the planned leg length. With the heading and TAS, the average GS and track also give the actual wind. `061.01.05.04.07`

> **Worked example · average GS.** Fixes 42 NM apart at 1012 and 1024 UTC (12 min): GS = 42 × 60 / 12 = **210 kt**.

In flight the **flight log** is kept up to date. At each waypoint record the actual time of arrival (ATA), work out the actual GS and wind, revise the ETAs of the following legs (ATA + leg time at the new GS), and compare the fuel remaining with the plan. Time gained or lost × fuel flow = fuel gained or lost. Update the expected landing fuel and compare it with final reserve plus alternate fuel. The trend of the fuel deviation tells you more than a single reading. `061.01.05.05.01`

### 2.9 Gradients and rates of climb and descent

Taking 1 NM ≈ 6000 ft turns gradients into simple rules based on the 1 in 60 rule:

- gradient (°) ≈ (height difference (ft) / 100) / distance (NM)
- gradient (%) ≈ (height difference (ft) / 60) / distance (NM)
- exactly, gradient (°) = arctan(height (ft) / distance (ft))

So **1° ≈ 100 ft per NM** and **1 % ≈ 60 ft per NM**. A 3° glide path is about 300 ft/NM (exactly 318 ft/NM) and about 5.2 %. Per cent and degrees are not the same: 10 % ≈ 5.7°. `061.01.05.06.01`

The **rate of descent** on a glide path follows: ROD (ft/min) = glide-path angle (°) × GS (NM/min) × 100, or ROD (ft/min) = gradient (%) × GS (kt). For a 3° path, **ROD ≈ GS × 5**. A tailwind on final raises the GS and so the ROD needed to stay on the same path. `061.01.05.06.02`

> **Worked example · ILS.** GS 140 kt on a 3° ILS: ROD ≈ 140 × 5 = **700 ft/min**.

![Glide path, gradient and rate of descent rules of thumb](img/gn-descent.jpg)

In general, **vertical speed (ft/min) = GS (kt) × gradient (ft/NM) / 60**, and gradient (ft/NM) = VS × 60 / GS. Climb gradients in SIDs are over the ground, so a tailwind needs a higher rate of climb. The standard PANS-OPS climb gradient of 3.3 % is about 200 ft/NM. `061.01.05.06.03`

> **Worked example · climb rate.** A climb gradient of 400 ft/NM at GS 180 kt needs VS = 180 × 400 / 60 = **1200 ft/min**.

> **Key numbers · gradients**
>
> | Relationship | Value |
> | --- | --- |
> | 1° | ≈ 100 ft/NM |
> | 1 % | ≈ 60 ft/NM |
> | 3° glide path | ≈ 300 ft/NM (318) ≈ 5.2 % |
> | ROD on 3° | ≈ GS × 5 |
> | 3.3 % climb | ≈ 200 ft/NM |
> | 3° descent distance | ≈ 3 NM per 1000 ft |

Before starting a descent, the aircraft's position must be **known accurately**, because terrain clearance in the descent depends on where the aircraft actually is. Errors are largest after long legs without fixes, or with unreliable aids. Never start a descent below the minimum safe or en-route altitude on dead reckoning alone; in mountains, cross-check two independent sources such as DME and GNSS first. `061.01.05.06.04`

### 2.10 Dead reckoning

A **dead reckoning** (DR) position is worked out from the last fix using heading, TAS and the forecast or last known wind over the time elapsed, or simply track and GS. It is the best estimate of position when no fix is available, and it tells you when landmarks or radio aids should appear. Its accuracy decays with time as wind errors build up. An *air position* uses heading and TAS only, with no wind; a DR position includes the wind. `061.01.07.01.01`

A **fix** is a position found from external references: a visual pinpoint, radio aids or GNSS. Comparing the fix with the DR position shows the error in the assumed wind: the line from the DR position to the fix, divided by the time since the last fix, is the **wind error vector**. Use it to correct heading and GS for the rest of the flight. Repeated errors in the same direction point to a steady wind error rather than poor flying. `061.01.07.01.02`

![Dead reckoning plot: from the fix, the air position and DR position, the actual fix and the wind error vector](img/gn-dr.jpg)

The **speed factor** (SF) is the speed divided by 60: the distance covered in one minute. TAS 120 kt is SF 2, 180 kt is SF 3, GS 420 kt is SF 7. It makes mental DR quick: time (min) = distance / SF. Use TAS for the WCA, GS for time and distance. `061.01.07.01.03`

From the 1 in 60 rule, for small angles **WCA (°) = XWC (kt) / SF**. It holds up to about 15–20°. It also sets a limit: the maximum possible drift is the full wind speed / SF, so a 30 kt wind at SF 3 can never cause more than 10° of drift. `061.01.07.01.04`

> **Worked example · WCA.** TAS 150 kt (SF 2.5), XWC 20 kt: WCA = 20 / 2.5 = **8°**.

### 2.11 Climb and descent planning

The TAS and wind change through a climb or descent, so problems use averages taken at a representative level. In the **climb**, use the TAS at **2/3 of the cruising altitude** (or 2/3 of the altitude change above the departure level), because the rate of climb falls with height and the aircraft spends more time in the upper part. A climb to FL 300 uses the TAS at FL 200. `061.01.08.01.01`

In the **descent**, use the TAS at **1/2 of the descent altitude**, because the rate of descent is roughly constant and the time is spread evenly through the band. A descent from FL 240 to the surface uses the TAS at FL 120; if the descent ends above sea level, take half of the band actually descended. `061.01.08.01.02`

The **wind** is averaged the same way: the W/V at 2/3 of the cruising altitude for the climb, interpolating between forecast levels if necessary, `061.01.08.02.01` and the W/V at 1/2 of the descent altitude for the descent. Strong jet-level tailwinds can greatly lengthen the descent distance, so plan the top of descent with the mid-level wind. `061.01.08.02.02`

Then solve the TOV with the average TAS and W/V for the true track to get the **average GS**, and distance covered = average GS × time in the climb or descent. It is the average GS, not the cruise GS, that places the top of climb and the top of descent. `061.01.08.02.03`

> **Worked example · average GS.** Climb to FL 240. At FL 160 (2/3) the TAS is 300 kt and the wind a 40 kt headwind, so the average GS = **260 kt**.

Most aircraft manuals give **graphs or tables** of time, fuel and distance to climb and descend, entered with pressure altitude, temperature, mass and wind. The results are usually in still air, so correct the distance for wind using the average GS. Climb data are often given from sea level; for an elevated aerodrome subtract the values for the aerodrome's altitude. `061.01.08.03.01`

Time = altitude change / average rate of climb or descent, and distance = average GS × time. On a 3° path, ROD = GS × 10 / 2 = SF × 3 × 100, and the descent distance is about the height in feet / 300 NM, roughly **3 NM per 1000 ft**. So a descent from 30 000 ft needs about 90–100 NM, more with a tailwind or a speed reduction. `061.01.08.03.02`

> **Worked example · descent time.** Descend 9000 ft at 1500 ft/min: 6 min. At GS 240 kt that is **24 NM**.

The **required rate** to reach an altitude at a point is the height change / time available, with time = distance / GS. Always convert distance to time with GS, never TAS, and leave room to slow down before a speed constraint. `061.01.08.03.03`

> **Worked example · required ROD.** 10 000 ft to lose in 40 NM at GS 300 kt: time = 40 × 60 / 300 = 8 min, so ROD = **1250 ft/min**.

The **distance required** is GS × (height change / rate). A tailwind raises the GS and so the distance, so start down earlier. `061.01.08.03.04`

> **Worked example · descent distance.** GS 270 kt (SF 4.5), 12 000 ft to lose at 1500 ft/min: 8 min, so **36 NM**.

And the **rate from distance, speed and height** is rate (ft/min) = height × GS / (distance × 60), which is the same as gradient (ft/NM) × GS / 60. `061.01.08.03.05`

> **Worked example · rate.** 6000 ft in 20 NM at 180 kt: 6000 × 180 / (20 × 60) = **900 ft/min**.

### Check yourself

1. How many NM is 1' of longitude at 60° N? *About 0.5 NM, cos 60°.*
2. You must cover 300 NM over the ground at TAS 240 kt with a 40 kt tailwind. How many NAM is that? *GS 280 kt, so NAM = 300 × 240 / 280 ≈ 257 NAM.*
3. Climbing at a constant CAS of 280 kt, what happens to TAS and Mach? *Both rise; Mach rises faster because the speed of sound falls with temperature.*
4. What is the TAS at M 0.78 in ISA at FL 350 (−54.3 °C)? *T = 218.9 K, LSS ≈ 576 kt, TAS ≈ 450 kt.*
5. TAS 120 kt and a crosswind of 18 kt. What WCA? *SF 2, so 9°.*
6. On a 3° approach at GS 160 kt, what rate of descent? *About 800 ft/min.*
7. From FL 330 to an airfield at sea level, roughly how far out should a 3° descent begin? *About 3 NM per 1000 ft: about 100 NM in still air, more with a tailwind.*
8. Which level gives the average TAS and wind for a climb to FL 270, and for a descent from FL 270 to sea level? *FL 180 for the climb; FL 135 for the descent.*
