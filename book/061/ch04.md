## Chapter 4 · Great Circles and Rhumb Lines

There are two natural ways to fly between two points on the Earth. One is the shortest route, a great circle, whose true track changes along the way. The other keeps a constant true track, a rhumb line, and is longer. This chapter explains both, why they differ, how much they differ, and how convergency and the conversion angle link the tracks of one to the other. The next chapter shows how charts draw them.

### 4.1 Great circles and small circles

A **great circle** is any circle on the Earth's surface whose plane passes through the **centre of the Earth**. It cuts the Earth into two equal halves. Only one great circle passes through two given points, unless they are antipodal (exactly opposite), when there are infinitely many. A **small circle** is any circle whose plane does not pass through the centre; every parallel of latitude except the equator is one. `061.03.01.01.01`

The **vertex** is the point of highest latitude on a great circle. There the great circle runs exactly east–west (track 090° or 270°), and the latitude of the vertex equals the inclination of the great circle to the equator. Every great circle has two vertices, one in each hemisphere, 180° of longitude apart.

Examples of great circles are the **equator**, **every meridian together with its anti-meridian**, and any other circle through the centre, such as the route from London to Los Angeles or the *terminator*, the line between day and night. The equator is the only parallel that is a great circle. `061.03.01.01.04`

### 4.2 Why the great circle is shortest

On a sphere, the shortest path between two points is the shorter arc of the great circle through them. The great circle has the largest possible radius, the Earth's own, so it curves least between the two points; any other route, a rhumb line or a small circle, bends more and is longer. Long-haul flights therefore follow great circles, adjusted for the winds, and those routes look curved on a Mercator chart. `061.03.01.01.03`

### 4.3 Rhumb lines

A **rhumb line** (loxodrome) cuts every meridian at the **same angle**, so its true track is constant. Meridians and the equator are both rhumb lines and great circles. Parallels of latitude are rhumb lines with track 090° or 270°. Any other rhumb line spirals towards a pole without ever reaching it. A constant true track is easy to fly with a compass, and on a Mercator chart a rhumb line is a straight line. `061.03.02.01.01`

Except along a meridian or the equator, the rhumb line is **longer** than the great circle between the same two points, because it is not an arc of a circle through the Earth's centre. The rhumb line always lies on the **equatorial side** of the great circle, and the great circle on the polar side. `061.03.02.01.02`

### 4.4 How much longer?

The difference between great-circle and rhumb-line distances grows with **latitude** and with **d.long**. Near the equator, or over short routes, it is negligible. On long east–west routes at high latitude it is large, because the great circle bulges strongly towards the pole. `061.03.03.01.01`

> **Worked example · GC and RL distances.** Along 60° N for 90° of d.long, the rhumb line is the parallel: 90 × 60 × cos 60° = **2700 NM**. The great circle between the same two points is about **2485 NM**, some 215 NM shorter.

### 4.5 Convergency

Meridians converge towards the poles. A great circle that is not a meridian or the equator therefore crosses each meridian at a different angle, and its **true track changes continuously** along the route. In the northern hemisphere the great-circle track **increases flying east and decreases flying west**; in the southern hemisphere it is the opposite. `061.03.01.02.01`

The angle between two meridians, measured where a line joining them crosses them, is the **convergency**, and it equals the total change of great-circle track between those meridians. For the Earth:

**Earth convergency = d.long × sin (mean latitude)**

At the equator the convergency is zero (the meridians are parallel there); at the poles it equals the d.long. Note that convergency uses the *sine* of latitude while departure uses the *cosine*. `061.03.01.02.02`

![Convergency and the conversion angle: the great circle on the polar side, the rhumb line between, and the tracks at each end](img/gn-gcrl.jpg)

The formula is a good approximation up to about 30° of d.long. `061.03.01.02.03`

> **Worked example · convergency.** A at 50° N 010° W, B at 60° N 020° E. d.long = 30°, mean latitude 55°. Convergency = 30 × sin 55° = 30 × 0.819 ≈ **24.6°**. If the initial great-circle track at A is 040° T, the final track at B is 040 + 24.6 ≈ **065° T**: northern hemisphere, eastbound, so the track increases.

### 4.6 The conversion angle

The **conversion angle** (CA) is the angle between the great circle and the rhumb line joining two points, measured at either point. It is half the convergency: `061.03.03.02.01`

**CA = ½ × d.long × sin (mean latitude)**

Because the great circle lies on the polar side of the rhumb line, the rhumb-line track is the **mean of the initial and final great-circle tracks**. In the northern hemisphere, eastbound, initial GC track = RL track − CA and final GC track = RL track + CA; westbound the tracks decrease instead.

Radio waves travel along great circles, so a radio bearing is a great-circle bearing. To plot one on a Mercator chart, where straight lines are rhumb lines, convert it to a rhumb-line bearing by applying the conversion angle **towards the equator**. On a Lambert chart, where straight lines are close to great circles, radio bearings are plotted directly, allowing only for the chart convergency between meridians (Chapter 5).

> **Worked example · conversion angle.** RL track 080° T, d.long 20° E, mean latitude 50° N. Convergency = 20 × sin 50° = 15.3°, so CA = 7.7°. Initial GC track ≈ 080 − 7.7 = **072°**, final GC track ≈ 080 + 7.7 = **088°**.

> **Exam trap.** The conversion angle is half the convergency. Sketch the meridians and the curve before answering: the great circle always bows towards the pole, and that settles every sign.

### Check yourself

1. Which parallel of latitude is a great circle? *Only the equator.*
2. A great circle crosses the equator at 30° to it. What is the latitude of its vertices, and what is the track there? *30° N and 30° S; 090° or 270°.*
3. In the southern hemisphere, flying east along a great circle, does the true track increase or decrease? *It decreases.*
4. Find the convergency between 040° W and 010° W at a mean latitude of 45° N. *30 × 0.707 ≈ 21.2°.*
5. The initial GC track from A to B is 058° T and the final GC track 082° T. What are the rhumb-line track and the conversion angle? *RL 070° T; convergency 24°, CA 12°.*
6. Is the great-circle route between two points at 55° N north or south of the rhumb line? *North, on the polar side.*
7. When is the great-circle distance equal to the rhumb-line distance? *Along a meridian or the equator.*
